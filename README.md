# Genesis Through Ancient Eyes

A small-group curriculum that walks through the book of Genesis as its first Hebrew hearers and ancient Jewish readers would have understood it.

## Why read Genesis this way

Genesis was not written to us, but it was written *for* us. Its first audience lived in the world of the Ancient Near East — surrounded by Babylonian, Canaanite, and Egyptian stories of gods, kings, temples, and floods. They read in Hebrew, heard its wordplay, and felt the weight of covenant, blessing, land, and seed.

This study slows down to ask, at every turn:

- **What did the Hebrew actually say?** Word choices, puns, and names carry meaning lost in translation (`adam` / `adamah`, `Babel` / `balal`, `Yitzchaq` / *he laughs*).
- **What world did the first hearers live in?** We compare Genesis to *Enuma Elish*, the *Atrahasis* and *Gilgamesh* flood accounts, and ANE temple and covenant patterns — to see where Genesis echoes its neighbors and where it deliberately breaks from them.
- **How did ancient Jews read it?** We listen to Second Temple readings, the Targums, and early rabbinic interpretation alongside the New Testament's own use of Genesis.
- **Why does it matter now?** Each session lands on what the text claimed about God, humanity, and the world — and what it still claims.

Many of us were trained to read Genesis as if it were a modern science-or-history textbook. So this study deliberately starts *slowly*, and not at "day one." We begin with the question of **genre**: we read the creation stories of Israel's neighbors, weigh the literary shape of Genesis 1, and notice the clues the author left in plain sight — a talking snake, a strange fixation on nakedness and shame — that tell us what *kind* of writing this is. Only once we know how to read it do we walk the text.

## How to use this site

Each session has its own page with the passage, background reading, discussion questions, and Hebrew/ANE notes. Work through them in order with your group, or jump to a session below. Leaders: skim the background notes before meeting; they flag the ANE parallels worth raising out loud.

Full text of Genesis 1:1–2:3 laid out block by block. 🎧 Podcasts: [medium (20m)](sessions/02-history-or-poetry/Medium_Podcast.m4a) · [long (40m)](sessions/02-history-or-poetry/Longer_Podcast.m4a)

**Part 2 — Primeval History (Genesis 1–11)**

4. [In the Beginning](sessions/04-in-the-beginning/) — Genesis 1:1–2:3 re-read now that we know its genre. The seven Session 3 "problems" that live in this passage, walked one at a time — but *not* solved: each bump gets its standard fix named and declined, then followed as a signpost. All seven arrows land in the same place: **temple cosmology**. God's "rest" is a *move-in*, not a nap; the image is the statue installed in the finished house; the week is a sanctuary dedication. With the rabbis who pointed this way — *Numbers Rabbah* 12:13 ("the Tabernacle is equal to the world"), Hillel and Shammai arguing Genesis 1:1 with a throne and a palace, the Levites singing the creation week in the Temple — and the places they went elsewhere instead. Also: the [full reference version](sessions/04-in-the-beginning/reference.html) (three times as long, every source quoted) and source sheets for [the rabbis on Genesis 1](sessions/04-in-the-beginning/texts/rabbinic-sources.md) · [temple and cosmos](sessions/04-in-the-beginning/texts/temple-and-cosmos.md) 🎧 Podcasts: [medium (20m)](sessions/04-in-the-beginning/Medium_Podcast.m4a) · [long (49m)](sessions/04-in-the-beginning/Longer_Podcast.m4a)
5. [The Garden](sessions/05-the-garden/) — Genesis 2–3, read along one thread: **nakedness**, the word the story puts at its exact center. What "naked and not ashamed" claims (and why nakedness is a *vestment* problem once you notice Eden is a sanctuary); who the snake is — Genesis calls him a field animal God made, and the identification with Satan turns out to arrive centuries later; and how he works. His method is four moves, and the first one is to quote God's most generous sentence back with the polarity reversed: *from every tree you may freely eat* becomes *you must not eat from any tree*. The con is never "here is something evil" — it is **"look at what you're missing"** — and what it actually pays out is not the promotion but a mirror. Then the difference between guilt and shame, and God's sharpest question: *who told you that you were naked?* With the earliest readers on all of it, dated — Ben Sira (c. 180 BCE), Jubilees, Josephus, the Apocalypse of Moses — and the rabbis, including the fifth-century midrash that puts *"hurry and eat now, before…"* in the serpent's mouth. Also: the [full reference version](sessions/05-the-garden/reference.html) (every source quoted and dated, plus the material the class version leaves out) and source sheets for [the earliest readings](sessions/05-the-garden/texts/second-temple-sources.md) · [the rabbis on Genesis 3](sessions/05-the-garden/texts/rabbinic-sources.md) 🎧 Podcasts: [medium (22m)](sessions/05-the-garden/Medium_Podcast.m4a) · [long (30m)](sessions/05-the-garden/Longer_Podcast.m4a)

    **5.2 [The Garden, Part 2](sessions/05-the-garden/part-2.html)** — the second meeting on Genesis 2–3, picking up where the clock ran out. **Part 6** first — the practical landings the class never reached — then the **last seven of Session 3's twenty-eight problems**, braided into three threads. **Dust that can name:** *Genesis Rabbah* 17:4, where the angels cannot name the animals, Adam can, and he names himself from the *adamah* before naming God. **The tree you could refuse:** Ben Sira (c. 180 BCE) on the *yetzer* — a command that cannot be refused is not a command — and why the tree confers *jurisdiction*, not information. **Where the center is:** *Jubilees* 8:19 calling Eden the holy of holies, so the river paragraph is a survey marker, and the river returns in Ezekiel 47 and Revelation 22 with the tree of life unbarred. Closes with a map of all twenty-eight problems and the synthesis the whole arc has been building toward: **the creation accounts are not about creation — they are about the Creator, and His last and most costly creature.** His design, our rejection, and the plan. 🎧 Podcasts: [medium (23m)](sessions/05-the-garden/Part2_Medium_Podcast.m4a) · [long (32m)](sessions/05-the-garden/Part2_Longer_Podcast.m4a)

6. [Cain, Lamech, and the Spread of Violence](sessions/06-cain-and-violence/) — Genesis 4, read as **arithmetic**: something in the chapter keeps escalating and something keeps refusing to. Why the offering was refused — the text gives three asymmetries and no verdict, and the answer most of us were taught (he kept back the firstfruits) turns out to be Philo's argument from silence, c. 30 CE. Then the door: *ḥaṭṭāʾt* is feminine but *rōbēṣ* is **masculine**, and the verb is cognate with the Akkadian **rābiṣu**, the demon that waits at a threshold — while verse 7 quotes Genesis 3:16 word for word, so what broke the first marriage is now outdoors. Then **the mark** — *ʾôt* is the rainbow-and-Sabbath **covenant** word, the Hebrew says God set a sign ***for*** Cain, and its only stated job is to keep him alive; with the *nāśāʾ* thread that ties God's offered "lifting" in 4:7 to Cain's *"too great to be lifted"* in 4:13, and the midrash where Adam discovers the power of repentance from the first murderer — plus the dissent, in the same paragraph. Also: where Cain's wife came from, and what happens when you actually trace the names — they lead to the language of whichever book invented them, not to a people group. Then the genealogy read as **an answer to the curse**: the ground will no longer yield to Cain, and his line goes on to invent a city, tents, the harp and the flute, and bronze — **everything except farming**. The trade-names cluster exactly there (*Jabal* "to lead," *Jubal* / *yôḇēl* the ram's horn, *Tubal-Cain* holding *qayin*, "smith" — Cain's own name), and the city is Cain declining the sign he was already given and walling in his own protection instead, which is how Josephus read it. Lands on Lamech's seventy-seven and Matthew 18:22, which is the same number in the same Greek, run in the other direction. Source sheets: [Genesis 4 with the Hebrew notes](sessions/06-cain-and-violence/texts/genesis-4.html) · [the earliest readings](sessions/06-cain-and-violence/texts/second-temple-sources.html) · [the rabbis on Genesis 4](sessions/06-cain-and-violence/texts/rabbinic-sources.html) · [where the wives came from](sessions/06-cain-and-violence/texts/the-wives.html) 🎧 Podcasts: [medium (20m)](sessions/06-cain-and-violence/Medium_Podcast.m4a) · [long (55m)](sessions/06-cain-and-violence/Longer_Podcast.m4a)
7. [The Generations of Adam](sessions/07-generations-of-adam/) — Genesis 4:17–5:32, the two family trees, read as **what a genealogy is for**. Opens with the question left over from Session 6 — what is Tubal-Cain doing with **iron** two thousand years before the Iron Age? — and answers it from the genre: an etiology projects the narrator's own inventory onto the founder, nobody asks the same question about Jubal's flute, and *Tubal* is the Anatolian metal-country where iron smelting actually began. Then the **Sumerian King List** and Berossus's ten kings before the flood: reigns in multiples of 3,600, a collapse after the flood, sages from the sea who bring the arts, and a **seventh king** from the city of the sun who is brought into the gods' assembly — set beside Enoch, the seventh from Adam, 365 years, "God took him." The two lists that rhyme (six of Cain's seven names have a twin in Seth's line) with both accounts of why — two sources joined, or one author's design — laid out fairly; the numbers as a composed pattern (Cassuto's fives-and-sevens; the Hebrew, Samaritan and Greek chronologies side by side; Methuselah dying in the flood year), and the refrain — *and then he died*, eight times — broken by Enoch — read with Hebrews 11:5, with the rabbis' dissent given its due — and by Noah, named *Rest* over the cursed ground by the ninth generation to live under it. Source sheets: [Genesis 4:17–5:32 with the Hebrew notes](sessions/07-generations-of-adam/texts/genesis-5.html) · [the Sumerian King List and Berossus](sessions/07-generations-of-adam/texts/sumerian-king-list.html) · [the earliest readings](sessions/07-generations-of-adam/texts/second-temple-sources.html) · [the rabbis on Genesis 4:17–5:32](sessions/07-generations-of-adam/texts/rabbinic-sources.html) 🎧 Podcasts: [medium (22m)](sessions/07-generations-of-adam/Medium_Podcast.m4a) · [long (60m)](sessions/07-generations-of-adam/Longer_Podcast.m4a)
8. [The Sons of God and the Nephilim](sessions/08-sons-of-god/) — Genesis 6:1–8, the strangest four verses in Genesis and the saddest four. The sons of God *saw* that the daughters of humankind were *good*, and *took* — Eve's three verbs from 3:6, and afterwards the grammar of Pharaoh, Shechem and David. Who the sons of God were: the Hebrew idiom ("son of X" = one of the class X), the eight verses that use the phrase, and the **divine council** — God enthroned among the "host of heaven" in 1 Kings 22, Job, Psalm 82 and Daniel 7, in the same words Ugarit used for El's seventy sons. The three historic readings (heavenly beings, the line of Seth, kings) laid out fairly, landing with Jude and 2 Peter. Then the **Nephilim**: *gigantes* was the Greek word for earth-born rebels against heaven, not tall men; the size enters with the spies' "evil report"; and the thread runs Anakim → Rephaim → Og → Gath → Goliath and ends with David — with the *Rephaim* as the royal dead of Ugarit and Sidon, the dolmens of Og's Bashan, the walls of Gath, and the fossil bones the ancients took for giants. How the story grew: Gilgamesh, two-thirds god, who wanted "to establish a name"; Hesiod's demigods; *1 Enoch*'s Watchers on Mount Hermon and the Dead Sea Scrolls' *Book of Giants*, which names Gilgamesh among them — and what Genesis leaves out, refusing to let the monsters take the blame. Lands on **two hearts** (6:5–6): Lamech's words for comfort and pain in 5:29 come back as God's own grief, which the rabbis read as a father mourning a son; and *Noah* spelled backward is *grace*, named before he is called righteous. Source sheets: [Genesis 6:1–8 with the Hebrew notes](sessions/08-sons-of-god/texts/genesis-6.html) · [the sons of God and the giants across the Bible](sessions/08-sons-of-god/texts/elsewhere-in-scripture.html) · [the neighbours](sessions/08-sons-of-god/texts/the-neighbors.html) · [the earliest readings](sessions/08-sons-of-god/texts/second-temple-sources.html) · [the rabbis on Genesis 6:1–8](sessions/08-sons-of-god/texts/rabbinic-sources.html) · [the church on Genesis 6](sessions/08-sons-of-god/texts/church-fathers.html) 🎧 Podcasts: [short (25m)](sessions/08-sons-of-god/08-sons-of-god-short.m4a) · [long (55m)](sessions/08-sons-of-god/08-sons-of-god-long.m4a)
9. [Regret, Grace, and a Story Told Twice](sessions/09-regret-and-grace/) — Genesis 6:5–8, the four verses the class did not reach in Session 8, and a first look at the Flood. **Whose fault?** Verse 5 turns from the sons of God to *humankind*: Eden's *"the serpent tricked me"* refused again, the two answers of Jesus' day (*1 Enoch*'s evil from above, Ben Sira's *"Say not: 'From God is my transgression'"*), James 1:13–14 and Mark 7 — and the *yēṣer* of 6:5 returning in 8:21 as the reason for mercy, because the flood washes the earth but not the heart. **"The LORD regretted"**: God *niḥam*s in 37 verses and Numbers 23:19 and 1 Samuel 15:29 say he does not; five ways readers have held both — the Greek and Aramaic translators who softened it, Philo's *God is not as a man / as a man*, Augustine, Aquinas and Calvin's accommodation (*"because it could not otherwise be known how great is God's hatred and detestation of sin"*), the rabbis' and Heschel's real grief, open theism and the ETS vote, and relenting as part of God's own creed (Jonah 4:2; Joel 2:13) — landing with Hebrews and Ephesians on a purpose that does not change and a Spirit who can be grieved. **Grace, the first time**: *find favor in the eyes of* as a word for someone lower before someone higher, and Genesis 6:8 as the only verse where the narrator says it of God; *charis* from Noah to Gabriel's *"you have found favor"*; and *wipe out, relent, favor* meeting again at Sinai after the golden calf, where Moses offers to be wiped out in the people's place and God refuses. **A story told twice**: the doublets of 6:9–9:17 and three ways of reading them, each at its strongest and with its hardest question — **J and P** (the Priestly and non-Priestly strands, what each lets you hear, and Isaiah 54:9's exiles), **the mirror** (Wenham's palistrophe on *"God remembered Noah"*, Cassuto's names and the rabbis' mercy and justice), and **the tradition's one Author** (Rashi and the Talmud on why each thing is said twice). Also faces what verse 7 costs — the children and the animals — with Abraham's question and Jonah's last line. Ends on a teaser for the neighbours' floods: Atrahasis's *"clamor,"* and the goddess in Gilgamesh XI who weeps that *"the former day is verily turned to clay."* Source sheets: [the Flood, told twice](sessions/09-regret-and-grace/texts/the-flood-told-twice.html) (the whole narrative, coloured by strand, with a J-only / P-only toggle) · [regret and favor across the Bible](sessions/09-regret-and-grace/texts/elsewhere-in-scripture.html) · [can God regret?](sessions/09-regret-and-grace/texts/god-who-regrets.html) · [one story or two?](sessions/09-regret-and-grace/texts/one-story-or-two.html) 🎧 Podcasts: [short (23m)](sessions/09-regret-and-grace/09-regret-and-grace-short.m4a) · [long (71m)](sessions/09-regret-and-grace/09-regret-and-grace-long.m4a)
10. [The Flood Everyone Knew](sessions/10-the-flood/) — Genesis 6:9–9:29 set beside the flood stories its first hearers already had in the air: *Atrahasis*, *Gilgamesh* XI, the Sumerian Ziusudra and Berossus. The outline is shared — a warning, a boat sealed with pitch (and Genesis's *kōper* is the Babylonian word *kupru*), a mountain, birds, a sacrifice the deity smells — so the point is **where Genesis breaks the pattern**, nine places in all: noise against violence and the heart; a council of gods who swear an oath, lose control of their flood and cower like dogs, against one God who opens the deep and closes it and *remembers Noah*; Ea's cover story for the neighbours against a Noah who never says a word (and, in 2 Peter, a herald); a cube with a pilot and the family silver against a *tēbâ*, the box with no rudder that holds baby Moses too; hungry gods who swarm the sacrifice *like flies* against *the soothing aroma* and a promise; a goddess who weeps during the flood and a god who protests it (*On the sinner place his sin*), against God's grief before it and Abraham's question afterward; a survivor made immortal against *a man of the soil* who gets drunk and dies (with the curse of Canaan, and how it was misused); *Atrahasis*'s population controls — barren women, a baby-snatching demon — against *be fruitful and multiply* and a law protecting every human life because it bears God's image; and a necklace of lapis flies against a war-bow hung in the cloud and the first covenant, made with every living creature and the earth. Then a chart of flood stories from every inhabited continent — Egypt's beer, Manu's fish, China's drained flood, Deucalion's stones, the Maya wooden people, Tata and Nene turned to dogs, the Ojibwe muskrat, the Gunaikurnai frog, Hawaiʻi in 1823 and 1878 — and a grid of which of Genesis's details turn up anywhere else: the outline everywhere; the meaning almost nowhere, except where missionaries had already been. Source sheets: [the neighbours' floods](sessions/10-the-flood/texts/the-neighbors-floods.html) · [floods around the world](sessions/10-the-flood/texts/floods-around-the-world.html) · [the Flood in Hebrew](sessions/10-the-flood/texts/the-flood-in-hebrew.html) · [Noah among his readers](sessions/10-the-flood/texts/early-readers.html) 🎧 Podcasts: [short (17m)](sessions/10-the-flood/10-the-flood-short.m4a) · [long (40m)](sessions/10-the-flood/10-the-flood-long.m4a)
11. [A Name for Ourselves](sessions/11-babel/) — Genesis 10:1–12:3, the close of the primeval history, read along one word: ***name***. **The whole world on one page:** Genesis 10 as *"be fruitful and fill the earth"* coming true — about seventy peoples on a new map drawn for the class, with every identification sourced and every uncertain one marked; the seventy bulls of Tabernacles that the rabbis said were offered *"for the seventy nations of the world"*; Israel left off the list, and Joktan, *"the little one,"* given the most sons; Nimrod, the first *gibbōr* since 6:4, founding both Babylon and Nineveh. **"A name for ourselves":** Babel as the close-up of chapter 10 — the bricks in sound, God *coming down* to see a tower meant to reach heaven, *šām / šēm / šāmayim*; the readings of what went wrong (Josephus's refusal to send out colonies, Ibn Ezra's city that would not disperse, the Talmud's Nimrod beside Abraham's *"dust and ashes"*), and the rabbis' two pictures of the builders — weeping for a fallen brick, and loving one another. Then Babylon's own story told back to it: no tower tablet has ever been found (George Smith's of 1876 turned out to be a lament), but *Enuma Elish* has the gods mould Babylon's bricks for a year, Nabopolassar raises Etemenanki *"to rival the heavens,"* Nebuchadnezzar gathers *"the widespread people of the inhabited world"* to build it and stamps his name on its bricks, and Herodotus's god comes down to sleep at the top. **"I will make your name great":** Shem, whose name *is* "name," and the ten generations to Abram (*Pirkei Avot* 5:2); Terah *"settled there"* in Haran in the builders' own words; *Go* as the builders' fear made a command, Rashi on what travel costs, and the midrash's flask of balsam that only spreads its fragrance when carried; five curses in Genesis 1–11 answered by five blessings in 12:2–3; *"all the families of the ground,"* and the Talmud's reading of *will be blessed* as *will be grafted* — Ruth the Moabite. Ends with a look back over Genesis 1–11 through who names whom. ## Sources we lean on

Primary text in Hebrew (BHS) and a literal English translation alongside; comparative ANE texts in translation; and standard reference works on Genesis and its world. Specific citations live in each session's notes.

We also draw heavily on two ministries that teach Scripture in its ancient context:

- **[BEMA Discipleship](https://www.bemadiscipleship.com/)** — Marty Solomon's podcast walking through the Bible the way its first listeners would have heard it.
- **[BibleProject](https://bibleproject.com/)** — Tim Mackie and team's videos, podcasts, and reading guides on the literary and Hebraic shape of the text.

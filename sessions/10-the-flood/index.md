# 10. The Flood Everyone Knew

**Passage:** Genesis 6:9–9:29 ([the whole Flood narrative, with its doublets marked](../09-regret-and-grace/texts/the-flood-told-twice.html) · [the last verses, 9:18–29, with notes on the Hebrew](texts/the-flood-in-hebrew.html))

## A note before we begin

Some of us first met *Gilgamesh* as an argument against the Bible: here is
the story of Noah, written down long before Genesis, with a different hero and
a crowd of gods. Some of us met the opposite argument: every culture on earth
remembers a great flood, so Genesis must be true. Today takes both seriously —
and finds that both are looking in the wrong place. Both rest on the outline:
rising water, a boat, a few survivors. To the first people who heard Genesis,
the outline was not the news. They lived in a world where the flood story was
already being told. What they had not heard was *this* telling of it.

If it unsettles you that Noah's story has older cousins, that is an honest
response, and you are in good company; it unsettled a great many people when
the first flood tablet was deciphered in 1872. If it unsettles you that "every
culture has a flood story" turns out to be less true than is often said, that
is honest too. Faithful readers explain the likeness in more than one way, and
this session lays them out. But however you explain it, today's claim is the
same: **the meaning is in the details, and the details are where Genesis is
different.**

## Aim of this session

Three movements: **the story everyone knew** — *Atrahasis* and *Gilgamesh*
set beside Genesis; **where Genesis breaks the pattern** — nine places, from
why the water came to what God remembers; and **floods around the world** — a
chart of flood stories from every inhabited continent, and what their details
show.

## Part 1 — The story everyone knew

You have met both stories before. In **Session 1** we read the opening of
***Atrahasis***, the Babylonian epic in which the gods make humankind out of
clay to carry their labour; its oldest dated copy was made around 1635 BCE,
and its climax is a flood. In **Session 8** we met **Gilgamesh**, the king who
wanted a name. In Tablet XI of his epic, afraid of dying, he finds the one man
who survived the flood and was given eternal life, **Uta-napishti** — and the
survivor tells him how. Tablet XI took its flood from *Atrahasis*; it even
slips and calls its hero "Atra-hasis."

These stories travelled. Copies of *Atrahasis* have been dug up in Babylonia,
at Ugarit on the Syrian coast, and at the Hittite capital in Turkey; a piece of
*Gilgamesh* turned up at Megiddo, in Canaan. No flood tablet has been found in
Israel itself, so the careful claim is this: **the story was in the air of
their world**, told and copied across the ancient Near East for well over a
thousand years. And Genesis's own details suggest its writer knew it — the
mountain, the birds, the altar, the aroma, and one word. The *pitch* of 6:14,
***kōper***, appears nowhere else in the Bible, and it matches the Babylonian
*kupru*, the pitch on Uta-napishti's boat. S. R. Driver, in 1904: *"it is
possible therefore that kōpher came into Heb., with the story, from
Babylonia."* (Possible: bitumen was traded, and a word can travel without a
story. The cluster of details is the stronger evidence.)

Listen to how much the stories share.

| | ***Gilgamesh* XI** | **Genesis** |
|---|---|---|
| **The decision** | *"The great gods, brought their hearts to send a deluge"* (XI 14) | *"I have decided that all living creatures must die"* (6:13; literally, *"the end of all flesh has come before me"*) |
| **A boat** | *"Tear down the house, build a ship!… Bring into the ship the seed of life of everything!"* (XI 24–27) | *"Make for yourself an ark of cypress wood"* (6:14) |
| **Pitch** | *kupru* (XI 55, 66) | *"cover it with pitch inside and out"* (6:14) |
| **Who goes in** | *"all my family and relatives. / The cattle of the field, the beast of the plain"* (XI 85–86) | *"you, your sons, your wife, and your sons' wives… two of every kind of living creature"* (6:18–19) |
| **Everyone else** | *"And all mankind was turned to clay"* (XI 135) | *"all living things that moved on the earth died… and all humankind"* (7:21) |
| **A mountain** | *"…the Ark on the Mountain of Nisir / Grounded"* (XI 142; the name is now read Nimush) | *"the ark came to rest on one of the mountains of Ararat"* (8:4) |
| **Birds** | a dove — *"It did not have a resting place; it returned"*; a swallow; a raven — *"it did not return"* (XI 147–156) | a raven; a dove — it *"could not find a resting place for its feet"* (8:7–12) |
| **A sacrifice** | *"I offered a sacrifice… The gods smelled the sweet savor"* (XI 157–162) | *"Noah built an altar… And the LORD smelled the soothing aroma"* (8:20–21) |
| **Remembering** | a necklace, to keep *"these days in remembrance"* (XI 165–167) | a bow: *"I will remember my covenant"* (9:15) |
{: .ff-wrap}

The likeness runs through both of the strands Session 9 traced: the pitch, the
measurements, the mountain and the bow belong to the Priestly telling; the
seven days, the birds, the altar and the aroma to the other. However Genesis
was put together, its knowledge of the old story runs through the whole.

### Two questions

**Did it happen?** For this class the New Testament settles it. Jesus set the
days of Noah beside his own coming (Matthew 24:37–39; Luke 17:26–27), and
Peter set the flood beside the judgment to come (2 Peter 2:5; 3:5–7). Genesis
itself tells it as history: it opens with a family heading (*"This is the
account of Noah,"* 6:9) and dates the waters by the day of Noah's life (7:11;
8:13–14). What Christians have not agreed on is **how far the water reached**.
Some read *"all the high mountains under the entire sky were covered"* (7:19)
as a flood over the whole globe; others, also holding Scripture's authority,
read a vast regional flood, noting that Genesis can say *"throughout the
earth"* of a famine in Egypt's world (41:57). This session does not settle
that; both views are on **[floods around the world](texts/floods-around-the-world.html)**.

**How are the stories related?** Here there are two real answers, and they
cannot both be right. **Genesis knew the Mesopotamian telling and answered
it.** Since 1872 most scholars have held that the Hebrew story came, directly
or not, out of the Mesopotamian tradition; Jewish scholars such as Umberto
Cassuto (who rejected the two-source theory) and Nahum Sarna, and later Tikva
Frymer-Kensky (*Biblical Archaeologist*, 1977), read the differences as
Genesis answering its neighbours. *Its strength:* the details cluster exactly
where the theology differs. *Its hard question:* shared details do not prove
copying, and not every difference is a rebuttal. **Both remember the same
event, independently.** The evangelical Assyriologist Alan Millard, who
co-edited *Atrahasis*, argued that borrowing has never been shown and that the
two accounts describe the same flood (*Tyndale Bulletin*, 1967); Kenneth
Kitchen added that in the ancient world simple accounts grow into elaborate
legends, not the reverse, so Genesis need not be a cleaned-up *Atrahasis*. Even
Alexander Heidel, who called the versions "genetically related," judged the
case for borrowing indecisive. *Its strength:* the Mesopotamians themselves
dated their history by the flood — the Sumerian King List (Session 7): *"Then
the flood swept over."* *Its hard question:* the flood layers archaeologists
have found in Mesopotamian cities are local and of different dates, so
archaeology cannot point to this one. You may hold either.

On either answer, the comparison works the same way. If you had grown up on
*Gilgamesh*, you would know where Genesis was going at every turn — and you
would keep being surprised.

## Part 2 — Where Genesis breaks the pattern

### 1. Why the water came

In *Atrahasis*, people multiplied, and their noise kept the god Enlil awake:
*"[Concerning] their clamor I am troubled."* He sent plague, then drought,
then famine — and still *"[The people] have not become less; they are more
numerous than before."* So he sent the flood. *Gilgamesh* gives no reason at
all: *"The great gods, brought their hearts to send a deluge."*

Genesis gives a reason, and gives it twice (Session 9): the human **heart** —
*"Every inclination of the thoughts of their minds was only evil all the
time"* (6:5) — and **violence**: *"the earth was filled with violence"* (6:11).
The word is ***ḥāmās***: what people do to one another. Genesis knows the
other worries too. Its flood story opens *"When humankind began to multiply on
the face of the earth"* (6:1), with heavenly beings crossing a line (6:2–4,
Session 8). But in Genesis multiplying is the blessing (1:28), never the
offence. (Scholars debate what the noise in *Atrahasis* means — too many
people, most think; some, humans pushing into the gods' own sphere. Either way,
it is not what Genesis names.) Did people know violence was wrong, with no law
yet given? Nachmanides answered: *"the prohibition against violence is a
rational commandment, there being no need for a prophet to admonish them
against it."*

### 2. Who is in charge

In Mesopotamia a council of gods decides, and swears an oath to keep it
secret. Ea, the clever god, has sworn with them — so he does not tell a man;
he tells the reed house the man lives in:

> Nin-igi-azag—'tis Ea—, (albeit) conspirator with them, / Unto a reed-hut
> their counsel betray'd he: "O Reed-hut, O Reed-hut! Wall, wall! Hearken, O
> Reed-hut, consider, O Wall!" *(Gilgamesh XI 19–22)*

And when the storm comes, the gods lose control of their own flood: *"The gods
fear the deluge. / They withdraw, they ascend to the heaven of Anu. / The gods
cower like a dog"* (XI 114–116).

In Genesis one God decides, warns, and saves. He opens *"all the fountains of
the great deep"* (7:11), and he closes them (8:2). In the middle of the story,
*"God remembered Noah… God caused a wind to blow over the earth and the waters
receded"* (8:1) — a wind over the waters, as in Genesis 1:2. The flood is
creation run backward, and God runs it forward again; Peter reads it the same
way — an earth *"formed out of water and by means of water"* by God's word,
then *"deluged with water"* (2 Peter 3:5–6). *"The LORD sits enthroned over the
engulfing waters"* (Psalm 29:10). Mesopotamia splits destruction and rescue
between two rival gods; Genesis puts both in one God — which is why, as we saw
last week, the tension lives inside God's own heart.

### 3. What the hero told his neighbours

Uta-napishti asks Ea, *"[But what] shall I answer the city, the people, and
the elders?"* Ea gives him a cover story — *"[I kn]ow that Enlil hates me, and
/ I may not dwell in your city"* — and a riddle to tell them, which in our
working translation runs: ***"In the morning, bread-cakes; at nightfall he will
rain on you a shower of wheat."*** On the usual modern reading the words are
deliberately double: the city hears a promise of plenty, and the rain that is
coming is not wheat. Then he throws his workmen a feast *"like the Akitu
festival,"* and sails. (*Atrahasis* is gentler: its hero tells his town that
his god and theirs have quarrelled, and at his farewell feast he cannot sit
still, because his heart is breaking.)

**In Genesis, Noah never says a word.** Read the whole Flood story and notice
who speaks: from 6:13 to 9:17 it is always God. Noah does not answer, ask,
argue or warn. What the narrator says instead is *"And Noah did all that God
commanded him—he did indeed"* (6:22). His first recorded words in the Bible
come after it is all over, in 9:25. Genesis records no word of his; the New
Testament tells us what his life said. Second Peter calls him *"a herald of
righteousness"* (2:5); Hebrews says that by building the ark in faith *"he
condemned the world"* (11:7). His obedience was itself the sermon, and
*"God patiently waited in the days of Noah as an ark was being constructed"*
(1 Peter 3:20). We read Genesis with them. The rabbis, filling the same
silence, pictured him planting cedars and cutting them down for a hundred and
twenty years so that people would ask: *"They said to him: 'Why are you doing
this?' He said to them: 'So said the Master of the world, that He is bringing
a flood upon the world'"* (*Genesis Rabbah* 30:7). The god of *Gilgamesh* helps
his man keep the city in the dark. The God of the Bible waits.

**Why him?** The names say it. *Atra-hasis* means "exceedingly wise";
*Uta-napishti*, "he found life." *Gilgamesh* gives no reason for choosing him
at all (the older Sumerian story does call its hero "humble… reverent").
Genesis: *"Noah found favor in the sight of the LORD"* (6:8) — grace first,
then *righteous* (Session 9).

### 4. A box with no rudder

Uta-napishti's boat is a **cube**, 120 cubits each way, with six decks. He
loads it — *"With all that I had of silver, I loaded it. / With all that I had
of gold, I loaded it"* (XI 81–82) — and his craftsmen, and he hands it to a
pilot: *"To the master of the ship, to Buzur-Amurru, the sailor, / I entrusted
the great house, including its possessions"* (XI 95–96).

Noah's ark is a ***tēbâ***, a box — 300 by 50 by 30 cubits, a long, flat
barge. It carries no silver and no gold: a family, the animals, food. There is
no word in the story for a rudder, an oar, a sail or a pilot. *Tēbâ* appears
in only one other place in the Bible: the basket, sealed with bitumen and
pitch, in which a mother set her baby among the reeds of the Nile (Exodus 2:3,
5). Both times God keeps the future safe on the water, in a box no one steers.
An ancient Jewish writer put it this way — *"The hope of the world, taking
refuge on a raft… Thy hand guiding the helm"* (Wisdom 14:6); his English
translator added, *"Noah was not acquainted with navigation."*

### 5. Hungry gods

Why do the Mesopotamian gods care whether anyone survives? Because in
*Atrahasis* (Session 1), humankind was made to do the gods' work — including
feeding them. With the people drowned, the gods go hungry. In our working
translation of *Atrahasis*, *"The Anunna, the great gods, sat in thirst and in
hunger"*; *"like sheep they crowded the trough."* So when the survivor offers
his sacrifice:

> The gods smelled the savor. / The gods smelled the sweet savor. / The gods
> like flies gathered about the sacrificer. *(Gilgamesh XI 161–163)*

Genesis keeps one line and drops the rest. *"And the LORD smelled the soothing
aroma"* (8:21). *Soothing* is ***nîḥōaḥ*** — the sound of Noah's name again,
and the first of forty-three times the Bible uses it; all but one of its later
uses in the Torah are in the laws of sacrifice. No flies, no hunger, no meal.
What follows the aroma is a **promise**: *"I will never again curse the
ground… I will never again destroy everything that lives"* (8:21). Israel
would sing it later: *"Even if I were hungry, I would not tell you, for the
world and all it contains belong to me. Do I eat the flesh of bulls?"* (Psalm
50:12–13). And the rabbis drew the same line through every offering called a
"soothing aroma": *"there is no 'eating' or 'drinking' before the Holy One
Blessed be He"* (*Sifrei Bamidbar* 143).

### 6. Who grieves — and who objects

In *Gilgamesh* the mother goddess weeps **during** the flood, for what she
herself agreed to:

> The lady of the gods wails with her beautiful voice. / The former day is
> verily turned to clay. / When I spoke evil in the assembly of the gods… *(XI
> 118–120)*

Afterwards Enlil sees the boat and is furious: *"Has anyone come out alive? /
No man shall survive the cataclysm."* And Ea rebukes him — *"On the sinner
place his sin; / On the evil doer place his crimes"* (XI 185–186).

Be honest about this one. The Babylonian story voices the objection readers
have always felt, ancient and modern: *why punish everyone?* Genesis's own
answer is that this was not a world with a few sinners in it — *"all living
creatures on the earth were sinful"* (6:12) — and that the one righteous
household was spared (7:1). That answer leaves out the children and the
animals, as Session 9 admitted. And Ea is not quite the champion of the
individual he sounds: his alternatives — *"Let a lion come and diminish the
people… Let there be a famine"* — still fall on everyone.

In Genesis no human objects. The rabbis held that against Noah: the Zohar
says that he *"did not intercede on behalf of his generation, but let them
perish. It is for this reason that the waters of the Flood are named after
him"* (Isaiah 54:9) — unlike Moses, who offered his own life (Session 9). But
what Ea pleads for, God says to his own heart: *"I will never again destroy
everything that lives, as I have just done"* (8:21). In Babylon it took a
second god to say it; in Genesis it is the God who sent the flood. And the
human protest will come — from Abraham, at Sodom: *"Will not the judge of the
whole earth do what is right?"* (18:25). God listens; the bargaining stops at
ten; and Sodom still falls, but Lot is brought out (19:29). Jesus and Peter
set the two stories side by side — the days of Noah, the days of Lot (Luke
17:26–30) — and draw the lesson Ea wanted: *"the Lord knows how to rescue the
godly from their trials, and to reserve the unrighteous for punishment"* (2
Peter 2:9). The question does not go away in the Bible; Ezekiel names Noah
when he raises it again (14:14). It is meant to be asked.

### 7. A god, or a man of the soil

In *Gilgamesh*, the god who sent the flood comes aboard and blesses the
survivors:

> Now, indeed, Uta-Napishtim and (also) his wife shall be equal / Like to us
> gods; in the distance afar at the mouth of the rivers Uta-Napishtim shall
> dwell *(XI 203–206)*

The Sumerian hero is given *"life like a god"*; Berossus's is *"gone to live
with the gods."* God blesses Noah too (9:1) — but the blessing is different:
not eternal life for one couple, far away, but *"be fruitful and multiply"* for
everyone. And notice who is raised with Uta-napishti: his wife. Noah's wife and
his sons' wives are never named and never speak (later Jewish writers gave her
a name — *Jubilees* calls her Emzara).

Genesis: *"Noah, a man of the soil, began to plant a vineyard"* (9:20). The
ground, cursed since 3:17, yields again — the hope his father spoke when he
named him *Rest*, *"because of the ground that the LORD has cursed"* (5:29).
Session 7 promised this would land here. It lands with a sting: the first
harvest is wine, and Noah gets drunk and lies uncovered in his tent (9:21).
And *"The entire lifetime of Noah was 950 years, and then he died"* (9:29) —
the refrain of Genesis 5 returns. Genesis will not make a god of its survivor.
Scripture still honours him — for his faith, not his perfection (Ezekiel
14:14; Hebrews 11:7) — but the hero of this story is the God who remembered
him.

**A note on 9:25.** *"Cursed be Canaan!"* — Canaan, Ham's son, not Ham; and it
is Noah who says it, waking from wine. God curses no one here. The rabbis asked
the question: *"Ḥam committed the sin and Canaan was cursed? This is
astonishing"* — one answered that God had already blessed Noah's sons (9:1),
another that Canaan himself had seen and told (*Genesis Rabbah* 36:7). Jewish
and Christian books also kept folk-tales of Ham coming out of the ark
"darkened," and centuries later these were fused with this verse into a "curse
of Ham," used to justify enslaving Black Africans and preached in America in
defence of slavery and segregation. The text says no such thing. The chapter
itself answers it: every human bears God's image (9:6), and *"from them the
whole earth was populated"* (9:19) — one family (Acts 17:26). Abraham ibn Ezra
saw through it in the twelfth century: *"There are some who say that the
Cushites are enslaved because Noah cursed Ham. However, they have forgotten that
the first king to rule after the flood was a Cushite."* Many churches that once
taught it have renounced it — the Southern Baptist Convention formally in 2018.
More on **[Noah among his readers](texts/early-readers.html)**, and next week,
when Noah's sons become the nations.

### 8. Fewer people, or more?

*Atrahasis*'s gods had tried drought and famine against humankind before the
flood. Genesis's God promises the opposite: *"While the earth continues to
exist, planting time and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter, and day
and night will not cease"* (8:22). And *Atrahasis* ends with the gods solving
the problem of humankind for good. Its last column (in our working
translation) orders: *"let there be a third (category) among the peoples:
among the peoples, the woman who gives birth, and the woman who does not give
birth. Let there be among the peoples the Pāšittu (the 'Obliterator'); let her
seize the baby from the lap of the one who bore it"* — and orders of
priestesses who may not bear children. **Fewer people**, so that the gods can
sleep.

Genesis:

> Then God blessed Noah and his sons and said to them, "Be fruitful and
> multiply and fill the earth." *(9:1)*

It is the blessing of 1:28, word for word — and God says it twice (9:1, 9:7).
Rashi heard the difference: the first time *"it was said as a blessing… here
it is a command."* Two words of Genesis 1 are missing: *subdue* and *rule*. In
their place, the animals will fear us (9:2). And then the law:

> "Whoever sheds human blood, by other humans must his blood be shed; for in
> God's image God has made humankind." *(9:6)*

Frymer-Kensky saw the shape of it. *Atrahasis* answers its flood with fewer
births. Genesis answers its flood — which came because of violence — with
more people, and a law that protects each one, because each bears God's
image. Rabbi Akiva: *"Anyone who sheds blood, it is considered for him as
though he has diminished God's image"* (*Genesis Rabbah* 34:14). Rashi passes
on the rabbis' reading of the two together: the command to have children comes
right after the law of murder *"in order to liken one who abstains from having
children to one who sheds blood."* Judaism calls the laws binding on every
nation "the seven commandments of the children of Noah." The Talmud reads them
out of God's command to Adam (2:16), with 9:6 as its proof for the law against
bloodshed (*Sanhedrin* 56b); Maimonides counts six given to Adam, and the
seventh — no flesh with its life still in it (9:4) — added for Noah.

### 9. What each one remembers

The goddess lifts a necklace of lapis lazuli beads carved as **flies** — in
*Atrahasis* the drowned became like flies, and the gods swarmed the sacrifice
like flies — and swears by it:

> 'O ye Gods! I will (rather) forget (this) my necklet of sapphires, / Than
> not maintain these days in remembrance, nor ever forget them. *(XI 165–167,
> Thompson)*

(Last week's handout quoted an older translation of this line, which modern
editions read differently; the meaning is the one here.)

God says: *"I will place my rainbow in the clouds"* (9:13). The Hebrew says
*my **bow*** — ***qešet***, the word for an archer's weapon in 72 of its 76
uses in the Bible, including God's own (*"He has prepared to shoot his bow,"*
Psalm 7:12). In Babylon's creation epic (Session 1), Marduk's war-bow is set in
the sky as a star after his battle. Many readers have heard the same picture
here: Josephus called it *"the bow of God,"* and
Nachmanides, in the thirteenth century, a bow turned away from the earth — *"It
is indeed the way of warriors to invert the instruments of war which they hold
in their hands when calling for peace from their opponents."* Others think
*bow* simply describes the shape. (Nachmanides also held that rainbows were
there from creation: God made an old thing into a sign.)

And the bow seals something new: the first time the Bible uses the word
**covenant**. God promised it at 6:18, before the rain; here it comes seven
times in ten verses (9:8–17), and the partners keep widening — *"you and your
descendants… every living creature that is with you"* — until it is
*"between me and the earth"* (9:13). The animals are named partners, not
cargo. The promise has no conditions; the laws of 9:1–7 come before it, not as
its price. And the sign is for God to look at: *"I will notice it and remember
the perpetual covenant between God and all living creatures"* (9:16). The
goddess remembers her grief. God remembers his promise. Isaiah's exiles held
him to it: *"this is like in Noah's time, when I vowed that the waters of
Noah's flood would never again cover the earth. In the same way I have vowed
that I will not be angry at you or shout at you. Even if the mountains are
removed and the hills displaced, my devotion will not be removed from you"*
(Isaiah 54:9–10).

### The same story, a different God

Not every difference is an argument; some are simply what the story looks
like when it is told by people who worship one God. But together they say a
great deal.

| | **The neighbours** | **Genesis** |
|---|---|---|
| **Why** | the people's noise; or no reason | violence; the heart |
| **Who decides** | quarrelling gods, under oath | one God |
| **During the flood** | gods terrified and hungry | *"God remembered Noah"* |
| **The neighbours** | a cover story | no recorded word — and, in 2 Peter, a herald |
| **The boat** | a cube, a pilot, silver and gold | a box with no rudder |
| **The sacrifice** | gods "like flies" | a soothing aroma — and a promise |
| **Who objects** | Ea: punish the sinner, send a plague instead | God himself, to his own heart (8:21); later Abraham |
| **The survivor** | made a god | a man of the soil, who dies |
| **Afterward** | fewer births | *"be fruitful"*; every human life protected |
| **Remembering** | a necklace of flies | a bow in the cloud; a covenant with all life |

## Part 3 — Floods around the world

**"Every culture has a flood story."** It is not quite true. Flood stories are
found on **every inhabited continent** — but **not in every culture**. They
are common in South and Southeast Asia, the Pacific and the Americas. They are
rare in Europe, missing from ancient Egypt, absent in the Noah sense from the
classical books of China and Japan, and nearly absent from Africa. Sir James
Frazer, who collected hundreds a century ago, wrote: *"In Africa, including
Egypt, native legends of a great flood are conspicuously absent; indeed no
single clear case of one has yet been reported."* Later collectors found more,
but few with Noah's shape.

Here is a sample, with the oldest record of each. The full chart has two dozen
more, story by story, on **[floods around the
world](texts/floods-around-the-world.html)**.

| **People** (first written down) | **Why the water came** | **Who survived — and why** | **What came after** |

**What the chart shows — and what it cannot.** The rows in the middle band are
Genesis's own details, so Genesis fills them by construction. Build the rows
from *Gilgamesh*'s details, or the Maya's, and Genesis would have blanks too —
the last band does exactly that. So the chart cannot prove that Genesis is more
original than other stories; every story is distinctive in its details. What
it shows is **what Genesis says that the others do not.** The outline — water,
a warning, a boat, a mountain — is common, and its fullest form clusters in one
family of stories: Mesopotamia, Greece and India. The details that carry
Genesis's meaning are found almost nowhere else. A few partial echoes are
early and independent — India's blessing of offspring, Ovid's righteous
couple, the Maya's moral cause. But **outside Genesis's own neighbourhood, the
closer a story matches Genesis, the later it was written down.** (Inside it the
reverse holds: *Atrahasis*, the closest match, is centuries older, which was
Part 1's point.) In 1823 a missionary in Hawaiʻi preached on Noah, and his
hearers told him their own tradition, of two people saved on a mountain-top;
and *"they said they had never before heard of a ship, or of Noah."* By 1878,
the version a Hawaiian convert gave had a houseboat, eight survivors, a
sacrifice and a rainbow. Several peoples expected the opposite of "never
again": Plato's Egyptian priest, the later Hindu cycles of ages, the Nahua,
Fiji's canoe-keepers and Chile's Mapuche all looked for destruction to come
again.

**Why are there so many?** Three answers, and they can be combined. **One
flood, remembered.** In its strongest form — the one Millard and Kitchen hold
— a real flood in Mesopotamia lies behind the Near Eastern stories, which is
why they share the most. A wider form, held by many Christians since Josephus
(*"all the writers of barbarian histories make mention of this flood"*), sees
the nations' stories as fading memories of Noah. Its hard question: the closest
matches outside the Near East come after missionaries, and Africa, where
Genesis 10 sends Ham's sons, has almost none. **Floods happen wherever people
live** — they are the commonest disaster on earth, and a flood that fills one
valley is the end of the world for the people who live there. Egypt, where the
Nile rose gently from below, has no flood story at all. Its hard question: why
do so many stories reach for *all* of humankind? **Stories travel** — in the
ancient world, and with the Bible. Its hard question: the Maya pictured a flood ending
an earlier world before any European arrived. All three are laid out on
the sheet.

None of these answers decides whether Noah's flood happened. Christians believe
it did because Jesus and the apostles speak of it, not because of how many
peoples tell flood stories. The existence of flood stories proves less than
both sides have claimed: it does not show that Genesis copied a myth, and by
itself it does not prove Genesis true. **The outline was common property. The
details were not.**

## Next week

Genesis 10–11: Noah's three sons become the nations of the world — and the
nations build a city and a tower, *"so that we may make a name for
ourselves"* (11:4). Gilgamesh wanted a name too.

## So what?

**1. The first hearers lived in a world that knew the story. What was new was
the God.** Not a council that panics, starves and quarrels, but one God who
grieves before he judges, remembers in the middle, and promises afterwards.

**2. God needs nothing from us — and binds himself to us, and to every living
thing, anyway.** The gods of Babylon needed humans to feed them. The LORD
smells the offering and answers with a promise, a covenant with *"every living
creature"* and with the earth itself. Paul said it to the Athenians: God is not
*"served by human hands, as if he needed anything"* (Acts 17:25).

**3. After the flood, the answer to violence was more life, and every life
protected.** Babylon's gods solved the human problem with fewer births.
Genesis answers with *"be fruitful and multiply"* and with a law of blood for
blood, grounded in the reason no human life may be taken lightly: *"in God's
image God has made humankind."* Lamech had boasted of vengeance
seventy-sevenfold (4:24); here it is limited to one life for one life.

**4. "Never again" — but not never.** The bow promises a world held steady —
seedtime and harvest (8:22) — while God waits. Jesus read the flood as a
picture of his own coming: *"they knew nothing until the flood came and took
them all away"* (Matthew 24:39). Peter says the present world is *"reserved for
fire"* (2 Peter 3:7), and explains the delay: the Lord *"is being patient
toward you because he does not wish for any to perish but for all to come to
repentance"* (3:9). And he saw in the ark a picture of the church's rescue:
*"In the ark a few, that is eight souls, were delivered through water. And this
prefigured baptism, which now saves you—not the washing off of physical dirt
but the pledge of a good conscience to God—through the resurrection of Jesus
Christ"* (1 Peter 3:20–21).

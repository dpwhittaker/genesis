# 10. The Flood Everyone Knew

**Passage:** Genesis 6:9–9:29 ([the whole Flood narrative, with its doublets marked](../09-regret-and-grace/texts/the-flood-told-twice.html) · [the last verses, 9:18–29, with notes on the Hebrew](texts/the-flood-in-hebrew.html))

## A note before we begin

Some of us first met *Gilgamesh* as an argument against the Bible: here is
the story of Noah, written down long before Genesis, with a different hero and
a crowd of gods. Some of us met the opposite argument: every culture on earth
remembers a great flood, so Genesis must be true. Today takes both seriously —
and finds that both are looking in the wrong place. Both rest on the outline:
rising water, a boat, a few survivors. But the outline was never the news. The
first people to hear Genesis lived in a world where the flood story was already
being told. What they had not heard was *this* telling of it.

If it unsettles you that Noah's story has older cousins, that is an honest
response, and you are in good company; it unsettled a great many people when
the first flood tablet was deciphered in 1872. If it unsettles you that "every
culture has a flood story" turns out to be less true than you were told, that
is honest too. Faithful readers explain the likeness in more than one way, and
this session lays them out. But however you explain it, today's claim is the
same: **the meaning is in the details, and the details are where Genesis is
different.**

## Aim of this session

Three movements: **the story everyone knew** — *Atrahasis* and *Gilgamesh*
set beside Genesis; **where Genesis breaks the pattern** — nine places, from
why the water came to what God remembers; and **floods around the world** — a
chart of flood stories from every inhabited continent, and what their details
show.

## Part 1 — The story everyone knew

You have met both stories before. In **Session 1** we read the opening of
***Atrahasis***, the Babylonian epic in which the gods make humankind out of
clay to carry their labour; its oldest copy was made around 1635 BCE, and it
ends in a flood. In **Session 8** we met **Gilgamesh**, the king who wanted a
name. In Tablet XI of his epic, afraid of dying, he finds the one man who
survived the flood and was given eternal life, **Uta-napishti** — and the
survivor tells him how. Tablet XI took its flood from *Atrahasis*; it even
slips and calls its hero "Atra-hasis."

These stories travelled. Copies of *Atrahasis* have been dug up in Babylonia,
at Ugarit on the Syrian coast, and at the Hittite capital in Turkey; a piece of
*Gilgamesh* turned up at Megiddo, in Canaan. No flood tablet has been found in
Israel itself, so the careful claim is this: **the story was in the air of
their world**, told and copied across the ancient Near East for well over a
thousand years. And Genesis's own vocabulary shows that its writer knew it. The
*pitch* of 6:14, ***kōper***, appears nowhere else in the Bible; it is the
Babylonian word *kupru*, the pitch on Uta-napishti's boat. S. R. Driver, in
1904: *"it is possible therefore that kōpher came into Heb., with the story,
from Babylonia."*

Listen to how much the stories share.

| | ***Gilgamesh* XI** | **Genesis** |
|---|---|---|
| **The decision** | *"The great gods, brought their hearts to send a deluge"* (XI 14) | *"I have decided that all living creatures must die"* (6:13) |
| **A boat** | *"Tear down the house, build a ship!… Bring into the ship the seed of life of everything!"* (XI 24–27) | *"Make for yourself an ark of cypress wood"* (6:14) |
| **Pitch** | *kupru* (XI 55, 66) | *"cover it with pitch inside and out"* (6:14) |
| **Who goes in** | *"all my family and relatives. / The cattle of the field, the beast of the plain"* (XI 85–86) | *"you, your sons, your wife, and your sons' wives… two of every kind of living creature"* (6:18–19) |
| **Everyone else** | *"And all mankind was turned to clay"* (XI 135) | *"all living things that moved on the earth died… and all humankind"* (7:21) |
| **A mountain** | *"…the Ark on the Mountain of Nisir / Grounded"* (XI 142) | *"the ark came to rest on one of the mountains of Ararat"* (8:4) |
| **Birds** | a dove — *"It did not have a resting place; it returned"*; a swallow; a raven — *"it did not return"* (XI 147–156) | a raven; a dove — it *"could not find a resting place for its feet"* (8:7–12) |
| **A sacrifice** | *"I offered a sacrifice… The gods smelled the sweet savor"* (XI 157–162) | *"Noah built an altar… And the LORD smelled the soothing aroma"* (8:20–21) |
| **Remembering** | a necklace, to keep *"these days in remembrance"* (XI 165–167) | a bow: *"I will remember my covenant"* (9:15) |
{: .ff-wrap}

**How are they related?** Two answers, and many readers hold both. **Genesis
retells a story its world already knew, and answers it.** Since 1872 most
scholars have held that the Hebrew story came, directly or not, out of the
Mesopotamian tradition — the word *kōper* is one hint — and that Genesis tells
it in order to say something different about God (Tikva Frymer-Kensky,
*Biblical Archaeologist*, 1977). **Both remember a real flood, and Genesis
tells it truly.** The evangelical Assyriologist Alan Millard, who co-edited
*Atrahasis*, argued that borrowing has never been shown and that the two
accounts describe the same flood (*Tyndale Bulletin*, 1967); Kenneth Kitchen
held that they share a common ancient tradition. The Mesopotamians themselves
dated their history by it — the Sumerian King List (Session 7): *"Then the
flood swept over."* And the New Testament speaks of Noah's flood as an event
(Matthew 24:37–39; 2 Peter 3:6).

Whichever you hold, the comparison works the same way. If you had grown up on
*Gilgamesh*, you would know where Genesis was going at every turn — and you
would keep being surprised.

## Part 2 — Where Genesis breaks the pattern

### 1. Why the water came

In *Atrahasis*, people multiplied, and their noise kept the god Enlil awake:
*"[Concerning] their clamor I am troubled."* He sent plague, then drought,
then famine — and still *"[The people] have not become less; they are more
numerous than before."* So he sent the flood. *Gilgamesh* gives no reason at
all: *"The great gods, brought their hearts to send a deluge."*

Genesis gives a reason, and gives it twice (Session 9): the human **heart** —
*"Every inclination of the thoughts of their minds was only evil all the
time"* (6:5) — and **violence**: *"the earth was filled with violence"* (6:11).
The word is ***ḥāmās***. What troubles God is not how many people there are,
or how loud, but what they do to one another. (Scholars debate what the noise
in *Atrahasis* means — too many people, most think; some, humans pushing into
the gods' own sphere. Either way, it is not wickedness in the Bible's sense.)

### 2. Who is in charge

In Mesopotamia a council of gods decides, and swears an oath to keep it
secret. Ea, the clever god, has sworn with them — so he does not tell a man;
he tells the reed house the man lives in:

> Nin-igi-azag—'tis Ea—, (albeit) conspirator with them, / Unto a reed-hut
> their counsel betray'd he: "O Reed-hut, O Reed-hut! Wall, wall! Hearken, O
> Reed-hut, consider, O Wall!" *(Gilgamesh XI 19–22)*

And when the storm comes, the gods lose control of their own flood: *"The gods
fear the deluge. / They withdraw, they ascend to the heaven of Anu. / The gods
cower like a dog"* (XI 114–116).

In Genesis one God decides, warns, and saves. He opens *"all the fountains of
the great deep"* (7:11), and he closes them (8:2). In the middle of the story,
*"God remembered Noah… God caused a wind to blow over the earth and the waters
receded"* (8:1) — a wind over the waters, as in Genesis 1:2. The flood is
creation run backward, and God runs it forward again. *"The LORD sits enthroned
over the engulfing waters"* (Psalm 29:10). Mesopotamia splits destruction and
rescue between two rival gods; Genesis puts both in one God — which is why, as
we saw last week, the tension lives inside God's own heart.

### 3. What the hero told his neighbours

Uta-napishti asks Ea, *"[But what] shall I answer the city, the people, and
the elders?"* Ea gives him a cover story — *"[I kn]ow that Enlil hates me, and
/ I may not dwell in your city"* — and a riddle to tell them, which in our
working translation runs: ***"In the morning, bread-cakes; at nightfall he will
rain on you a shower of wheat."*** The city hears a promise of plenty. The rain
that is coming is not wheat. Then he throws his workmen a feast *"like the
Akitu festival,"* and sails. (*Atrahasis* is gentler: its hero cannot sit
still at his farewell feast, because his heart is breaking.)

**Noah never says a word.** Read the whole Flood story and notice who speaks:
from 6:13 to 9:17 it is always God. Noah does not answer, ask, argue or warn.
What the narrator says instead is *"And Noah did all that God commanded him—he
did indeed"* (6:22). His first recorded words in the Bible come after it is
all over, in 9:25. The New Testament fills the silence: Noah was *"a herald of
righteousness"* (2 Peter 2:5), while *"God patiently waited in the days of
Noah as an ark was being constructed"* (1 Peter 3:20). The rabbis pictured him
planting cedars and cutting them down for a hundred and twenty years so that
people would ask: *"They said to him: 'Why are you doing this?' He said to
them: 'So said the Master of the world, that He is bringing a flood upon the
world'"* (*Genesis Rabbah* 30:7). Genesis does not record what he said; 2 Peter
tells us he said it, and we read with 2 Peter. The god of *Gilgamesh* helps his
man keep the city in the dark. The God of Genesis waits, while the ark goes up
in plain sight.

**Why him?** The names say it. *Atra-hasis* means "exceedingly wise";
*Uta-napishti*, "he found life." *Gilgamesh* gives no reason for choosing him
at all (the older Sumerian story does call its hero "humble… reverent").
Genesis: *"Noah found favor in the sight of the LORD"* (6:8) — grace first,
then *righteous* (Session 9).

### 4. A box with no rudder

Uta-napishti's boat is a **cube**, 120 cubits each way, with six decks. He
loads it — *"With all that I had of silver, I loaded it. / With all that I had
of gold, I loaded it"* (XI 81–82) — and his craftsmen, and he hands it to a
pilot: *"To the master of the ship, to Buzur-Amurru, the sailor, / I entrusted
the great house, including its possessions"* (XI 95–96).

Noah's ark is a ***tēbâ***, a box — 300 by 50 by 30 cubits, a long, flat
barge. It carries no silver and no gold: a family, the animals, food. There is
no word in the story for a rudder, an oar, a sail or a pilot. *Tēbâ* appears
in only one other place in the Bible: the pitch-sealed basket in which a mother
set her baby on the Nile (Exodus 2:3, 5). Both times, God carries the future
across the water in a box with no one steering. An ancient Jewish writer put
it this way — *"The hope of the world, taking refuge on a raft… Thy hand
guiding the helm"* (Wisdom 14:6); his English translator added, *"Noah was
not acquainted with navigation."*

### 5. Hungry gods

Why do the Mesopotamian gods care whether anyone survives? Because in
*Atrahasis* (Session 1), humankind was made to do the gods' work — including
feeding them. With the people drowned, the gods go hungry. In our working
translation of *Atrahasis*, *"The Anunna, the great gods, sat in thirst and in
hunger"*; *"like sheep they crowded the trough."* So when the survivor offers
his sacrifice:

> The gods smelled the savor. / The gods smelled the sweet savor. / The gods
> like flies gathered about the sacrificer. *(Gilgamesh XI 161–163)*

Genesis keeps one line and drops the rest. *"And the LORD smelled the soothing
aroma"* (8:21). *Soothing* is ***nîḥōaḥ*** — the sound of Noah's name again,
and the first of forty-three times the Bible uses it; every later use in the
Torah is in the laws of sacrifice. No flies, no hunger, no meal. What follows
the aroma is a **promise**: *"I will never again curse the ground… I will never
again destroy everything that lives"* (8:21). Israel would sing it later:
*"Even if I were hungry, I would not tell you, for the world and all it
contains belong to me. Do I eat the flesh of bulls?"* (Psalm 50:12–13).

### 6. Who grieves — and who objects

In *Gilgamesh* the mother goddess weeps **during** the flood, for what she
herself agreed to:

> The lady of the gods wails with her beautiful voice. / The former day is
> verily turned to clay. / When I spoke evil in the assembly of the gods… *(XI
> 118–120)*

Afterwards Enlil sees the boat and is furious: *"Has anyone come out alive? /
No man shall survive the cataclysm."* And Ea rebukes him — *"On the sinner
place his sin; / On the evil doer place his crimes"* (XI 185–186) — send a
lion, a wolf, a famine, a plague, but not a flood on everyone.

Be honest about this one. The Babylonian story voices the objection that
modern readers feel: *why punish everyone?* In Genesis, no one objects. God
grieves **before** the flood (6:6, Session 9); he remembers Noah **in the
middle** of it (8:1); and afterwards he does not say he was wrong. He promises
never to do it again, *"even though the inclination of their minds is evil
from childhood on"* (8:21). The protest will come — from a man, Abraham, at
Sodom: *"Will not the judge of the whole earth do what is right?"* (18:25).
And God will listen to him.

### 7. A god, or a man of the soil

The survivor's reward in *Gilgamesh*:

> Now, indeed, Uta-Napishtim and (also) his wife shall be equal / Like to us
> gods; in the distance afar at the mouth of the rivers Uta-Napishtim shall
> dwell *(XI 203–206)*

The Sumerian hero is given *"life like a god"*; Berossus's is *"gone to live
with the gods."* That is the point of the story for Gilgamesh: it happened
once, and it will not happen for him.

Genesis: *"Noah, a man of the soil, began to plant a vineyard"* (9:20). The
ground, cursed since 3:17, yields again — the hope his father spoke when he
named him *Rest*, *"because of the ground that the LORD has cursed"* (5:29).
Session 7 promised this would land here. It lands with a sting: the first
harvest is wine, and Noah gets drunk and lies uncovered in his tent (9:21).
And *"The entire lifetime of Noah was 950 years, and then he died"* (9:29) —
the refrain of Genesis 5 returns. Genesis will not make a god of its survivor;
it hardly lets him stay a hero. The hero of this story is the God who
remembered him.

**A note on 9:25.** *"Cursed be Canaan!"* — Canaan, Ham's son, not Ham; and
nothing about skin or Africa. The rabbis already asked: *"Ḥam committed the sin
and Canaan was cursed? This is astonishing"* (*Genesis Rabbah* 36:7). Centuries
later a "curse of Ham" was used to justify enslaving Black Africans, and was
preached in defence of American slavery. The text does not say it, and the
churches that once taught it have renounced it (the Southern Baptist
Convention did so formally in 2018). More on **[Noah among his
readers](texts/early-readers.html)**, and next week, when Noah's sons become
the nations.

### 8. Fewer people, or more?

*Atrahasis* ends with the gods solving the problem of humankind for good. Its
last column (in our working translation) orders: *"let there be a third
(category) among the peoples: among the peoples, the woman who gives birth, and
the woman who does not give birth. Let there be among the peoples the Pāšittu
(the 'Obliterator'); let her seize the baby from the lap of the one who bore
it"* — and orders of priestesses who may
not bear children. **Fewer people**, so that the gods can sleep.

Genesis:

> Then God blessed Noah and his sons and said to them, "Be fruitful and
> multiply and fill the earth." *(9:1)*

It is the blessing of 1:28, word for word — and God says it twice (9:1, 9:7).
Two words of Genesis 1 are missing: *subdue* and *rule*. In their place, the
animals will fear us (9:2) — *"fear and dread were restored, but dominion was
not restored,"* said the rabbis (*Genesis Rabbah* 34:12). And then the law:

> "Whoever sheds human blood, by other humans must his blood be shed; for in
> God's image God has made humankind." *(9:6)*

Frymer-Kensky saw the shape of it. *Atrahasis* answers its flood with fewer
births. Genesis answers its flood — which came because of violence — with
more people, and a law that protects each one, because each bears God's
image. Rabbi Akiva: *"Anyone who sheds blood, it is considered for him as
though he has diminished God's image"* (*Genesis Rabbah* 34:14). Judaism
calls 9:1–7 the covenant with all humankind, the source of the seven
commandments of the children of Noah.

### 9. What each one remembers

The goddess lifts a necklace of lapis lazuli beads carved as **flies** — the
flies the drowned became, the flies the gods became at the sacrifice — and
swears by it:

> 'O ye Gods! I will (rather) forget (this) my necklet of sapphires, / Than
> not maintain these days in remembrance, nor ever forget them. *(XI 165–167,
> Thompson)*

(Last week's handout quoted an older translation of this line, which modern
editions read differently; the meaning is the one here.)

God says: *"I will place my rainbow in the clouds"* (9:13). The Hebrew says
*my **bow*** — ***qešet***, the word for an archer's weapon in 72 of its 76
uses in the Bible, including God's own (*"He has prepared to shoot his bow,"*
Psalm 7:12). In Babylon's creation epic (Session 1), Marduk hung his war-bow in the
sky as a star after his battle. Many readers since the nineteenth century
have heard the same picture here — God hanging up his weapon when the storm
is over — though others think *bow* simply describes the shape.
Josephus already called it *"the bow of God."*

And the bow is a sign of something new in the Bible: the first **covenant**.
The word comes seven times in ten verses (9:8–17), and the partners keep
widening — *"you and your descendants… every living creature that is with
you"* — until it is *"between me and the earth"* (9:13). Nothing is asked of
them in return. And the sign is for God to look at: *"I will notice it and
remember the perpetual covenant between God and all living creatures"* (9:16).
The goddess remembers her grief. God remembers his promise. Isaiah's exiles
held him to it: *"this is like in Noah's time, when I vowed that the waters of
Noah's flood would never again cover the earth. In the same way I have vowed
that I will not be angry at you"* (Isaiah 54:9).

## Part 3 — Floods around the world

**"Every culture has a flood story."** It is not quite true. Flood stories are
found on **every inhabited continent** — but **not in every culture**. They
are common in South and Southeast Asia, the Pacific and the Americas. They are
rare in Europe, missing from ancient Egypt and from the classical books of
China and Japan, and nearly absent from Africa. Sir James Frazer, who
collected hundreds a century ago, wrote: *"In Africa, including Egypt, native
legends of a great flood are conspicuously absent; indeed no single clear case
of one has yet been reported."* Antarctica, which never had people, has none.

**What the chart shows.** Read it from the top down. The **outline** — water,
a warning, a boat, a mountain — is found in many places. The details that
carry Genesis's **meaning** are found almost nowhere else. And where a story
does match Genesis closely, look at the date. In 1823 a missionary in Hawaiʻi
preached on Noah, and his hearers told him their own tradition, of two people
saved on a mountain-top; and *"they said they had never before heard of a
ship, or of Noah."* By 1878, the version a Hawaiian convert gave had a
houseboat, eight survivors, a sacrifice and a rainbow. The same pattern appears
in Fiji, among the Maasai, and in the late Greek tellings: the closer a story
matches Genesis, the later it was written down. Several cultures say the
opposite of "never again" — Egypt, the Nahua, Fiji and Chile's Mapuche all
expected the water to come back.

**Why are there so many?** Three answers, and they can be combined. **One
flood, remembered by the nations** — the oldest answer (Josephus: *"all the
writers of barbarian histories make mention of this flood"*); the stories
nearest Israel keep the most detail, and a memory carried for thousands of
years would keep the outline and lose the rest. Its hard question: the closest
matches come after missionaries, and Africa, near the Bible's own world, has
almost none. **Floods happen wherever people live** — they are the commonest
disaster on earth, and after the last ice age the seas rose by some 120 metres.
Its hard question: why do so many stories reach for *all* of humankind?
**Stories travel** — in the ancient world, and with the Bible. Its hard
question: it cannot explain Manu, or the Maya. All three are laid out on the
sheet.

What none of them changes is the point of today. The existence of flood stories
proves less than both sides have claimed: it does not show that Genesis copied
a myth, and by itself it does not prove Genesis true. **The outline was common
property. The details were not.**

Genesis 10–11: Noah's three sons become the nations of the world — and the
nations build a city and a tower, *"so that we may make a name for
ourselves"* (11:4). Gilgamesh wanted a name too.

## So what?

**1. The first hearers knew the story. What was new was the God.** Not a
council that panics, starves and quarrels, but one God who grieves before he
judges, remembers in the middle, and promises afterwards.

**2. God needs nothing from us — and binds himself to us anyway.** The gods of
Babylon needed humans to feed them. The LORD smells the offering and answers
with a promise, a covenant with *"every living creature"* and with the earth
itself, and he asks nothing in return.

**3. The answer to violence was more life, and every life protected.**
Babylon's gods solved the human problem with fewer births. Genesis answers
with *"be fruitful and multiply"* and with the reason no human life may be
taken lightly: *"in God's image God has made humankind."*

**4. "Never again" — and a warning.** The bow still hangs in the cloud, and
God still looks at it. But Jesus read the flood as a picture of his own coming
— *"they knew nothing until the flood came and took them all away"* (Matthew
24:39) — and Peter as a picture of baptism, the washing that does reach the
heart (1 Peter 3:21).

<div class="discuss" markdown="1">
**Talk about it — to close.** *First:* the neighbours' gods remembered their
own grief; the God of Genesis remembers his promise. What difference does it
make which kind of God you pray to? *Second, this week:* every time you see a
rainbow, read it the Hebrew way — a weapon hung up — and give thanks for one
promise God has kept.

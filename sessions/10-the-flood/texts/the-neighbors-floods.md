# The neighbours' floods

A source sheet for **Session 10**. It gathers the flood stories Israel's
neighbours told — the Babylonian ***Atrahasis***, Tablet XI of
***Gilgamesh***, the older **Sumerian** flood story, and the Babylonian priest
**Berossus** — and sets them beside Genesis 6–9. The rest of the world's flood
stories are on **[floods around the world](floods-around-the-world.html)**;
the Hebrew details on **[the Flood in Hebrew](the-flood-in-hebrew.html)**.

You have met two of these before. **Session 1** read the opening of
*Atrahasis*, where the gods make humankind out of clay to carry their labour;
**Session 8** met Gilgamesh, the king who wanted a name. Today we read the
flood they share.

**A word about translations.** The best modern translations of these texts
are in copyright, so this sheet quotes older public-domain ones: **A. T.
Clay** (1922), who prints the Akkadian with a very literal English, and **R.
Campbell Thompson** (1928), who turned *Gilgamesh* into English verse. Both
are a century old. Where scholars now read a line differently, the sheet says
so, and sometimes gives **our own working translation** of the Akkadian,
always labelled. Line numbers for *Gilgamesh* XI follow the standard modern
edition (A. R. George, 2003).

## 1. The texts, and where they were found

| Text | Oldest copy | Found at | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***Atrahasis*** (Old Babylonian) | copied by the scribe Ipiq-Aya, **c. 1635 BCE** (composed by c. 1700) | Sippar, Babylonia | a three-tablet epic of 1,245 lines: creation of humankind → noise → plague, drought, famine → flood → a new order |
| **Sumerian Flood Story** | c. 1600 BCE | Nippur | creation → kingship → five cities → flood; the hero is **Ziusudra** |
| *Atrahasis* in the West | 14th–13th c. BCE | **Ugarit** (coastal Syria), **Ḫattuša** (Hittite capital), Nippur | including a first-person version, and a fragment with a dove |
| ***Gilgamesh*** in the West | 2nd half of 2nd millennium BCE | **Megiddo** (Canaan), Emar, Ugarit, Ḫattuša; translated into Hittite and Hurrian | the Megiddo piece is from a different part of the epic (Enkidu's death), not the flood |
| ***Gilgamesh*** Tablet XI (Standard version) | 8th–7th c. BCE | **Nineveh** (Ashurbanipal's library) and elsewhere | the flood told by its survivor to Gilgamesh; it borrows its flood from *Atrahasis* |
| **Berossus**, *Babyloniaca* | c. 280 BCE | known through later Greek writers (Eusebius, Syncellus) | a Babylonian priest's history in Greek; his flood hero is Xisouthros |

**Did the first hearers of Genesis know these stories?** The flood tradition
was copied in Babylonia for over a thousand years, and *Atrahasis* and
*Gilgamesh* travelled west to Syria, Anatolia and Canaan in the centuries
before Israel. No flood tablet has been found in Israel or Judah itself. So
the careful claim is this: **the story was in the air of their world** — told,
copied and sung across the ancient Near East for well over a thousand years —
and Genesis's own details (the pitch, the mountain, the birds, the sacrifice,
the deity smelling it) show that its writer knew it.

The Mesopotamians themselves treated the flood as a real dividing line in
history. The Sumerian King List (Session 7) runs: *"In 5 cities 8 kings; they
ruled for 241200 years. Then the flood swept over."*

## 2. *Gilgamesh* Tablet XI: the flood, in its survivor's words

Gilgamesh, terrified of dying after his friend Enkidu's death, has crossed the
waters of death to find **Uta-napishti**, the one man who survived the flood
and was given eternal life. He asks how. The survivor tells him a secret.

**The decision (XI 14).** No reason is given.

> The great gods, brought their hearts to send a deluge.
> — Clay

**The warning through a wall (XI 19–22).** Ea, the clever god of the fresh
waters, has sworn with the others not to tell. So he does not tell a man; he
tells the reed house the man lives in:

> Nin-igi-azag—'tis Ea—, (albeit) conspirator with them, / Unto a reed-hut
> their counsel betray'd he: "O Reed-hut, O Reed-hut! Wall, wall! Hearken, O
> Reed-hut, consider, O Wall!"
> — Thompson

(Clay renders XI 19 as Ea "counseled with them"; the Akkadian is now read
*"Ea was under oath with them."*)

**Build a ship (XI 24–31).**

> Tear down the house, build a ship! / Leave possessions, take thought for
> life! / Abandon property; save life! / Bring into the ship the seed of life
> of everything!
> — Clay

> Let its dimensions be measured! / Let its breadth and its length be
> proportioned!
> — Clay

**What to tell the neighbours (XI 35–47).** Uta-napishti asks, *"[But what]
shall I answer the city, the people, and the elders?"* Ea gives him the
words:

> [I kn]ow that Enlil hates me, and / I may not dwell in your city; / Nor on
> the soil of Enlil set my face. / I will go down to the ocean; with [Ea] my
> lord, I will dwell.
> — Clay

And he promises the city plenty: *"[Upon] you will he (Enlil) then rain
abundance. / [A catch of] birds; a catch of fish."* The last lines (XI 46–47)
are a riddle — in our working translation, ***"In the morning, bread-cakes; at
nightfall he will rain on you a shower of wheat."*** The townspeople hear a
promise of food. Modern scholars (A. R. George; M. Worthington, *Ea's
Duplicity in the Gilgamesh Flood Story*, 2019) read the words as deliberately
double: the "rain" that is coming is not wheat.

**The boat: a cube (XI 57–67).**

> I placed its walls about its perimeter 120 cubits high. / … / 120 cubits was
> proportioned the length of its upper part.
> — Clay

Modern editions read it as an acre in area, with walls ten rods (about 60
metres) high and a deck ten rods on each side — **a cube**. *"I covered it
(i. e. made decks) six times. / I divided (into divisions) seven times. / I
divided its interior nine times."* It is caulked with ***kupru***, pitch — the
same word as the *kōper* of Genesis 6:14 — which "the rich man" brings (XI
55) and which Uta-napishti pours by the thousands of measures into the kiln
(XI 66).

**A feast for the builders (XI 71–76).**

> For [the people] oxen were slaughtered. / I slew sheep daily. / Must, sesame
> wine, oil and wine. / I gave the workmen to drink like water from the river.
> — Clay

*"[I made a fe]ast like the Akitu festival"* — the New Year.

**The cargo (XI 81–96).**

> With all that I had of silver, I loaded it. / With all that I had of gold, I
> loaded it. / With all the seed of life that there was, I loaded it. / I
> caused to go up into the ship all my family and relatives. / The cattle of
> the field, the beast of the plain, the craftsmen, all of them, I caused to go
> up.
> — Clay

And a pilot: *"To the master of the ship, to Buzur-Amurru, the sailor, / I
entrusted the great house, including its possessions"* (the name is now read
Puzur-Enlil).

**The storm, and the gods (XI 98–131).**

> There rises from the foundation of the heavens a black cloud. / Adad thunders
> in the midst of it.
> — Clay

*"Brother could not see brother."* And the gods are frightened by what they
have done:

> The gods fear the deluge. / They withdraw, they ascend to the heaven of Anu.
> / The gods cower like a dog; they lie down in the enclosure.
> — Clay

**The goddess weeps (XI 117–127).**

> The lady of the gods wails with her beautiful voice. / The former day is
> verily turned to clay. / When I spoke evil in the assembly of the gods — /
> … / For the destruction of my people, I ordered the cataclysm.
> — Clay

Thompson's version of her complaint: *"Sooth, then, am I to give birth, unto
(these) mine own people Only to glut (with their bodies) the Sea as though
they were fish-spawn?"* *"The gods of Anunnaki weep with her."* (Clay's text
also names Ishtar in XI 117; modern editions take the weeping goddess to be
Bēlet-ilī, the mother goddess who shaped humankind from clay in *Atrahasis*.)

**Seven days (XI 128–139).** *"Six days and six nights"* the storm blows (one
manuscript reads *six days and seven nights*); *"When the seventh day
arrives, the deluge-tempest subsides."*

> The sea rested; the hurricane had spent itself, the flood was at an end. /
> … / And all mankind was turned to clay. / Like a log they floated about. /
> I opened the hatch, and the light fell upon my countenance. / I was
> horrified, and I sat down and wept. / Over my countenance ran my tears.
> — Clay

**The mountain (XI 142).** *"…the Ark on the Mountain of Nisir / Grounded"*
(Thompson; the name is now read Nimush).

**The birds (XI 147–156).**

> When the seventh day arrived, / I brought out and released a dove. / The dove
> went forth; it turned; / It did not have a resting place; it returned. / I
> brought out and released a swallow. / … / I brought out and released a raven.
> / The raven went forth; it saw the drying up of the water; / It approached;
> it waded; it croaked (?); it did not return.
> — Clay

**The sacrifice, and the gods like flies (XI 157–163).**

> I sent (everything) to the four winds. I offered a sacrifice. / I made a
> libation upon the summit of the mountain. / Seven and seven adagur pots I set
> out. / Beneath them I piled reeds, cedar wood and myrtle. / The gods smelled
> the savor. / The gods smelled the sweet savor. / The gods like flies gathered
> about the sacrificer.
> — Clay

**The necklace (XI 165–167).** The mother goddess lifts a string of lapis
lazuli beads carved as **flies**:

> 'O ye Gods! I will (rather) forget (this) my necklet of sapphires, / Than
> not maintain these days in remembrance, nor ever forget them.
> — Thompson

Our working translation of the Akkadian: *"She lifted up the great flies that
Anu had made… 'O gods, these shall be the lapis lazuli of my neck — these days
I will remember, and never forget.'"* (Session 9's teaser quoted Clay's
1922 rendering of this line — *"I shall not forget my necklace"* — which
modern editions read differently.) She bars Enlil from the meal: *"Enlil shall
not come to the offering; / Because he took not counsel; and sent the deluge;
/ And my people he numbered for destruction."*

**Enlil's rage (XI 172–176).**

> He saw the ship; then Enlil was wroth; / He was filled with anger against the
> Igigi gods. / Has anyone come out alive? / No man shall survive the
> cataclysm.
> — Clay

**Ea's rebuke (XI 183–195).** *"Why, O why hast thou not taken counsel; and
hast sent a flood?"*

> On the sinner place his sin; / On the evil doer place his crimes;
> — Clay

> (Aye,) visit his sin on the sinner Visit his guilt on the guilty, (but) O,
> have mercy, that (thereby) He shall not be cut off; be clement, that he may
> not [perish].
> — Thompson

Instead of a flood, Ea says, send a lion, a wolf, a famine, a plague: *"Let a
lion come and diminish the people. … Let a wolf come and diminish the people.
… Let there be a famine and ruin the land. … Let Urra come and destroy the
people."* And he covers himself: *"I have not revealed the decision of the
great gods. / I caused Atra-hasis to see a dream, and he heard the decision of
the gods."* (The slip — calling the hero *Atra-hasis* — is how scholars know
Tablet XI took its flood from *Atrahasis*.)

**The survivor made a god (XI 199–206).**

> (Then) to the Ark came up Enlil; my hand did he grasp, and uplifted / Me,
> even me, and my wife, too, he raised, and, bent-kneed beside me, / Made her
> to kneel; our foreheads he touch'd as he stood there between us, / Blessing
> us … "Now, indeed, Uta-Napishtim and (also) his wife shall be equal / Like to
> us gods; in the distance afar at the mouth of the rivers Uta-Napishtim shall
> dwell"
> — Thompson

That is the point of the story *for Gilgamesh*: the survivor's immortality was
a one-time gift. Gilgamesh will not get it.

## 3. *Atrahasis*: the flood in its original setting

*Atrahasis* tells the flood as the climax of a longer story. In Session 1 we
read its opening: the junior gods went on strike over their labour, and the
gods made humankind from clay and a slain god's blood to do the work. Then:

**The noise (Tablets I–II).** People multiply, and their noise keeps the god
Enlil awake. In Clay's translation of the later Assyrian version:

> [Concerning] their clamor I am troubled.
> — *Atrahasis*, Assyrian version (Clay)

The Old Babylonian copy opens Tablet II: *"The land had become great; the
people had multiplied"* — and, in our working translation from Clay's own
Akkadian, *"The land was bellowing like a bull. At their uproar the god grew
troubled. [Enlil] heard their clamour."*

**Three tries before the flood.** First plague. Then drought: *"Above, let
Adad make his rain scarce."* *"Let the [womb] be closed, that it bring forth no
infant."* Then famine, which grows so terrible that *"When the sixth year
arrives, they prepare the [daughter] for a meal."* Each time the wise
Atrahasis appeals to his god Enki — *"O lord, mankind is in misery"* — and
each time Enki finds a way around it. And each time, *"[The people] have not
become less; they are more numerous than before."*

**What the noise means.** Scholars disagree. Many (A. D. Kilmer, W. L. Moran,
T. Frymer-Kensky) read it as a sign of **overpopulation**: the gods' problem
is that there are too many people. Others read it as humanity **encroaching
on the gods' sphere** (R. Oden), or as human energy and independence (P.
Michalowski). Either way, it is not wickedness in the Bible's sense.

**The boat — round.** A tablet published in 2014 (the "Ark Tablet") has Enki's
instructions. Our working translation: *"The boat you will build — draw her
out as a circular plan; let her length and her breadth be equal; let her floor
be one acre and her walls one rod high."* Roofed *"like the Apsû"* so the sun
cannot see inside, sealed with *kupru*, pitch.

**The farewell feast.** Atrahasis invites his people to a banquet before he
boards. He cannot sit still; his heart is breaking. (Old Babylonian Tablet
III, col. ii; paraphrased.)

**The gods go hungry.** With humankind drowning, no one is left to feed the
gods. In our working translations from the Akkadian of Tablet III: *"The
Anunna, the great gods, sat in thirst and in hunger."* The mother goddess
weeps, and *"she thirsted for beer. Where she sat weeping, they sat too; like
sheep they crowded the trough. Their lips were parched with dread; from hunger
they kept trembling."* She sees the drowned: *"the offspring have become like
flies."*

**The sacrifice.** When Atrahasis offers his sacrifice, the gods smell it and
*"like flies they gathered over the offering."* The goddess: *"Let these flies
be the lapis of my neck"* — that she may remember the days.

**Enlil, and Enki's answer.** Enlil sees the boat: *"our mouth took the oath
together! Where did the secret get out?"* Enki owns up — and protests, in
words close to Ea's in *Gilgamesh*: *"On the guilty one lay your
punishment."*

**The new order.** And then the gods solve the problem of humankind for good.
Enki tells the birth goddess (our working translation; the first line is
partly restored):

> [You,] birth-goddess, creatress of destinies, [assign death] to the peoples.
> Moreover, let there be a third (category) among the peoples: among the
> peoples, the woman who gives birth, and the woman who does not give birth.
> Let there be among the peoples the *Pāšittu* (the "Obliterator"); let her
> seize the baby from the lap of the one who bore it. Establish
> *ugbabtu*-women, *entu*-women and *egiṣītu*-women; let them be taboo, and
> (so) cut off childbirth.
> — *Atrahasis* III vi 47–vii 9

Barren women; a demon who snatches newborns; orders of priestesses forbidden
to bear children. **Fewer people**, so that the gods can sleep. (The word
*death* in the first line is a scholar's restoration, not legible text.)

## 4. The Sumerian flood: Ziusudra

Older still, and broken into pieces. The gods swear an oath: *"An, Enlil,
Enki and Ninhursaja made all the gods of heaven and earth take an oath by
invoking An and Enlil."* The hero is a king and a priest, and — unlike
*Gilgamesh*'s survivor — he is described as devout:

> In those days Zi-ud-sura the king, the gudu priest, ....... He fashioned
> ....... The humble, committed, reverent ....... Day by day, standing
> constantly at ....... Something that was not a dream appeared,
> — ETCSL 1.7.4, Segment C

The warning comes through a wall again: *"Side-wall standing at my left side,
....... Side-wall, I will speak words to you; take heed of my words, pay
attention to my instructions."* And: *"A decision that the seed of mankind is
to be destroyed has been made. The verdict, the word of the divine assembly,
cannot be revoked."* The storm rocks *"the huge boat for seven days and seven
nights"*; *"The king sacrificed oxen and offered innumerable sheep."* At the
end, *"they granted him life like a god, they brought down to him eternal
life,"* and *"because of preserving the animals and the seed of mankind, they
settled Zi-ud-sura the king in an overseas country, in the land Dilmun, where
the sun rises."*

## 5. Berossus: the flood in Greek

A Babylonian priest writing for a Greek king around 280 BCE. His hero,
Xisouthros, is warned in a dream: *"Kronos appeared to him in his sleep and
said that on the fifteenth of the month Daisios men would be destroyed by a
deluge."* He is to build a boat five furlongs long *"and two furlongs in
width,"* and *"go aboard it with his family and close friends"* — and if
asked where he is going, to say *"To the gods, in order to pray that men may
have blessings."* The birds go out three times: *"as they found no food nor
place to alight, they came back into the boat"*; then *"they came back to the
boat with their feet muddy"*; *"But when they were let go for the third time,
they did not come back to the boat again."* He lands in Armenia, *"built an
altar, and sacrificed to the gods,"* and vanishes: *"because of his piety he
was gone to live with the gods."* In Berossus's day, *"people get pitch from
the boat by scraping it off, and use it for amulets."*

## 7. How are the stories related?

Readers of every kind agree that these stories are related — the same outline,
and some of the same details, from the reed wall's warning to the birds. They
disagree about **how**.

**Genesis borrowed and answered.** Since George Smith deciphered the flood
tablet in 1872, many scholars have held that the Hebrew story came, directly
or indirectly, from the Babylonian. L. W. King (1918): *"The general
dependence of the biblical Versions upon the Babylonian legend as a whole has
long been recognized."* Tikva Frymer-Kensky (*Biblical Archaeologist*, 1977)
argued that Genesis knew the *Atrahasis* pattern — creation, a problem, a
flood, a solution — and deliberately rejected its theme: Genesis is not about
too many people, and it answers *Atrahasis*'s population controls with *"be
fruitful"* and with laws against bloodshed. The single Babylonian word in
Genesis 6:14, *kōper*, "pitch," supports some contact. *Its strength:* the
details cluster exactly where the theology differs. *The question it has to
answer:* a shared word, or a shared outline, does not prove copying.

**Both remember the same event.** Others hold that Genesis and the
Mesopotamian stories are independent memories of a real, ancient flood, and
that Genesis tells it truly. A. R. Millard, an evangelical Assyriologist who
co-edited *Atrahasis*, argued that borrowing has not been shown and that the
two accounts describe the same flood (*Tyndale Bulletin*, 1967); K. A. Kitchen
held that they go back to a common tradition without borrowing from each other
(*Ancient Orient and Old Testament*, 1966). Even A. Heidel (1946), who called
the versions "genetically related," judged the arguments for Babylonian
dependence indecisive. *Its strength:* the Mesopotamians themselves dated their
history by the flood. *The question it has to answer:* archaeology has found
flood deposits at several Mesopotamian cities, but they are local and of
different dates; the event, if there was one, cannot be pinned down.

These two views are not exclusive. One can hold that a real flood lies behind
all the stories, and that Genesis, written for a people who knew the others,
tells it in a way that answers them. The New Testament treats Noah's flood as
a real event (Matthew 24:37–39; 2 Peter 3:6).

**What none of them settles** is what Genesis *means*. That is in the
differences.

## 8. Where Genesis differs — and where it doesn't

An honest list, so that no difference is claimed that is not there.

- **One God.** *Unique, and the deepest difference.* In Mesopotamia the god who
  destroys (Enlil) and the god who saves (Enki/Ea) are rivals. In Genesis one
  God decides, warns, saves and makes covenant — so the tension moves *inside*
  God (6:6–8; 8:21), Session 9's theme.
- **A moral cause.** *Mostly distinctive.* *Gilgamesh* and Berossus give no
  cause; *Atrahasis* gives noise, whose meaning is debated. But Ea's rebuke
  assumes there is such a thing as "the sinner." What Genesis adds is a moral
  diagnosis, given *before* the flood, by the one God.
- **A hero chosen for righteousness.** *Partly shared.* Ziusudra is "humble,
  reverent"; Atrahasis is Enki's devotee; Berossus names piety. Only
  *Gilgamesh* is silent. Genesis's own note is that Noah first *found favour*
  (6:8).
- **No trickery.** *Mostly distinctive.* Ea evades his oath by talking to a
  wall and gives the hero a misleading message for his city. Genesis's God
  speaks openly — but Genesis also gives Noah no message for his neighbours.
- **Gods frightened and hungry.** *Unique to the neighbours.* Genesis has
  nothing like it.
- **The sacrifice.** *Shared, and changed.* Genesis keeps the deity *smelling*
  the offering (8:21), and drops the flies and the hunger. The smell leads to a
  promise, not a meal.
- **Grief before or after.** *Not a clean contrast.* The LORD grieves before
  (6:6); the goddess during and after. But God also speaks after the flood,
  resolving never to do it again *"as I have just done"* (8:21).
- **Who objects.** *A real difference, in an unexpected direction.* In
  Mesopotamia a god protests on moral grounds — punish the guilty, not
  everyone. In Genesis no one objects to the flood; the protest *"Will not the
  judge of the whole earth do what is right?"* comes later, from a man, at
  Sodom (18:25).
- **The aftermath.** *The strongest structural contrast.* Fewer births in
  *Atrahasis*; *"be fruitful and multiply"* and a law protecting human life in
  Genesis (9:1–7).
- **The hero's fate.** *Unique.* Elsewhere the survivor becomes immortal and
  disappears. Noah stays mortal, farms, gets drunk and dies (9:20–29).
- **A covenant with all life.** *The covenant is unique; the remembering is
  shared.* The goddess wears her flies *"that I may remember the days"*; God
  sets his bow and says *"I will remember my covenant"* (9:15).
- **The ark's shape.** *Real, but not theological.* A ship-like box in Genesis,
  a cube in *Gilgamesh*, a round coracle in the Ark Tablet, 5:2 in Berossus.
- **The birds.** *Shared, in a different order.* *Gilgamesh*: dove, swallow,
  raven. Genesis: raven, then the dove three times. Berossus: three releases,
  the second with muddy feet.

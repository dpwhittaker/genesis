# The Flood in Hebrew: what the comparison turns on

A source sheet for **Session 10**. The whole Flood narrative, Genesis
6:5–9:19, is laid out on Session 9's sheet **[the Flood, told
twice](../../09-regret-and-grace/texts/the-flood-told-twice.html)**, with ten
notes on its Hebrew. This sheet adds the last verses of the chapter and twelve
more notes — the details that matter when you set Genesis beside *Atrahasis*
and *Gilgamesh* (on **[the neighbours' floods](the-neighbors-floods.html)**).

All counts are from the Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible. Where the NET's English
hides a Hebrew repetition, the note says so.

---

## The last verses: Genesis 9:18–29

> **18** The sons of Noah who came out of the ark were Shem, Ham, and Japheth.
> (Now Ham was the father of Canaan.) **19** These were the three sons of Noah,
> and from them the whole earth was populated.
>
> **20** Noah, a man of the soil, began to plant a vineyard. **21** When he
> drank some of the wine, he got drunk and uncovered himself inside his tent.
> **22** Ham, the father of Canaan, saw his father's nakedness and told his two
> brothers who were outside. **23** Shem and Japheth took the garment and
> placed it on their shoulders. Then they walked in backwards and covered up
> their father's nakedness. Their faces were turned the other way so they did
> not see their father's nakedness.
>
> **24** When Noah awoke from his drunken stupor he learned what his youngest
> son had done to him. **25** So he said, "Cursed be Canaan! The lowest of
> slaves he will be to his brothers." **26** He also said, "Worthy of praise is
> the Lord, the God of Shem! May Canaan be the slave of Shem! **27** May God
> enlarge Japheth's territory and numbers! May he live in the tents of Shem and
> may Canaan be the slave of Japheth!"
>
> **28** After the flood Noah lived 350 years. **29** The entire lifetime of
> Noah was 950 years, and then he died.

## Twelve notes

### 1. Noah never speaks

Read every verb of speaking in Genesis 6–9 and look at who says it. From 6:13
to 9:17, the speaker is always God. Noah does not answer, ask, argue, pray or
warn. What the narrator says of him instead is a refrain: *"And Noah did all
that God commanded him—he did indeed"* (6:22); *"And Noah did all that the
LORD commanded him"* (7:5). Noah's first words in the Bible are the curse and
blessings of 9:25–27, after the flood is over. (In *Gilgamesh*, by contrast,
the survivor tells the whole story himself, in the first person.)

God's first words to him are starker than the NET lets them sound. *"I have
decided that all living creatures must die"* (6:13) is, in Hebrew, *qēṣ
kol-bāśār bāʾ ləpānay* — "the end of all flesh has come before me" — the
idiom the prophets use for doom (*"The end has come,"* Amos 8:2; Ezekiel 7:2).

### 2. *Tēbâ*: a box, not a ship

The word for the ark, ***tēbâ***, occurs 28 times in the Bible: 26 times in
Genesis 6–9, and twice more — for the basket in which the baby Moses was laid
on the Nile (Exodus 2:3, 5). The NET calls that one *"a papyrus basket"*; in
Hebrew, Moses goes into an ark. The lexicons trace it to an Egyptian word for a
chest or coffin — *"prob. Egypt. loan-word from Ṯ-b-t, chest, coffin"*
(Brown–Driver–Briggs, 1906). The Bible's ordinary word for a ship, *ʾŏnîyâ*,
never appears in the Flood story, and neither does any word for a sailor, a
pilot, an oar, a mast or a sail. The ark has a door, decks and a roof, and
nothing to steer it with.

### 3. *Kōper*: pitch — and perhaps a borrowed word

*"Cover it with pitch inside and out"* (6:14) is, in Hebrew, *wəkāpartā… bakkōper*
— "pitch it with pitch." This ***kōper*** occurs **only here** in the Bible;
everywhere else the Bible's words are *ḥēmār*, "bitumen" (11:3; 14:10), and
*zepet*, "pitch" — Moses' basket is sealed with both (Exodus 2:3), not with
*kōper*. It matches the Babylonian word, Akkadian ***kupru***, which, as S. R.
Driver noted in 1904, is *"used repeatedly by Nebuchadnezzar in his descriptions of
buildings, and also occurring in the Babylonian account of the Flood"* —
*"it is possible therefore that kōpher came into Heb., with the story, from
Babylonia."* The word may be a souvenir of the story the first hearers
already knew. *May*: bitumen was a Mesopotamian trade good, and its name could
have travelled with the cargo rather than the story (A. Heidel made this
point). The stronger evidence is the whole cluster of shared details — the
mountain, the birds, the altar, the aroma.

You may have heard that *kōper* is the word for *atonement*. The letters are
the same (*k-p-r*), and preachers have long made the connection. The standard
lexicon (Brown–Driver–Briggs, 1906) lists them as two different words. Whether
they share a distant ancestor is still debated — Akkadian has *kapāru*, "wipe,
smear," and *kuppuru*, "purify," and some scholars (Jacob Milgrom, for one)
read the Bible's *kipper* as "purge." Either way, the verb in 6:14 — the only
time in the Bible this verb appears in its simple form — means "coat it with
pitch." Its 101 other uses, all in other forms, mean "atone," "appease,"
"purge." Genesis 6:14 is about waterproofing, not atonement.

### 4. 300 × 50 × 30

*"The ark is to be 450 feet long, 75 feet wide, and 45 feet high"* (6:15) —
in Hebrew, 300 cubits by 50 by 30. Six times as long as it is wide; a long,
flat box, like a barge. (The boat in *Gilgamesh* XI is a cube.) It has three
decks, a door in the side, and a roof or opening called a ***ṣōhar***, a word
found nowhere else in this form; *ṣohŏrayim*, from the same root, means
"noon." The rabbis split over it: *"Rabbi Abba bar Kahana said: It was a
window. Rabbi Levi said: It was a [luminous] precious stone"* (*Genesis Rabbah*
31:11) — and Rashi gives both. The
*"rooms"* of 6:14 are literally ***qinnîm***, "nests."

### 5. *Bərît*: the first time the Bible says "covenant"

***Bərît***, covenant, appears for the first time in the Bible at 6:18 — *"but
I will confirm my covenant with you"* — a promise made before the rain:
covenant comes before rescue. (Some Christian traditions speak of an earlier
covenant with Adam; the Westminster Confession calls it "the first covenant."
The *word* first appears here.) Then it comes **seven times** in 9:8–17.
The partners keep widening: *"with you and your descendants after you and with
every living creature that is with you"* (9:9–10), and finally *"between me
and the earth"* (9:13). The animals are named partners, not cargo. The
promise has no conditions; the laws of 9:1–7 come before it, not as its
price. And the sign is
for *God* to look at: *"I will notice it and remember the perpetual covenant"*
(9:16). The verb is *hēqîm*, "establish, confirm," not "cut" — the only
"cutting" in this covenant is the promise that no flesh will be *cut off*
again (9:11).

### 6. *Zākar*: "God remembered"

*"But God remembered Noah"* (8:1) — and then the wind blows and the waters go
down. The narrator says *God remembered* at the turning points of the Bible's
story: Abraham, and Lot is pulled out of Sodom (19:29); Rachel, and Joseph is
born (30:22); *"God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with
Jacob"* (Exodus 2:24), and the Exodus begins; Hannah, and Samuel is born (1
Samuel 1:19). Every time, rescue or a birth follows. The NET hides three of
them — *"God honored Abraham's request,"* *"God took note of Rachel,"* *"the
LORD called her to mind."*

### 7. *Rêaḥ hannîḥōaḥ*: the soothing aroma

*"And the LORD smelled the soothing aroma"* (8:21). ***Nîḥōaḥ***, "soothing,
restful," occurs 43 times in the Bible, 39 of them in the Torah — and 8:21 is
the first. All but one of its later uses in the Torah are in the laws of
sacrifice; the exception is a threat *not* to smell them (Leviticus 26:31). The
Greek Bible translates it *osmē euōdias*, "a fragrant aroma" — the phrase Paul
uses of Christ's self-offering: *"a sacrificial and fragrant offering to God"*
(Ephesians 5:2). The rabbis drew a line through every offering called a
"soothing aroma": *"there is no 'eating' or 'drinking' before the Holy One
Blessed be He, but (that His) only ('gratification') is that He speaks and His
will is done"* — and then quote Psalm 50:12–13 (*Sifrei Bamidbar* 143). And the
sound of Noah's name, *nōaḥ*, runs through the whole ending: the ark *rested*
(*wattānaḥ*, 8:4); the dove found no *resting place* (*mānôaḥ*, 8:9); the
LORD smelled the *restful* aroma (*nîḥōaḥ*, 8:21).

### 8. The birds

The **raven** *"kept flying back and forth"* — in Hebrew, *"it went out,
going out and returning"* — and never came back to Noah. The **dove** went
out three times: no resting place; then *"a freshly plucked olive leaf in its
beak"* — *ṭārāp*, "freshly plucked," a word found only here — then it did not
return. The first olive in the Bible. And a wordplay the NET cannot show: the
waters *"had receded"* (8:8, 11) is *qallû*, literally "had grown light," and
ten verses later God promises never again to *ləqallēl* the ground — to treat
it as light, to curse it (8:21). The same root.

### 9. Genesis 9:1–7: the blessing again, with fear in place of rule

> Then God blessed Noah and his sons and said to them, "Be fruitful and
> multiply and fill the earth." *(9:1)*

The first three Hebrew words are exactly those of 1:28: *pərû ûrəbû ûmilʾû
ʾet-hāʾāreṣ*. Then Genesis 1 went on, *"subdue it! Rule over the fish of the
sea…"* — and here those two verbs are gone. In their place: *"Every living
creature… will be terrified of you"* (9:2; Hebrew *"fear of you and dread of
you"*). Meat is now permitted (9:3), but not *"with its life (that is, its
blood) in it"* (9:4). And then the reason human life is protected:

> "Whoever sheds human blood, by other humans must his blood be shed; for in
> God's image God has made humankind." *(9:6)*

The Hebrew is a mirror: *šōpēk dam hāʾādām / bāʾādām dāmô yiššāpēk* — "sheds
the blood of the human / by the human his blood shall be shed." *Image*,
*ṣelem*, occurs in Genesis only at 1:26–27, 5:3 and here. Its last use in
Genesis is the reason murder is the gravest crime. The section is bracketed by
the blessing — *"be fruitful and multiply"* (9:1, 9:7).

### 10. *Qešet*: the bow

*"I will place my rainbow in the clouds"* (9:13) — in Hebrew, *"my **bow** I
have set in the cloud."* ***Qešet*** occurs 76 times in the Bible. In four it
means the rainbow (Genesis 9:13, 14, 16 and Ezekiel 1:28). In the other 72 it
is the archer's weapon — including God's own: *"He has prepared to shoot his
bow"* (Psalm 7:12); *"He prepared his bow like an enemy"* (Lamentations 2:4);
*"Your bow is ready for action"* (Habakkuk 3:9). Josephus already called it
*"the bow of God"*; Nachmanides, in the thirteenth century, reported earlier
commentators who saw a war-bow turned away from the earth, as warriors invert
their weapons to sue for peace; and many modern scholars since Wellhausen and
Gunkel have read 9:13 as God hanging up his war-bow when the storm is over.
Others think *bow* simply describes the shape (both sides are on **[Noah among
his readers](early-readers.html)**, §5). One prophet sets the two bows side by
side, though he does not cite Genesis: in the coming covenant *"with the wild
animals, the birds of the air, and the creatures that crawl on the ground,"*
God says, *"I will abolish the warrior's bow and sword"* (Hosea 2:18).

### 11. "A man of the soil" (9:20)

*"Noah, a man of the soil, began to plant a vineyard."* The ground,
*ʾădāmâ*, has been under a curse since 3:17; it would not yield for Cain
(4:11–12); and Noah was named in hope *"because of the ground that the LORD
has cursed"* (5:29). Now God has said *"I will never again curse the ground"*
(8:21), and the ground grows a vineyard. Session 7's promise is kept — with a
sting. The first harvest is wine, and the next verse is drunkenness. *Began*,
*wayyāḥel*, marks new starts in Genesis 4–11 (6:1; 10:8; 11:6), and several
of them go wrong.

### 12. "Cursed be Canaan" (9:25)

Canaan is named five times in these twelve verses; Ham is always *"the father
of Canaan."* The curse falls on **Canaan**, not on Ham, and says nothing of
skin or of any people but Canaan. It is the first curse in the Bible spoken by
a human being, and the first appearance of the word ***ʿebed***, *slave*. How
these verses were later misused, and how the earliest readers answered the
question "why Canaan?", is on **[Noah among his readers](early-readers.html)**,
§8.

## Firsts in Genesis 6–9

The Flood story is full of first appearances: the first use of the word
**covenant** (6:18);
the first **altar** (8:20); the first **soothing aroma** (8:21); the first
**olive** (8:11); the first **cloud** (9:13) — its next appearance is the
pillar of cloud in Exodus 13:21; the first **sign of a covenant** (9:12; the
next is circumcision, 17:11); the first **wine** (9:21); the first **slave**
(9:25); and the first words of **Noah** (9:25).

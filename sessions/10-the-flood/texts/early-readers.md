# Noah among his readers

A source sheet for **Session 10**. The Mesopotamian floods are on
**[the neighbours' floods](the-neighbors-floods.html)**; the world's flood
stories on **[floods around the world](floods-around-the-world.html)**; the
Hebrew details on **[the Flood in Hebrew](the-flood-in-hebrew.html)**. This
sheet gathers how Jews and Christians read the Flood — from the centuries
around Jesus to the Middle Ages — including the readers who had heard the
nations' flood stories too, and said what they made of them.

**How to read it.** The New Testament comes first, because for this class it
is authoritative: where it reads a passage, that reading settles the question
for us. The other readers are witnesses to how
the story was heard: sometimes they fill a silence in Genesis, sometimes they
argue with each other, and sometimes they notice exactly what we notice. Dates
are given so you can see who was reading whom. For the Jewish sources: the
Mishnah (including *Pirkei Avot*), edited c. 200 CE; the Tosefta and *Sifrei*,
3rd century; *Genesis Rabbah*, c. 5th century, though its sages taught in the
2nd–4th; the Babylonian Talmud, c. 6th century; Rashi (1040–1105); Abraham ibn
Ezra (c. 1089–1167); Maimonides (1138–1204); Nachmanides (1194–1270); the
Zohar, late 13th century.

## 1. The New Testament

> For just like the days of Noah were, so the coming of the Son of Man will
> be. For in those days before the flood, people were eating and drinking,
> marrying and giving in marriage, until the day Noah entered the ark. And
> they knew nothing until the flood came and took them all away. It will be
> the same at the coming of the Son of Man.
> — *Matthew 24:37–39* (also Luke 17:26–27)

Jesus' point is that the flood generation was *ordinary*: eating, marrying —
and unaware.

> By faith Noah, when he was warned about things not yet seen, with reverent
> regard constructed an ark for the deliverance of his family. Through faith
> he condemned the world and became an heir of the righteousness that comes
> by faith.
> — *Hebrews 11:7*

Hebrews adds a sermon in the building: Noah was *warned* by God, and his
obedience *"condemned the world."* Peter calls him a herald, so he proclaimed;
Hebrews says his ark proclaimed too.

> Because Christ also suffered once for sins, the just for the unjust, to bring
> you to God, by being put to death in the flesh but by being made alive in the
> spirit. In it he went and preached to the spirits in prison, after they were
> disobedient long ago when God patiently waited in the days of Noah as an ark
> was being constructed. In the ark a few, that is eight souls, were delivered
> through water. And this prefigured baptism, which now saves you—not the
> washing off of physical dirt but the pledge of a good conscience to God—through
> the resurrection of Jesus Christ, who went into heaven and is at the right
> hand of God with angels and authorities and powers subject to him.
> — *1 Peter 3:18–22*

Verse 19 is one of the hardest in the New Testament, and the church has read
it three main ways. (1) Christ, *"in the spirit,"* preached **through Noah** to
the people of his day, who are now *"in prison"* — Augustine's reading (Letter
164), defended by Wayne Grudem (*Trinity Journal*, 1986); on this reading 1
Peter itself says Noah preached. (2) Christ preached to **the dead** between
his death and resurrection — the reading of many church fathers. (3) The risen
Christ proclaimed his victory to the **imprisoned spirits** of Genesis 6:1–4,
the reading Session 8 followed (W. J. Dalton, *Christ's Proclamation to the
Spirits*, 1965). On every reading Peter's own point is verse 22: Christ is at
God's right hand, with *"angels and authorities and powers subject to him."*

> …but did protect Noah, a herald of righteousness, along with seven others,
> when God brought a flood on an ungodly world, and if he turned to ashes the
> cities of Sodom and Gomorrah… and if he rescued Lot, a righteous man… —if
> so, then the Lord knows how to rescue the godly from their trials, and to
> reserve the unrighteous for punishment at the day of judgment
> — *2 Peter 2:5–9*

The Greek is *ogdoon Nōe dikaiosynēs kēryka* — literally "Noah, the eighth, a
herald of righteousness." Jesus pairs the same two stories: *"Just as it was in
the days of Noah… Likewise, just as it was in the days of Lot… It will be the
same on the day the Son of Man is revealed"* (Luke 17:26–30). And Peter's
second letter reads the Flood as the pattern of a judgment still to come:

> For they deliberately suppress this fact, that by the word of God heavens
> existed long ago and an earth was formed out of water and by means of water.
> Through these things the world existing at that time was destroyed when it
> was deluged with water. But by the same word the present heavens and earth
> have been reserved for fire, by being kept for the day of judgment and
> destruction of the ungodly.
> — *2 Peter 3:5–7*

And it explains the delay: *"The Lord is not slow concerning his promise, as
some regard slowness, but is being patient toward you because he does not wish
for any to perish but for all to come to repentance"* (3:9).

**The prophets before them.** Isaiah's exiles heard the Flood as an oath: *"As
far as I am concerned, this is like in Noah's time, when I vowed that the
waters of Noah's flood would never again cover the earth. In the same way I
have vowed that I will not be angry at you or shout at you. Even if the
mountains are removed and the hills displaced, my devotion will not be removed
from you, nor will my covenant of friendship be displaced"* (Isaiah 54:9–10).
Ezekiel says that even men as righteous as Noah, Daniel and Job *"would save
only their own lives by their righteousness"* (Ezekiel 14:14).

**The two arks.** Matthew, Luke, Hebrews and 1 Peter call Noah's ark
*kibōtos*, and Hebrews 9:4 uses the same word for the ark of the covenant. In
Hebrew the two arks have different names (*tēbâ* and *ʾărôn*); in Greek they
share one.

## 2. Did Noah warn anyone?

**Genesis is silent.** In the whole Flood story Noah never says a word: every
speech from 6:13 to 9:17 is God's, and Noah's first recorded words are the
curse and blessings of 9:25–27. What Genesis gives instead is a delay — *"They will
remain for 120 more years"* (6:3) — and a man who *"did all that God commanded
him"* (6:22). The New Testament calls him *"a herald of righteousness"* (2
Peter 2:5), and the tradition, Jewish and Christian, filled in what he said.
As the translator of the Sibylline Oracles noted in 1899: *"The O. T.
narrative of the flood records nothing of Noah's preaching."*

**The rabbis.** *Genesis Rabbah* 30:7:

> …all one hundred and twenty years, Noah would plant cedars and chop them
> down [for timber]. They said to him: 'Why are you doing this?' He said to
> them: 'So said the Master of the world, that He is bringing a flood upon the
> world.'

Rabbi Abba bar Kahana puts it in God's mouth: *"I had a single herald who
arose for me in the generation of the Flood – this is Noah"* — the same idea as 2 Peter's *kēryx*. In the
Talmud (*Sanhedrin* 108a), Rabbi Yosei of Caesarea: *"This teaches that Noah
the righteous would rebuke the people of his generation and say to them:
Repent. And if you do not, the Holy One, Blessed be He, will bring a flood
upon you and float your corpses on the water like wineskins."* And Rava
(108b): *"he said to them statements that are harsh as torches [kelapidim],
and they would treat him with contempt. They said to him: Old man, why are you
building this ark?"* Among the page's explanations of the seven days' delay
(7:4, 10) is a last chance: *"one hundred and twenty years, to repent, and
thereafter designated a brief period for them, an additional seven days, as a
final opportunity for them to repent."* The Mishnah draws the lesson — and
looks past Noah to Abraham:

> [There were] ten generations from Adam to Noah, in order to make known what
> long-suffering is His; for all those generations kept on provoking Him, until
> He brought upon them the waters of the flood. [There were] ten generations
> from Noah to Abraham, in order to make known what long-suffering is His; for
> all those generations kept on provoking Him, until Abraham, came and received
> the reward of all of them.
> — *Pirkei Avot* 5:2

**Josephus** (c. 94 CE) has Noah try and fail, then flee:

> But Noah was very uneasy at what they did: and being displeased at their
> conduct, persuaded them to change their dispositions, and their actions for
> the better. But seeing they did not yield to him, but were slaves to their
> wicked pleasures, he was afraid they would kill him, together with his wife
> and children, and those they had married. So he departed out of that land.
> — *Antiquities* 1.3.1

**The Sibylline Oracles** (Jewish, reworked by Christians; Book 1) give Noah a
commission and a sermon. God speaks:

> Noah, be of good cheer / In thyself and to all the people preach /
> Repentance, so that they may all be saved.
> — *Sibylline Oracles* 1.157–159 (Terry, 1899)

Noah preaches twice — *"Immortal Saviour overseeing all, / Who bade me warn
you, that ye perish not"* — and *"when they heard him each turned up his nose,
/ Calling him mad."* This Noah even asks his hearers to pray *"For life of
cities and of all the world, / And of four-footed beasts and flying fowls."*

**The early church.** Clement of Rome (c. 96 CE): *"Noah preached repentance,
and as many as listened to him were saved"* (*1 Clement* 7:6).
Theophilus of Antioch (c. 180) even explains the Greek name for the flood
hero by it: Noah, *"when he announced to the men then alive that there was a
flood coming, prophesied to them, saying, Come thither, God calls you to
repentance. On this account he was fitly called Deucalion"* (*To Autolycus*
3.19) — the translator's note: *"Deucalion, from Deute, come, and kaleo, I
call."*

**Philo** (c. 20 BCE–50 CE) makes the ark itself the warning: *"The kind
Saviour of the world allows a space for the repentance of sinners, in order
that when they see the ark placed in front of them as a sort of type, … they
might believe the predictions of the deluge which had been made to them"*
(*Questions on Genesis* 2.13).

**And one reader who went the other way.** In *1 Enoch*'s animal vision (c.
160 BCE), Noah is told in secret and builds his vessel without a word to
anyone: *"one of those four went to that white bull and instructed him in a
secret, without his being terrified: he was born a bull and became a man, and
built for himself a great vessel and dwelt thereon"* (89:1).

## 3. Readers who knew the nations' floods

**Josephus** was the first to collect them.

> Now all the writers of Barbarian Histories make mention of this flood, and
> of this Ark: among whom is Berosus the Chaldean.
> — *Antiquities* 1.3.6

He quotes Berossus — *"It is said there is still some part of this ship in
Armenia, at the mountain of the Cordyæans; and that some people carry off
pieces of the bitumen: which they take away, and use chiefly as amulets"* —
and adds *"Hieronymus the Egyptian also, who wrote the Phenician Antiquities;
and Mnaseas, and a great many more make mention of the same,"* and Nicolaus of
Damascus on a mountain in Armenia where *"one who was carried in an Ark, came
on shore upon the top of it… this might be the man about whom Moses, the
Legislator of the Jews, wrote."*

**Philo and Justin: Deucalion is Noah.** Philo: *"Now this man the Greeks call
Deucalion, but the Chaldæans name him Noah; and it was in his time that the
great deluge took place"* (*On Rewards and Punishments* 23; Philo's
"Chaldaeans" are the Hebrews). Justin Martyr (c. 155), to the Romans: *"the
flood left no one but him only with his family who is by us called Noah, and by
you Deucalion"* (*2 Apology* 7).

**Theophilus: the same flood, a different story.** He accepts the name but
rejects the Greek version: *"But others say that there existed Deucalion and
Pyrrha, and that they were preserved in a chest; and that Deucalion, after he
came out of the chest, flung stones behind him, and that men were produced
from the stones."* Moses, he says, told *"in what manner the flood came upon
the earth… and relating no fable of Pyrrha nor of Deucalion or Clymenus"*
(*To Autolycus* 3.18–19).

**Augustine: not the same flood at all.** The flood of Ogyges was *"not that
greatest one from which no man escaped except those who could get into the
ark, for neither Greek nor Latin history knew of it"*; and Deucalion's flood
came later and was local — *"this flood did not at all reach Egypt or its
vicinity"* (*City of God* 18.8, 18.10).

## 4. A covenant with all humankind (Genesis 9:1–7)

**"Fear and dread," but not "rule."** Genesis 9:1 repeats the blessing of 1:28
word for word — *be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth* — and then leaves
out *subdue* and *rule*, putting *fear and dread* in their place. The rabbis
noticed: *"fear and dread were restored, but dominion was not restored. When
was it restored? It was in the days of Solomon"* (*Genesis Rabbah* 34:12, citing
1 Kings 5:4). On the plain sense, 9:2 still ends *"under your authority"*
(literally "into your hand they are given"): rule gives way to fear, but is not
abolished. And on 9:6: *"Rabbi Akiva expounded: Anyone who
sheds blood, it is considered for him as though he has diminished God's
image"* (*Genesis Rabbah* 34:14). Rashi, on the doubled *be fruitful* (9:1,
9:7), separates the plain sense from the midrash himself: *"According to the
real sense, the first time this was said to man (Genesis 9:1) it was said as a
blessing (an assurance that they would be fruitful); here it is a command…
According to the Midrashic explanation this command is mentioned here after the
mention of murder in order to liken one who abstains from having children to
one who sheds blood."* In the Talmud that saying is Rabbi Eliezer's, and Ben Azzai pressed it hardest
— *"It is as though he sheds blood and also diminishes the Divine Image"* —
though he never married, and when challenged answered, *"What shall I do, as my
soul yearns for Torah"* (*Yevamot* 63b). The church has its own word here: Jesus and
Paul honour those who remain unmarried for the kingdom (Matthew 19:12; 1
Corinthians 7:7–8), and Isaiah promises those who could have no children *"a
monument that will be better than sons and daughters"* (Isaiah 56:5).

**Judged without a law?** If no law had been given, how could the flood
generation be guilty? Nachmanides answered on 6:13: *"the prohibition against
violence is a rational commandment, there being no need for a prophet to
admonish them against it. Besides, it is evil committed against both heaven and
mankind."*

**The seven laws of the children of Noah.** Judaism names the commandments
binding on every nation after Noah's children — all humanity:

> The sons of Noach were given seven commandments: courts, idolatry,
> [blasphemy,] forbidden sexual relations, bloodshed, theft, and [consuming]
> the limb of a living animal.
> — *Tosefta Avodah Zarah* 8:4 (Sefaria 9:4)

The Talmud gives the same seven (*Sanhedrin* 56a), and records other sages who
counted more (56b). It does not derive them mainly from Genesis 9. Rabbi
Yoḥanan reads them out of God's command to Adam in 2:16 — *"'The man,' this
alludes to bloodshed; and so it states: 'One who sheds the blood of man, by man
his blood shall be shed' (Genesis 9:6)"* (*Sanhedrin* 56b). Maimonides counts
six given to Adam, and the seventh, the limb from a living animal, *"added for
Noah, as Genesis 9:4 states"* (*Mishneh Torah*, Kings 9:1). Why the laws
matter to Jews is the other half: the Tosefta (*Sanhedrin* 13:2) records Rabbi
Yehoshua's teaching that there are righteous people among the nations who have
a share in the world to come, and Maimonides makes the seven laws the measure
of such a person — on the condition that they are kept as God's command (Kings
8:11). Josephus paraphrases 9:5–6
as a command to *"abstain from shedding the blood of men; and to keep your
selves pure from murder; and to punish those that commit any such thing"*
(*Antiquities* 1.3.8). Many readers have set beside these the four things the
apostles asked of Gentile believers in Acts 15 — *"to abstain from things
defiled by idols and from sexual immorality and from what has been strangled
and from blood"* (15:20) — though whether James had Noah in mind is debated.

***Jubilees*** (c. 160 BCE) makes it an oath and a feast. *"And Noah and his
sons swore that they would not eat any blood that was in any flesh"* (6:10);
the bow is *"a sign of the eternal covenant that there should not again be a
flood on the earth"* (6:16); and *"it is ordained and written on the heavenly
tables, that they should celebrate the feast of weeks in this month once a
year, to renew the covenant every year"* (6:17) — Shavuot, the feast later
tied to Sinai. Late in life Noah teaches his grandsons *"to observe
righteousness, and to cover the shame of their flesh, and to bless their
Creator, and honour father and mother, and love their neighbour"* (7:20).

## 5. The bow

**God's weapon, hung up.** Josephus (c. 94 CE): *"But I will give you a sign that I have
left off my anger by my bow; whereby is meant the rain-bow: for they determined
that the rain-bow was the bow of God"* (*Antiquities* 1.3.8). Philo argues that
the sign really is a *bow*: *"a bow is not itself a weapon, but only an
instrument for the use of weapons, namely, for the arrow which strikes"*
(*Questions on Genesis* 2.64). Nachmanides (1194–1270) reports the reading
of earlier commentators: God *"has not made the rainbow with its feet bent
upward because it might have appeared that arrows were being shot from heaven…
in order to show that they are not shooting at the earth from the heavens. It
is indeed the way of warriors to invert the instruments of war which they hold
in their hands when calling for peace from their opponents. Moreover… the bow
has no rope upon which to bend the arrows."* And he answers the question every
class asks — were there no rainbows before the flood? — by agreeing with the
Greek philosophers that the rainbow is natural: *"The rainbow which I have set
in the clouds from the day of creation will be from this day on a sign of
covenant between Me and you."*

**God's likeness?** The rabbis tried a pun and drew back from it: *"'My
rainbow [kashti] I have set' – My likeness [kishuti]; something that is likened
to Me. Is that possible? It is, rather, like the straw [kashin] of the grain"*
(*Genesis Rabbah* 35:3). Ezekiel did see a rainbow around the throne — *"like
the appearance of a rainbow in the clouds after the rain… it looked like the
glory of the LORD"* (1:28; so Revelation 4:3) — and the Talmud records that one who sees a rainbow should fall on his face, as
Ezekiel did (though in the Land of Israel they objected, lest it look like
bowing to the rainbow), and that all agree he says a blessing. Rav Pappa combined the two forms
given:
*"Blessed…Who remembers the covenant and is faithful to His covenant and
fulfills His word"* (*Berakhot* 59a). They added a striking corollary: a generation
righteous enough would need no rainbow at all. Rabbi Shimon bar Yoḥai, told
that a rainbow had been seen in a certain sage's lifetime, replied: *"If a
rainbow has been sighted in his days, he is not worthy to see my countenance"*
(*Genesis Rabbah* 35:2).

**The scholars.** The two readings are still both held. S. R. Driver (1904):
*"It is not impossible that the representation found here rests ultimately
upon a mythological basis; and that the rainbow was regarded originally by the
Hebrews as Jehovah's war-bow"*, *"laid aside as the sign of pacified anger"*;
*"but perhaps (Riehm, Dillm.) the rainbow is viewed merely as the emblem of
returning favour, and the name is based simply on the similarity of form."*
John Skinner (1910) compared the Babylonian creation epic, *"where Marduk's
bow, which he had used against Tiamat, is set in the heavens as a
constellation"* — in that epic it is the god Anu who raises the bow in the
gods' assembly and names it the Bow-star.

## 6. The ark and the church

**A boat God steered.** The Wisdom of Solomon (first century BCE) calls Noah
*"The hope of the world, taking refuge on a raft… Thy hand guiding the
helm,"* and the editor of the 1913 translation adds: *"Noah was not acquainted
with navigation."* Augustine: an ark *"not made with curved ribs but straight
timbers,"* *"which was not to be launched by its builders, but to be lifted by
the natural pressure of the water when it reached it, and which was to be
preserved from shipwreck as it floated about rather by divine oversight than
by human skill"* (*City of God* 15.27).

**A body and a church.** Philo noticed that the ark's proportions are a
human body's: *"For three hundred is six times as many as fifty, and ten
times as many as thirty; … Such then are also the proportions of the body"*
(*Questions on Genesis* 2.5). Augustine took it to Christ: *"For even its very
dimensions, in length, breadth, and height, represent the human body in which
He came, as it had been foretold. … And its having a door made in the side of
it certainly signified the wound which was made when the side of the
Crucified was pierced with the spear"* (*City of God* 15.26). Cyprian (c. 251):
*"If any one could escape who was outside the ark of Noah, then he also may
escape who shall be outside of the Church"* (*On the Unity of the Church* 6).
Tertullian heard the dove at Jesus' baptism: *"a dove was the herald which
announced to the earth the assuagement of celestial wrath, when she had been
sent her way out of the ark, and had returned with the olive-branch, a sign
which even among the nations is the fore-token of peace"* (*On Baptism* 8).
Justin Martyr counted the eight people as *"a symbol of the eighth day,
wherein Christ appeared when He rose from the dead"* (*Dialogue* 138).

**History and meaning both.** Augustine opens *City of God* 15.27 by saying
how he held them together: *"Yet no one ought to suppose either that these things
were written for no purpose, or that we should study only the historical
truth, apart from any allegorical meanings; or, on the contrary, that they
are only allegories, and that there were no such facts at all"* (*City of God*
15.27).

## 7. "Righteous in his generations" — how righteous?

Genesis 6:9 says Noah was *blameless among his contemporaries* — literally
*"in his generations."* The rabbis argued over the phrase (*Sanhedrin* 108a):

> Rabbi Yoḥanan says: Relative to the other people of his generation he was
> righteous and wholehearted, but not relative to those of other generations.
> And Reish Lakish says: In his generation he was righteous and wholehearted
> despite being surrounded by bad influences; all the more so would he have
> been considered righteous and wholehearted in other generations.

*Genesis Rabbah* 30:9 has the same debate between two other sages, Rabbi
Yehuda and Rabbi Neḥemya, with parables — against: *"In a marketplace
of completely blind people, they call those who are blind in one eye, 'the one
of abundant light'"*; for: *"a bowl of balsam oil that was sealed with a
tight-fitting lid. It was situated in a graveyard and its fragrance wafted
through the air. Had it been outside the graveyard, how much more so!"* Philo
took the first side: *"Showing that he was not perfect absolutely, but that he
was good in comparison with the others who lived at that time."* The next
paragraph of the midrash (30:10) compares him with Abraham: Noah *"walked with
God"* (6:9), Abraham walked *before* him (17:1). Rashi sums it up: *"Noah needed
God's support to uphold him in righteousness, Abraham drew his moral strength
from himself."*

**The other half of the silence.** The tradition heard Noah's silence two
ways. It made him a preacher (§2) — and it also faulted him twice: for wavering,
and for not interceding. Rashi, following Rabbi Yoḥanan: *"Noah, also, was of those people who are
wanting in faith: he believed and he did not believe that the Flood would come,
and he would not enter the Ark until the waters forced him to do so"* (on 7:7).
The Zohar (late 13th century) sets him against Moses: *"when God bade Noah save himself and his
household in the ark from the universal destruction at the time of the Flood,
he did not intercede on behalf of his generation, but let them perish. It is
for this reason that the waters of the Flood are named after him, as it is
written, 'for this is as the waters of Noah unto me' (Is. 54, 9)."* But the same sages
also defend him. Rabbi Judah: *"What could he do? He was in
fear for his own life, lest he should perish along with the wicked, whose
iniquities and provocations he had observed for so long."* And Rabbi Isaac — who had just
faulted him — says God saved him because *"he daily warned the people, who, however, disregarded
his warning."* A herald to people and an intercessor before God are different
roles; the tradition found
Noah the first and missed the second. Hebrews, for the church, names what his
righteousness rested on: *"the righteousness that comes by faith"* (11:7) —
*"By faith Noah."*

## 8. After the flood: the vineyard and the curse (9:18–29)

**A man of the soil.** *"Noah, a man of the soil, began to plant a vineyard"*
(9:20). The rabbis heard two things in it. *Genesis Rabbah* 36:3 plays on
*vayaḥel*, "began," as *ḥullin*, "profane": *"he became profaned and unholy
[ḥulin]. Why? 'And he planted a vineyard.' Should he not have planted
something else, that was constructive, a fig tree branch or an olive tree
branch?"* But the same passage also hears the promise of Noah's name (5:29)
kept: *"he remade the surface of the soil; because of him the soil was
watered; he filled the entire surface of the soil."*

**"Cursed be Canaan" — not Ham.** The text curses Canaan, Ham's son, and
never Ham himself. The earliest readers saw the puzzle:

> Ḥam committed the sin and Canaan was cursed? This is astonishing. Rabbi
> Yehuda and Rabbi Neḥemya, Rabbi Yehuda said: Because it is written: "God
> blessed Noah and his sons" (Genesis 9:1), and a curse cannot be in a place
> where there is blessing. That is why he said: "Cursed is Canaan." Rabbi
> Neḥemya says: Canaan saw and told Ḥam; therefore, one ascribes the curse to
> the corrupt one.
> — *Genesis Rabbah* 36:7

Justin Martyr, at about the same time, gave the same answer: *"For the Spirit of
prophecy would not curse the son that had been by God blessed along with [his
brothers]"* (*Dialogue* 139). Rashi gives two more Jewish readings: Ham had
mutilated or abused his father (on 9:22, after *Sanhedrin* 70a), and so lost
Noah a fourth son — *"cursed, therefore, be your fourth son"* (on 9:25). And
notice who speaks: it is Noah, waking from wine, who curses. God curses no one
in this chapter.

**The tradition's own troubling strand.** The same midrash, a few lines later,
keeps folk-tales that explain dark skin by this story. Rav Huna's comes as a
comment on "Cursed be Canaan" itself — Noah says that Canaan *"will be ugly and
darkened"* and Rabbi Ḥiyya bar Abba says that
*"Ḥam emerged darkened"* from the ark (*Genesis Rabbah* 36:7). The Talmud lists
Ham among three who were punished for breaking the ark's rule of separation:
*"Ham was afflicted in that his skin turned black"* (*Sanhedrin* 108b, in the
Davidson edition's English; the Talmud's own words say only that he was
afflicted in his skin, and "turned black" is the translator's gloss). These are
stories about skin colour, not arguments for slavery. What came later —
traced in David Goldenberg's study (below) — was moving the curse from Canaan
to Ham and to Cush, and using it to justify enslaving Africans.

**How the verse was misused.** Nothing in Genesis 9 mentions skin, Africa, or
any people but Canaan. Centuries later a "curse of Ham" was used to justify
the enslavement of Black Africans — first in the Near East from the seventh
century, then in Europe and, most destructively, in the Americas, where it was
preached in defence of slavery and segregation (D. M. Goldenberg, *The Curse
of Ham*, Princeton, 2003; S. R. Haynes, *Noah's Curse*, Oxford, 2002). Many
churches that once taught it have renounced it; the Southern Baptist
Convention did so formally in 2018, as a misreading of Genesis 9:25–27.

An early and explicit Jewish rejection of the racial reading is that of the
Spanish-born Abraham ibn Ezra, in the twelfth century, on this very verse:

> There are some who say that the Cushites are enslaved because Noah cursed
> Ham. However, they have forgotten that the first king to rule after the flood
> was a Cushite. Thus it is written, and Cush begot Nimrod…And the beginning of
> his kingdom was Babel, and Erech, and Accad, and Calneh, in the land of
> Shinar (Gen. 10:8-10).
> — Ibn Ezra on Genesis 9:25

And the chapter answers it itself: every human bears God's image (9:6), and
*"from them the whole earth was populated"* (9:19) — *"From one man he made
every nation of the human race to inhabit the entire earth"* (Acts 17:26). Augustine, reading this same chapter, noticed
that the word *slave* appears here for the first time in Scripture, and drew
the opposite lesson: God made his rational creature to rule *"not man over
man, but man over the beasts"* — *"It is a name, therefore, introduced by sin
and not by nature"* (*City of God* 19.15). (He did not, for all that, call for
slavery's end.)

## 9. Noah's wife

Genesis never names Noah's wife or his sons' wives, and none of them speaks.
(In *Gilgamesh*, the survivor's wife is raised to godhood beside him.) Later
readers filled the gap. *Jubilees* names her: *"Noah took to himself a wife,
and her name was ’Ĕmzârâ, the daughter of Râkê’êl, the daughter of his father's
brother"* (4:33 in Charles's numbering). One rabbi made her Naamah, the sister
of Tubal-Cain (*Genesis Rabbah* 23:3; see Session 7's sheet) — which would mean that
everyone after the flood descends from both of Adam's lines. Rashi noticed that the
family goes into the ark with the men and the women listed apart (7:7), and is
told to come out as couples — *"you, your wife, your sons, and your sons' wives
with you"* (8:16): *"The men separately and the women separately, because they
were forbidden to live together as man and wife since the world was living in a
state of distress."* And the medieval English
mystery plays (Chester, Wakefield) turned her into a comic shrew who has to be
dragged aboard — a reminder of how much a later age can write into a silence.

## 10. The flood at the font

Most churchgoers have heard the flood not in a Bible study but at a baptism.
First Peter's *"this prefigured baptism"* (3:21) became part of the church's
worship. Martin Luther's baptismal order of 1523 includes his "Flood Prayer," which
recalls how God saved Noah's family through the flood and Israel through the
sea, and asks that in baptism all that is sinful be drowned and the one
baptized be kept safe in the ark of the church. The Easter Vigil in many
churches (the Book of Common Prayer and Lutheran orders among them) reads the
flood among its readings of salvation history. And in the Revised Common Lectionary, used by many
churches, the first Sunday in Lent in Year B pairs God's covenant with Noah
(Genesis 9:8–17) with 1 Peter 3:18–22 and Jesus' baptism and temptation (Mark
1:9–15).

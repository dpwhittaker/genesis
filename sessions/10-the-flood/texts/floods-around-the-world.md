# Floods around the world

A source sheet for **Session 10**. The Mesopotamian floods — *Atrahasis*,
*Gilgamesh*, the Sumerian flood and Berossus — have a sheet of their own,
**[the neighbours' floods](the-neighbors-floods.html)**. This one goes
further afield: Africa, Asia, Europe, the Americas, Australia and the
Pacific, with the oldest record of each story we could find, who wrote it
down, and what it actually says.

**Why this sheet exists.** You will often hear that *every culture has a
flood story*. Sometimes that is offered as proof that Genesis is true — the
nations remember Noah. Sometimes it is offered as proof that Genesis is just
one more myth. Both arguments rest on the bare outline: water, a few
survivors, a new start. This sheet looks past the outline to the **details**,
because that is where stories say what they mean.

**Three things to keep in mind as you read.**

- **Who wrote it down, and when.** Most of these stories were told aloud for
  generations and written down by outsiders — conquistadors, friars,
  missionaries, colonial officers — and some of those wrote to justify a
  conquest. Some stories were recorded *after* the hearers had been told about
  Noah. Each entry below says who recorded it and when, and flags what scholars
  think came in from the Bible — whether the tellers absorbed it, or the
  recorder framed it.
- **These are living traditions.** Nearly every people named here still
  exists and still tells its own stories, often differently from the old
  records. We use only stories long published in public-domain books, we name
  the people each belongs to, and we note where it is told differently today.
- **These are not only exhibits.** It is easy to read other peoples' stories
  only as evidence for or against Genesis. The New Testament reads the nations
  more generously: God *"did not leave himself without a witness by doing good,
  by giving you rain from heaven and fruitful seasons"* (Acts 14:17). Each of
  these stories says something about what its people hoped and feared; the
  chart in §4 tries to show that too.
- **A flood is not always a flood story.** Some famous "flood stories" turn
  out to be about something else — a drained river system, a broken pot, a
  forged manuscript. They are here too, because they are part of the honest
  picture.

---

## 1. The claim, stated accurately

Flood stories are found on **every inhabited continent** — but **not in every
culture**. They are thick in South and Southeast Asia, the Pacific and the
Americas. They are **rare in Europe**, **absent from ancient Egypt**,
**absent from the classical literatures of China and Japan** in the
Noah sense, and **nearly absent from Africa**. Sir James Frazer, who
collected hundreds of them a century ago, put it bluntly:

> In Africa, including Egypt, native legends of a great flood are
> conspicuously absent; indeed no single clear case of one has yet been
> reported.
> — J. G. Frazer, *Folk-Lore in the Old Testament* I (1918), p. 333

> …it is remarkable that very few popular traditions of a universal or
> widespread deluge have been recorded in Europe.
> — Frazer, p. 174

Frazer was describing what Europeans had reported by 1918, and his framework —
"savage" peoples climbing toward "civilization" — is not ours. Later collectors
found more African stories than he knew, but few with Noah's shape. Antarctica
has no story at all: no people ever lived there.

And where flood stories *are* found, they mostly share the **bare idea** of
destroying waters — not Genesis's particular details. Section 4 shows which
details travel and which do not.

## 2. The stories

### Africa

#### Egypt: the *Destruction of Mankind* — a "flood" of beer

The oldest copy is on a gilded shrine from **Tutankhamun's tomb** (c. 1325
BCE); full copies are in the tombs of Seti I and Ramesses II and III. Egypt
has **no flood story** — the Nile's flood was its blessing, rising from below
— but this is its story of a god destroying humankind. The aged sun-god Ra
hears that people are plotting against him. He consults the other gods — *"I
will not slay them until I have heard what ye shall say to me concerning
it"* — and sends his Eye, the goddess Hathor, who becomes the lioness
Sekhmet and begins to slaughter. Then Ra relents: *"It is good, it is good,
nevertheless I must protect men and women against her."* He has 7,000 jars
of beer brewed, coloured red to look like blood, and poured over the fields
by night:

> …she drank [of the beer and blood], and her heart rejoiced, and she became
> drunk, and she gave no further attention to men and women.

No one is chosen; the rest of humanity is spared because the god changed his
mind. Afterwards Ra, *"my heart hath become exceedingly weary with existence
with them,"* withdraws to the sky. A festival with beer remembers it.

*Why it matters here:* a god who **regrets** — but *after* he has begun, and
wearily. Two other Egyptian texts are worth knowing. In Plato's *Timaeus* an
Egyptian priest tells the Greek Solon that *"There have been, and will be
again, many destructions of mankind arising out of many causes"* — but not in
Egypt, where *"neither then nor at any other time, does the water come down
from above on the fields."* And
in the Book of the Dead (spell 175) the gods complain of humankind in words
close to Genesis 6:11 — *"They have waged war, they have upheld strife, they
have done evil"* — and the creator threatens that one day the world will
return to the primeval waters. Egypt expects the water to come **again**.

*Bible contact:* none — the text is older than any copy of Genesis.

#### Maasai (Kenya and Tanzania): Tumbainot — the case of contact

Recorded by **Moritz Merker**, a German officer, in *Die Masai* (1904). God
resolves to destroy humankind after the first murder; *"Tumbainot was a
righteous man whom God loved"*; he builds an ark, takes animals, sends out a
dove and then a vulture; and

> As he stepped out of the ark, Tumbainot saw no less than four rainbows, one
> in each of the four quarters of the sky, and he took them as a sign that the
> wrath of God was over.
> — Frazer's English, after Merker

*Bible contact: high.* Frazer judged the East African stories *"plainly mere
variations of the Biblical narrative."* Merker set his story beside Genesis
himself (*"Die biblische Darstellung in Gen. 6 bis 9 setze ich als bekannt
voraus"* — "I take the biblical account in Genesis 6–9 as known"), argued
that the Maasai were a "Semitic people," and was criticised by his own
contemporaries for unrepresentative sources (K. Holter, *Scriptura* 116,
2017). A similar story from the Mkulwe mission station on Lake Rukwa (1909)
has a dove and a hawk. The missionary Robert Moffat had already warned in
1842 that stories *"originally obtained at a missionary station, or from some
godly traveller, get, in course of time, so mixed up and metamorphosed by
heathen ideas, that they look exceedingly like native traditions."*

#### Lunda (Angola–Congo border): Lake Dilolo

Told to **David Livingstone** in 1854. A woman chief asks for food; one
household gives it, the next village mocks her. She sings her own name —

> As she prolonged the last note, the village, people, fowls, and dogs sank
> into the space now called Dilolo.
> — Livingstone, *Missionary Travels* (1857)

A drowned village and the origin of a lake, punished for **inhospitality**.
Livingstone wondered whether it was *"a faint tradition of the Deluge,"* and
called it *"the only one I have met with in this country."* *Bible contact:*
none apparent.

#### Yoruba (Nigeria and Benin): Olokun

Recorded by **A. B. Ellis** (1894). The sea-god Olokun, offended,

> …in a fit of rage he destroyed nearly all the inhabitants of the world in a
> great flood, only a few being saved by Obatala, who drew them up into the
> sky by means of a long iron chain.

In a second version Obatala *"bound him with seven iron chains till he
promised to abandon his design"* — a promise forced out of the destroyer.
Ellis himself thought the first version *"probably derived from the
Mohammedans."*

#### Africa, in sum

There are genuinely African stories of destroying water — a drowned village,
a lake's origin, a broken pot (Kwaya, Lake Victoria), the sun and moon
colliding (Lower Congo: *"there was a flood, and the ancient people… turned
into monkeys"*). They are not Noah stories. The African stories with an ark, a
dove and a rainbow were written down around 1900 in a colonial and missionary
setting. Africa is the strongest single exception to "every culture has a
flood story."

### Asia

#### India: Manu and the fish

The oldest version is in the ***Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa*** (1.8.1), Vedic prose
usually dated to the eighth–sixth centuries BCE. Manu, washing, finds a small
fish that says: *"Rear me, I will save thee!… A flood will carry away all
these creatures: from that I will save thee!"* He raises it; it warns him; he
builds a ship;

> The fish then swam up to him, and to its horn he tied the rope of the ship,
> and by that means he passed swiftly up to yonder northern mountain.
> — Eggeling, *SBE* 12 (1882)

*"The flood then swept away all these creatures, and Manu alone remained
here."* He comes down the slope still called *"Manu's descent,"* and offers
sacrifice: *"he offered up in the waters clarified butter, sour milk, whey,
and curds. Thence a woman was produced in a year."* She is Iḍā, the offering
itself, and she tells him, *"If thou wilt make use of me at the sacrifice,
thou wilt become rich in offspring and cattle."* *"Through her he generated
this race, which is this race of Manu."*

No reason is given for the flood, and Manu is saved because he saved the fish.
In later versions (the *Mahābhārata*, the Purāṇas) the fish is the god Vishnu,
the ship carries *"the seven sages… [and] all the seeds,"* and the flood
becomes the regular dissolution of the world at the end of an age.

*Why it matters here:* a warning, a ship, a mountain, a sacrifice, and a
**blessing of offspring** that comes out of the offering — the closest
early parallel anywhere to Genesis 8:20–9:1. *Bible contact:* none; some
nineteenth-century scholars thought it came from Babylon, Frazer and Max
Müller did not, and the question is still open.

#### China: Gun and Yu — the flood that was drained

China's great flood, in the ***Book of Documents*** (*Shujing*) and in
*Mencius* (fourth century BCE), is not survived but **managed**. The emperor
Yao reports:

> In their vast extent they embrace the hills and overtop the great heights,
> threatening the heavens with their floods, so that the lower people groan
> and murmur!
> — "Canon of Yao," Legge, *SBE* 3 (1879)

Gun tries to dam it and fails: *"For nine years he laboured, but the work was
unaccomplished."* His son Yu digs channels to the sea instead — *"He dug open
the ground [which impeded their flow], and led them to the sea"* — and works
so hard that he is *"eight years away from his house, thrice passing by his
door without entering it."* No one is chosen, nothing floats, and no one is
punished but the failed engineer. The missionary scholar James Legge noticed
in 1865: *"Not a word is said as to the flood's being a punishment of the sins
either of ruler or people."* After the flood, Heaven gives Yu a law for
government, *"the Great Plan with its nine divisions."*

#### Miao (southern China): the brother and sister in the gourd

Southern China's minority peoples tell a very different flood. In a Miao song
recorded by the missionary **Samuel Clarke** (1911), *"When Thunder threatened
to destroy the earth with a deluge, A-Zie hollowed out a large gourd for
himself, and collected a hundred kinds and a thousand sorts of seeds."* In
another version an old woman from heaven saves the younger of two brothers
*"because he had been kind to her."* The survivors are a brother and sister,
who marry after a series of tests; their child is cut in pieces, and *"Next
morning they discovered that these pieces had all changed into men and women,
and thus the earth was re-peopled."* *Bible contact:* none in the story, but
the recorders read Noah into it (Clarke on a neighbouring people: *"They
manifestly trace their genealogy from Noah"*).

### Europe

#### Greece: Deucalion and Pyrrha

The earliest clear mention is **Pindar** (468 BCE): *"Truly men say that once
a mighty water swept over the dark earth, but by the craft of Zeus an ebb
suddenly drew off the flood."* The full story is in Apollodorus (first or
second century CE) and **Ovid** (8 CE). Zeus decides to destroy the race of
bronze; Ovid's Jupiter, after Lycaon serves him human flesh, condemns a race
grown savage: *"But that which is incurable must be cut away with the knife,
lest the untainted part also draw infection."* Deucalion, warned by his father
Prometheus, builds a **chest**:

> But Deucalion, floating in the chest over the sea for nine days and as many
> nights, drifted to Parnassus, and there, when the rain ceased, he landed and
> sacrificed to Zeus, the god of Escape.
> — Apollodorus 1.7.2, Frazer (1921)

Ovid gives the reason he and his wife Pyrrha were spared: *"There was no
better man than he, none more scrupulous of right, nor than she was any woman
more reverent of the gods."* No animals are taken — the earth breeds them
afresh from the mud — and humanity is restored by **stones** thrown over the
shoulder, which become people. And the gods' worry about losing humankind is
the worry of *Atrahasis*: *"Who would bring incense to their altars?"*

*Why it matters here:* the clearest early parallel for **a righteous man
chosen**, and a moral cause. The Greek poet Hesiod had described the bronze
race in words close to Genesis 6 — *"They loved the lamentable works of Ares
and deeds of violence… hard of heart like adamant"* — but in Hesiod *"These
were destroyed by their own hands,"* not by a flood.

#### Greece, later: animals in pairs and a dove

Two late Greek versions look like the Near East. **Lucian** (c. 150 CE)
describes Deucalion's flood at Hierapolis in Syria: *"there came to him wild
boars and horses, and generations of lions and serpents, and all the other
beasts which roam the earth, all in couples."* **Plutarch** (c. 100 CE):
*"the dove which Deucalion sent out of his ark, returning back again, was to
him a certain sign of the storm not ceased."* Frazer: *"In this form the Greek
legend of the great flood was unquestionably coloured, if not moulded, by
Semitic influence."* Coins of Apamea in Phrygia (third century CE) show an
ark inscribed **NOE**.

#### Norse: the flood of a giant's blood

In Snorri Sturluson's *Prose Edda* (c. 1220), the gods kill the first giant,
Ymir, and *"there gushed forth so much blood out of his wounds that with it
they drowned all the race of the Rime-Giants, save that one, whom giants call
Bergelmir, escaped with his household; he went upon his ship."* This is
creation, not judgment, and the survivor is a giant. Even the ship may be
Snorri's: the older poem's word, says the translator's footnote, is
*"Literally, mill-bench or mortar."*

#### Wales: Dwyfan and Dwyfach — a forgery

A Welsh "Noah" pair, saved *"in a naked vessel"* with *"a male and a female of
every animal species,"* appears in triads printed in 1801. They were
composed by **Iolo Morganwg** (Edward Williams, 1747–1826), a gifted forger of
Welsh antiquities. In 1809 a clergyman cited them as *"evident traditions of
the deluge."* Sir John Rhŷs saw through them in 1901: *"the writer of Triad
iii. 13 has developed his universal deluge on the basis of the scriptural
account of it."* A good warning about how "flood stories" can multiply.

### North America

#### Innu and Anishinaabe (Ojibwe): Messou, Nanabozho and the earth-divers

Two peoples' stories, told across the Algonquian-speaking peoples of the
Canadian Shield and the Great Lakes, with heroes scholars link to one another.
The first record is **Innu** (Montagnais), taken down by the Jesuit **Paul Le
Jeune** in 1633: the hero **Messou**'s lynxes are held by monsters in a lake,
and when he goes in after them the lake overflows and floods the world. The
**Anishinaabe** (Ojibwe) story of **Nanabozho**, first written down in 1839
and dictated in Ojibwe in 1903–05, comes out of a feud: the water-spirits kill
the hero's nephew, he takes revenge, and they raise the water against him. In
neither is anyone judged. Nanabozho survives on a raft or a tree-top — *"He addressed the tree:
'Grandfather, stretch yourself.' The tree did so. But the waters still rose."*
(Schoolcraft, 1839) — and then the animals dive for earth:

> He sent a raven to find a small piece of the earth, with which to build up
> another world. The raven was unable to find any, everything being covered
> with water. He made an otter dive down, but the depth of the water prevented
> it from going to the bottom. At last a muskrat descended, and brought back
> some earth.
> — Le Jeune, *Relation* of 1633, of Messou (Thwaites, 1897)

*"When Nanabushu blew his breath upon it, behold! a small island floated on
the water"* (Jones, *Ojibwa Texts*, dictated 1903–05). The world is not
dried out but **remade from mud**. In Jones's text the hero even wonders,
*"Perhaps it is true that I have done a wrong (which may never be
repaired)."*

*Bible contact:* Le Jeune at once read it as a garbled Noah story; scholars
today treat the earth-diver as one of the oldest native creation motifs in
North America and northern Asia. Pairs of animals and a corpse-eating raven
appear only in versions recorded in the twentieth century. *How it is told
today:* Nanabozho stories are told in winter; a widely used Ojibwe telling,
Edward Benton-Banai's *The Mishomis Book* (1979), frames the flood as the
Creator's purification of a world fallen into disharmony, and ends with the
new earth on Turtle's back — "Turtle Island."

#### Cherokee: the dog's warning

Told to Henry Schoolcraft by the Cherokee leader **Stand Watie** in 1846, and
to James Mooney in 1890 by a man who had heard it from his grandmother
eighty years before. A man's dog goes to the river every day and howls; when
scolded, it speaks:

> Very soon there is going to be a great freshet and the water will come so
> high that everybody will be drowned; but if you will make a raft to get upon
> when the rain comes you can be saved, but you must first throw me into the
> water.
> — Mooney, *Myths of the Cherokee* (1900)

No god, no reason, no chosen man — only a dog. When the family come down they
find *"great piles of bones of the people who had been drowned, and then he
knew that the ghosts had been dancing."* Mooney set aside the missionaries'
versions as *"simply the Bible narrative as told by the Indians,"* and kept
this one as native.

### Mesoamerica

#### K'iche' Maya: the wooden people (*Popol Vuh*)

Written in K'iche' by Maya nobles in the 1550s, copied with a Spanish
translation by the friar Francisco Ximénez around 1701; Maya inscriptions and
a page of the Dresden Codex show a flood myth before the conquest. The gods
make people of wood. They multiply, but *"they had neither heart nor
intelligence, they forgot their Creator"* (Spence, 1908):

> Because they had not thought on Hurakan, therefore the face of the earth
> grew dark, and a pouring rain commenced, raining by day and by night.
> — L. Spence, *The Popol Vuh* (1908)

Ximénez's Spanish: *"y esto fué en castigo y escarmiento porque no hicieron
gracias delante de su madre y padre y Señor: el corazón del cielo que se llama
Huracán"* — "and this was as punishment and warning, because they did not give
thanks before their mother and father and Lord, the Heart of Heaven, who is
called Huracán" (our working translation). Their own dogs, pots and grinding
stones rise against them for the way they were treated. **No one is saved**:
*"their posterity are the little monkeys who live in the woods."* Later the
gods make true humans from maize.

*Why it matters here:* the clearest early, native **moral cause** outside the
Near East — forgetting and failing to thank the Maker, and cruelty to the
animals and tools that served them. And
the wooden people have *no heart*, where Genesis faults the heart's
inclination (6:5). *How it is told today:* the *Popol Vuh* is Guatemala's
national book, and K'iche' scholars have made their own translations.

#### Nahua (Aztec): the Fourth Sun and Tata and Nene

The *Legend of the Suns* (1558), written in Nahuatl a generation after the
conquest, and the carved Sun Stone before it, describe four previous ages,
each ended by its own catastrophe. The fourth, "4 Water," ends in a flood
that turns people into fish. **No reason is given** — it is the end of an age.
The god Tezcatlipoca warns one couple, Tata and Nene: *"hollow out to yourselves
a great cypress, into which you shall enter when, in the month Tozoztli, the
waters shall near the sky"*; *"Thou shalt eat but a single ear of maize, and
thy wife but one also."* The god himself seals them in, as the LORD shut Noah
in (7:16). But when the water goes down they make a fire to roast fish, and
the smoke rises to heaven:

> O divine Lord! what is this fire that they make there? wherefore do they so
> fill the heaven with smoke?
> — Bancroft, *Native Races* III (1875), after Brasseur

The god comes down in anger and turns them into dogs. **The smoke that pleases
God in Genesis 8:21 offends the gods here.** Humankind is later remade from
the bones of the dead and the blood of a god, and the present age, too, will
end. (An old mistranslation — *"Make now no more pulque"*, *"Brew no more
wine"* — still circulates; modern editors read the Nahuatl as roughly "do not
want anything more.")

### South America

#### Inca: Viracocha's flood

In **Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa**'s history (1572), written in Cuzco for the
Spanish viceroy, the Creator Viracocha's first people sin:

> But as there arose among them the vices of pride and covetousness, they
> transgressed the precept of Viracocha Pachayachachi and falling, through
> this sin, under his indignation, he confounded and cursed them.

*"…over all there came a general flood which they call uñu pachacuti, which
means 'water that overturns the land.'"* In Cristóbal de Molina's version (c.
1575) no reason is given, and *"No living things survived except a man and a
woman who remained in a box."* Afterwards Viracocha models each nation in
clay and sends it up out of its own cave or spring. *Bible contact: strong.*
Sarmiento wrote to discredit the Inca and framed their religion as the devil's
counterfeit; "precept" and "sin" may be his. His translator Clements Markham
suspected that some Andean flood stories had been *"obtained by asking
leading questions about a deluge."*

#### Cañari (Ecuador): the mountain that grew

Also in Molina (c. 1575): *"two brothers escaped to a very high mountain
called Huaca-ynan. As the waters rose the hill also increased in height, so
that the waters never reached them."* Afterwards two macaws bring them food —
*"They came dressed as Canaris"* — and one becomes a brother's wife: *"from
these brothers and sisters, children of the guacamayo, all the Cañaris
proceed."* No cause, no god, no sacrifice. The Cañari still honour the hill.
In Sarmiento's version (1572) the brothers also *"became sad and prayed to
Viracocha for pardon for their sins"* — a line Molina's version lacks, and
probably the chronicler's addition.

#### Huarochirí (Peru): the llama's warning

From a Quechua manuscript of about 1608. A herder's llama refuses to eat and,
when he throws a corncob at it, speaks: *"Learn that I am not sad without good
cause; for within five days the sea will rise and cover the whole earth,
destroying all there is upon it."* He climbs a mountain, where the animals
have already gathered by themselves; the fox's tail dips in the water, *"which
they say is the reason that the tips of foxes' tails are black."* The priest
who recorded it called it the devil's lie; the Quechua writer noted the
Christian comparison himself and kept his own people's story.

#### Tupinambá (coastal Brazil): the brothers' quarrel

Among the earliest records of any American flood belief. The German gunner
**Hans Staden**, a captive in 1554–55: *"they say, once there had been a great
water, which had drowned their forefathers, some of whom had escaped in a
canoe, and others on high trees."* The friar André Thevet (1575) gives the
story: a warrior brother insults his peaceable, farming brother, who stamps on
the ground until a fountain covers the earth. Both brothers survive in trees,
and test the water not with birds but by dropping fruit — *"they perceived by
the splash that the water was still high."* *Bible contact:* none in the
details; Thevet himself allowed it might be *"autre déluge que celuy du temps
de Noë"* — "a flood other than the one in Noah's time."

#### Mapuche (Chile): the floating mountain

Recorded by Jesuits in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: a few people
survived on a three-peaked mountain, *"which had three points, and possessed
the property of moving upon the water."* After earthquakes the Mapuche would
climb the hills with food, **expecting it to happen again**. The Jesuit Juan
Ignacio Molina (1787) judged it *"probably very different from that of
Noah."* Today it is usually told as the battle of two serpents, one raising
the sea and one raising the land.

### Australia

#### Gunaikurnai (Gippsland, Victoria): the frog and the pelican's canoe

Recorded by **R. Brough Smyth** (1878) from the people of Lake Tyers, and by
**A. W. Howitt** (1904). A giant frog has swallowed all the water in the
world. The animals try to make him laugh; at last the eel dances,

> He laughed outright. When he laughed, all the waters came out of his mouth,
> and there was a flood (Koorpa). Great numbers were drowned in the flood.

The Pelican, who had been a man, *"cut a very large canoe (Gre), and sailed
among the islands which appeared here and there in the great waters, and he
took the people into his canoe, and he kept them alive."* It is not a
judgment at all — the cure for a drought went too far. *How it is retold:*
children's books call the frog **Tiddalik**, and usually leave out the
drowning; Gunaikurnai people tell their own stories in their own way.

*A different kind of memory.* Patrick Nunn and Nicholas Reid (*Australian
Geographer* 47, 2016) gathered stories from 21 places on the Australian coast
that describe land being drowned by the sea, and argue that they remember the
rise in sea level that ended more than 7,000 years ago. These are stories of
coastlines lost, not of a world-flood sent on humankind; the claim of memory
that old is striking and debated.

### The Pacific

#### Hawaiʻi: how a story changed

The clearest case on this sheet of a story absorbing the Bible, because it was
written down three times.

- **1823.** The missionary William Ellis preached on Noah, and his hearers
  told him their own tradition. *"They said they were informed by their
  fathers, that all the land had once been overflowed by the sea, except a
  small peak on the top of Mouna-Kea, where two human beings were preserved
  from the destruction that overtook the rest, but they said they had never
  before heard of a ship, or of Noah."*
- **1843.** J. J. Jarves: now there is rain, and a canoe.
- **1878.** Abraham Fornander, from a Hawaiian Catholic convert: Nuʻu builds a
  houseboat at his god's command, is saved with his wife, three sons and
  their wives — eight — lands on Mauna Kea and offers a sacrifice. *"Then
  Kane descended on the rainbow… and left the rainbow as a token of his
  forgiveness."*

The folklorist Martha Beckwith (1940): *"The story of Nu‘u as told to the
missionaries shows a decided tendency to strain after biblical analogy."*
The 1878 teller should be read as a reader, not only as "contamination": a
Hawaiian Christian who set his people's story and Scripture side by side as
one, just as Philo and Justin Martyr said the Greek Deucalion was Noah (see
**[Noah among his readers](early-readers.html)**, §3).

#### Fiji: the god's slain bird

Recorded by the U.S. Exploring Expedition (1840) and the missionary Thomas
Williams (1858). *"The cause of this great flood was the killing of Turukawa —
a favourite bird belonging to Ndengei — by two mischievous lads, the
grandsons of the god."* The survivors are the **rebels themselves**, saved in
canoes. Williams noticed the number: *"The number saved — eight — exactly
accords with the 'few' of the Scripture record"*; the missionary R. H.
Codrington warned that, in the Banks Islands, the older flood story of the hero
Qat was likely to be retold with eight people in the canoe once Noah's story was
known. Fijians kept great canoes ready **against the next flood**.

#### Māori (Aotearoa New Zealand): Tāwhaki's revenge

From Māori manuscripts gathered by Sir George Grey (1855). Attacked and left
for dead by his brothers-in-law, the hero Tāwhaki *"called aloud to the Gods,
his ancestors, for revenge, and they let the floods of heaven descend, and the
earth was overwhelmed by the waters and all human beings perished."* Violence
causes this flood — but it is the hero's revenge, not a judgment on violence.

**Notes on the chart.** *Mesopotamia* is *Atrahasis* and *Gilgamesh*
together: *Atrahasis*'s cause is noise; its Sumerian cousin calls the hero
"reverent"; the goddess's necklace of lapis flies is a sign "that I may
remember the days." *Greece*: Pindar (468 BCE) has only the flood, Parnassus
and the stones; the warning, the chest, the sacrifice, the moral cause and the
righteous couple are in the full tellings of Apollodorus and Ovid, which are
not suspected of late borrowing from the Near East — unlike Lucian's pairs of animals
and Plutarch's dove (marked ○); the gods' worry about losing their incense is
Ovid's. *India*: animals appear only in the later Purāṇas; Iḍā's blessing of
offspring comes from the sacrifice; the fish is the rescuer; the cycles of ages
are Purāṇic, not in the earliest version (marked ○ on the last row). *Greece*:
Ovid's Jupiter holds back his thunderbolts because *"'twas in the fates that a
time would come when sea and land, the unkindled palace of the sky and the
beleaguered structure of the universe should be destroyed by fire"* — so the
world will end again, not by water. *Maya*: the maize people. *Nahua*:
humankind remade from the bones of the dead and a god's blood; the present age
will end too, though not by water; ✕ — the survivors' cooking smoke **angers**
the gods. *Innu, Ojibwe*: a raft or a tree on a mountain; animals cling on;
the raven and the divers look for mud, not dry land; the muskrat's earth
remakes the world. *Gunaikurnai*: the Pelican's canoe. *Hawaiʻi*: the 1878
version adds a houseboat, eight survivors, a sacrifice and a rainbow; the 1823
record has none of them (though it was given just after Ellis had preached on
Noah).

**What the chart shows — and what it cannot.** The middle band is built from
Genesis's own details, so Genesis fills it by construction. Build the rows from
*Gilgamesh*'s details, or the Maya's, and Genesis would have blanks too — the
last band does exactly that. So the chart cannot prove that Genesis is more
original than other stories; every story is distinctive in its details. What
it shows is **what Genesis says that the others do not**: one God who grieves
before he acts, a promise never to do it again sworn to every living thing, a
renewed blessing, a law that protects every human life. Two more things stand
out. The fuller outline — warning, vessel, mountain, sacrifice — clusters in
three traditions, Mesopotamia, Greece and India; elsewhere often only the
bare idea of destroying water remains. And **outside the Near East, the details that match Genesis most closely —
pairs of animals, the dove, the rainbow, eight survivors — appear only in late
Greek tellings that borrowed from the east (Lucian, Plutarch) or in records
made after missionaries arrived** (inside the Near East the closest
match, *Atrahasis*, is the oldest; early India and Greece share the outline,
not those details). A few partial echoes are early and apparently independent — India's
blessing of offspring, Ovid's righteous couple, the Maya's moral cause (which
looks native, though the *Popol Vuh* was written down after the conquest). Several peoples
say the opposite of "never again": Plato's Egyptian priest (*"will be again"*),
the Purāṇic cycles, the Nahua ages, the Mapuche climbing their hills, the
Fijians keeping their canoes ready.

## 5. Why are there so many flood stories?

Three answers have been given. They are not exclusive, and serious people hold
each. None of them, it should be said at once, decides whether Noah's flood
happened. For this class, the reason to believe it did is that Jesus and the
apostles speak of it (Matthew 24:37–39; 2 Peter 3:5–7), not how many peoples
tell flood stories.

**1. One flood, remembered.** The oldest answer. Josephus, in the first
century, already gathered the nations' witnesses: *"Now all the writers of
barbarian histories make mention of this flood, and of this ark."* It comes in
two forms. *The narrower form* — K. A. Kitchen, following W. W. Hallo, points to a flood
in southern Mesopotamia around 2900 BCE — sees a real, ancient flood behind the
Near Eastern stories, which is why they share the most detail: the boat, the pitch, the
animals, the birds, the mountain, the sacrifice. *The worldwide form*, argued by
Josephus and held by many Christians since, reads all the world's flood stories as fading
memories of Noah carried by his descendants; a memory carried across thousands
of years and miles would be expected to keep the outline and lose the details.
*Its strength:* it takes the stories' own claim — "this really happened" —
seriously, and the nearer stories do keep more. *The question it has to
answer:* outside the Near East, the details that match Genesis most closely —
pairs of animals, the dove, the rainbow, eight survivors — turn up mainly in
records made **after** missionaries arrived (Hawaiʻi, Fiji, the Maasai), or in
a forgery (Wales); and Africa, where Genesis 10 sends Ham's sons Cush, Egypt
and Put, has the fewest flood stories of all. (The regional form does not face
that question.) Frazer described the older approach: *"Formerly, under the
influence of the Biblical tradition, inquirers were disposed to identify
legends of a great flood, wherever found, with the familiar Noachian
deluge."*

**How far did Noah's flood reach?** Christians who hold Scripture's authority
disagree, and have for a long time. Many read it as **worldwide**: *"all the
high mountains under the entire sky were covered"* (7:19), and Peter speaks of
*"the world existing at that time"* (2 Peter 3:6) — the case made at length by
J. C. Whitcomb and H. M. Morris, *The Genesis Flood* (1961). Others read a vast
**regional** flood, covering the whole world of the people in the story:
Genesis can say a famine was *"severe throughout the earth"* when it means the
lands around Egypt (41:57). Bernard Ramm made this case in *The Christian View
of Science and Scripture* (1954), a book written largely because of what
geology had found; Tremper Longman and John Walton, *The Lost World of the
Flood* (2018), argue differently, that a real flood is told in deliberately
universal, hyperbolic language, as ancient accounts of great events often
were. The class does not need to settle
it to read the flood with the New Testament.

**2. Floods happen wherever people live.** Floods are the most common natural
disaster — about 44 per cent of all recorded disasters in 2000–2019 (UNDRR and
CRED, 2020) — and people have always settled on rivers, deltas and coasts. A
flood that fills one valley, or drowns one island, is the end of the world for
the people who live there. After the last ice age the sea rose by roughly 120
metres over thousands of years, drowning coastlines all over the world (which
is what Nunn and Reid's Australian stories seem to remember — a slow loss of
land, not a sudden deluge). Some stories were inferred from what people saw —
sea-shells on hilltops, a basin that looked as if it had drained — what Frazer
called *"myths of observation."* Frazer concluded that *"some and probably many
diluvial traditions are merely exaggerated reports of floods which actually
occurred,"* and that *"there still remain deluge legends which have originated
independently."* Egypt is a telling test: where the Nile rose gently from
below and was a blessing, there is no flood story at all. *Its strength:* it
explains the variety — the local mountains, the local animals, the local
causes. *The question it has to answer:* why do so many stories reach for
*all* of humankind and a handful of survivors?

**3. Stories travel.** They travelled in the ancient world: Greek myth
borrowed from the Near East long before Lucian (Hesiod's story of the gods'
succession has a close Hittite parallel), and Lucian's animals in pairs and
Plutarch's dove came into Greek tellings late, from the east. And they
travelled with the Bible: the missionary Moffat saw it happen in southern
Africa, and the three Hawaiian records show it step by step. Frazer wrote that
*"through the medium of Christian literature, the Babylonian legend has indeed
gone the round of the world and been echoed in tales told under the palms of
coral islands, in Indian wigwams, and amid the Arctic ice and snow."* *Its
strength:* every step is documented. *The question it has to answer:* stories
that existed before any contact — above all the Maya, whose inscriptions and a
painted book from before the conquest are read by epigraphers as showing a
flood ending an earlier world (Palenque, 734 CE; the Dresden Codex). Whether Manu's story came from
Babylon is still open.

**What all three can agree on.** The bare fact that many peoples tell of a
flood proves less than either side has claimed. It does not show that Genesis
copied a myth, and by itself it does not prove Genesis true. What a story
*means* lives in its details — who sends the water, and why; who is saved, and
why; and what comes afterwards.

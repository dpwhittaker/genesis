## Part 1 — The story everyone knew
<!-- ~1,000 words: who Atrahasis and Gilgamesh are; the copy-paste slip; the stories travelled but no flood tablet in Israel; koper and Driver's "possible"; the shared details as a read-along; both strands; did it happen (the NT settles it; global or regional); did Genesis borrow (two camps, each with its best case and weak spot; Wenham); the question to bring. -->

ETHAN: [relaxed and easygoing, settling in] So, quick reminder of who these characters are, because we've met them both.

SARAH: [easy, matter-of-fact] Atrahasis was Session one. The gods make people out of clay—

ETHAN: [jumping in, dry] —to do their work for them.

SARAH: [wry, a little amused] Which tells you a lot about those gods. And the big finish of that story is a flood.

ETHAN: [relaxed, picking up the thread] And Gilgamesh was Session eight. The king who wanted to make a name for himself.

SARAH: [thoughtful, filling it in] And he's scared of dying. So in Tablet Eleven he goes looking for the one man who survived the flood and got eternal life, and the man tells him how it happened.

ETHAN: [warm, clearly enjoying this detail] And here's a detail I love. Tablet Eleven took its flood from Atrahasis, and at one point it slips and calls its own hero "Atra-hasis."

SARAH: [small laugh, dry] Someone forgot to change the name.

ETHAN: [chuckling, deadpan] A very, very old copy-paste error.

SARAH: [curious, quick] And these stories got around. Copies of Atrahasis have turned up in Babylonia, on the Syrian coast, and at the Hittite capital, up in Turkey. And a piece of Gilgamesh turned up at Megiddo. [quietly delighted] In Canaan! So Israel had it.

ETHAN: [small laugh, gently cautioning] Slow down. That piece is from a different part of the epic, and nobody's found a flood tablet in Israel. So the careful way to say it is: the story was in the air.

SARAH: [curious, pressing a little] Okay, but then how do we know whoever wrote Genesis knew it?

ETHAN: [warm, building to the reveal] The details. The mountain, the birds, the altar, the smell of the sacrifice. And one word. In chapter six, God tells Noah to "cover it with pitch inside and out." And the Hebrew word for pitch there is koper.

SARAH: [dry, knowing] Let me guess.

ETHAN: [quietly pleased, explaining] It's nowhere else in the Bible. And it matches the Babylonian word for the pitch on Uta-napishti's boat: kupru.

SARAH: [laughing softly, dry] Koper. Kupru. That's not subtle.

ETHAN: [easy, reading the quote carefully] A scholar named Driver, back in nineteen-oh-four, put it like this: "it is possible therefore that kopher came into Hebrew, with the story, from Babylonia."

SARAH: [wry, teasing] "Possible." That is such a scholar word.

ETHAN: [laughs, warmly defending him] It's a good word, though. Pitch was traded. A word can travel with the cargo and not the story. It's the whole cluster of details that makes the case.

SARAH: [easy, playful] Okay, let's hear the cluster. You read Gilgamesh, I'll read Genesis.

ETHAN: [small laugh, then reading with a little drama] Deal. The boat: "Tear down the house, build a ship!"

SARAH: [reading, calm and clear] "Make for yourself an ark of cypress wood."

ETHAN: [reading, steady] Where it lands: "the Ark on the Mountain of Nisir grounded."

SARAH: [reading, calm] "The ark came to rest on one of the mountains of Ararat."

ETHAN: [reading slowly, savouring the details] The birds. A dove: "It did not have a resting place; it returned." Then a swallow. Then a raven, and "it did not return."

SARAH: [quietly amazed, dry] Genesis has a raven, and a dove that "could not find a resting place for its feet." Even the birds!

ETHAN: [amused, then reading slowly] Even the birds. Just in a different order. And then the sacrifice: "I offered a sacrifice… The gods smelled the sweet savor."

SARAH: [reading, calm] "Noah built an altar… And the Lord smelled the soothing aroma." [a beat, quietly convinced] Yeah. Okay. That's not a coincidence.

ETHAN: [warm, unhurried] And at the very end, something to remember it all by. Gilgamesh has a necklace, to keep "these days in remembrance."

SARAH: [reading, gentle] And Genesis has a bow. "I will remember my covenant." [a beat, then thinking out loud] Okay, so here's where my brain gets tangled. There are two questions. Did it happen? And did Genesis borrow it?

ETHAN: [calm, clear, steadying] And the trick is to keep them apart. Did it happen? For this class, the New Testament settles that. Jesus compares his coming to the days of Noah, and Peter puts the flood right next to the judgment to come.

SARAH: [thoughtful, adding to it] And Genesis tells it like history. It has a family heading, and dates, down to the day of Noah's life.

ETHAN: [matter-of-fact, relaxed] And archaeology can't settle it either way. The flood layers they've dug up in Mesopotamian cities are local, and from different times.

SARAH: [curious] So if you believe it happened, what's left to argue about?

ETHAN: [easy, explaining] How far the water went. Over the whole globe: "all the high mountains under the entire sky were covered."

SARAH: [thoughtful, offering the other view] Or a huge regional flood, told in the language of "all." The way Genesis can say a famine was "severe throughout the earth," when it means the countries around Egypt.

ETHAN: [relaxed, even-handed] And the handout doesn't pick. Both views are on the sheet about floods around the world.

SARAH: [easy, moving on] Okay. Question two. Did Genesis borrow?

ETHAN: [clear and fair, laying it out] Two camps, and both believe the flood happened. Camp one: Genesis knew the Babylonian story, and it's answering it. That Genesis drew on that story has been the majority view since eighteen seventy-two. The "answering it" part comes from Jewish scholars of the last century, people like Umberto Cassuto, Nahum Sarna and Tikva Frymer-Kensky.

SARAH: [thoughtful, giving it its due] And the best thing going for that one is that the biggest differences land exactly where the theology is.

ETHAN: [fair, even-handed] The weak spot: shared details don't prove copying, and not every difference is a rebuttal.

SARAH: [quietly intrigued] Camp two is the one I didn't expect. Nobody copied anybody. They both go back to an older tradition they shared. And that's Alan Millard, an evangelical who actually co-edited Atrahasis, and Kenneth Kitchen.

ETHAN: [warm, a little teasing] And Gordon Wenham lands somewhere interesting.

SARAH: [warm, genuinely appreciative] I loved him. He calls Genesis "a truly revolutionary work that fundamentally challenged the beliefs of that era." And then he says the parallels are "not so close that we can suppose the writer of Genesis borrowed from them or had even read them."

ETHAN: [thoughtful, starting the thought] So he keeps the answering—

SARAH: [jumping in, finishing his sentence, then explaining] —and drops the borrowing. And the case for camp two is that the flood memory is really old. The Babylonians dated their whole history by it. That King List from Session seven goes through all its kings and then just says, "Then the flood swept over."

ETHAN: [relaxed, adding to it] And Genesis is different even where nothing's at stake. The shape of the boat. The order of the birds.

SARAH: [curious, fair] And its weak spot?

ETHAN: [honest, even-handed] Nobody's ever found the shared source. And the birds, and the smell, and that word for pitch look an awful lot like contact with the Babylonian story itself.

SARAH: [wry, half-joking] So which one am I supposed to pick?

ETHAN: [warm, reassuring] You can hold either one. The comparison works the same way on both. If you grew up on Gilgamesh, you'd know where Genesis was going at every turn—

SARAH: [jumping in, warm, then thoughtful] —and you'd keep getting surprised. Okay, one to chew on before class. Imagine hearing Genesis for the first time with Gilgamesh already in your head. What would you be listening for?

## Part 2 — Nine places Genesis goes its own way: 1. Why, 2. Who's in charge
<!-- ~650 words: the noise complaint and Enlil's three tries vs the heart and violence; multiplying is the blessing; Ea and the wall (Thompson); the gods cower like a dog; one God opens and closes the deep; a wind over the waters, creation run backward (2 Pet 3:5-6); one God, so the tension is in his heart. -->

SARAH: [wry, clearly enjoying it] All right. Nine places where Genesis goes its own way. And number one is my favourite, because it's ridiculous. Why does the flood happen in Atrahasis?

ETHAN: [chuckling] Go on.

SARAH: [dry, deadpan, savouring the absurdity] Noise. People multiplied, and they got so loud the god Enlil couldn't sleep. "Concerning their clamor I am troubled."

ETHAN: [deadpan] The world's first noise complaint.

SARAH: [laughs softly, then dry] And he really tries. He sends a plague. Then a drought. Then a famine. And still, "The people have not become less; they are more numerous than before."

ETHAN: [dry] So, the flood.

SARAH: [wry, dry] So, the flood. And Gilgamesh doesn't even bother with a reason. "The great gods, brought their hearts to send a deluge."

ETHAN: [warmer, more serious] And Genesis gives a reason twice. We spent last session on it. The heart: "Every inclination of the thoughts of their minds was only evil all the time." And violence: "the earth was filled with violence."

SARAH: [quietly, filling it in] Violence meaning what people do to each other.

ETHAN: [thoughtful, then noticing something] What people do to each other. And here's the thing: chapter six opens with people multiplying too. "When humankind began to multiply on the face of the earth."

SARAH: [mildly surprised] Oh. So Genesis knows the other story.

ETHAN: [warm, gentle] It knows it. But in Genesis, multiplying is the blessing, all the way back to chapter one. It's never the crime.

SARAH: [easy, moving on] Okay. Number two. Who's actually in charge.

ETHAN: [warm, clearly delighted, settling into the story] Oh, I could read this one all day. In the Babylonian story, a council of gods decides on the flood, and they all swear an oath to keep it secret. Including Ea, the clever one.

SARAH: [wry, amused] Uh-oh. Clever.

ETHAN: [playful, conspiratorial] Clever. So he can't tell a man. But nothing in the oath says he can't talk to… a house. So he goes to the reed hut where the man lives, and he talks to the wall. This is an old translation from the twenties, so everybody sounds like Shakespeare.

ETHAN: [lower, storytelling, a touch of mock grandeur] "Nin-igi-azag — 'tis Ea — albeit conspirator with them, unto a reed-hut their counsel betrayed he: O Reed-hut, O Reed-hut! Wall, wall! Hearken, O Reed-hut, consider, O Wall!"

SARAH: [laughing, dry] "Wall, wall!" He's whispering secrets to the drywall.

ETHAN: [dry, amused] And technically, he kept his oath.

SARAH: [still laughing a little] A loophole god. Okay, then what?

ETHAN: [relaxed, then reading with relish] Then the storm comes, and the gods lose control of their own flood. "The gods fear the deluge. They withdraw, they ascend to the heaven of Anu. The gods cower like a dog."

SARAH: [laughs softly] Like a dog in a thunderstorm. Hiding under the bed.

ETHAN: [dry, amused] From their own storm.

SARAH: [quieter, more serious] And Genesis is just… the opposite. One God. He decides, he warns, he saves. He opens "all the fountains of the great deep," and he closes them again.

ETHAN: [warm, slowing down] And right in the middle: "God remembered Noah… God caused a wind to blow over the earth and the waters receded."

SARAH: [quietly surprised] Wait. A wind over the waters. That's Genesis one.

ETHAN: [warm, pleased, explaining] That's Genesis one. The flood is creation running backward, and then God runs it forward again. Peter reads it that way too: an earth "formed out of water and by means of water," and then "deluged with water."

SARAH: [thoughtful, putting it together] So in Babylon, the god who destroys and the god who rescues are two different gods.

ETHAN: [gently, a little wry] And they don't get along. Genesis puts both in one God.

SARAH: [thoughtful, quieter] Which is why last session felt so heavy. The tension's inside God's own heart.

## 3. What the hero told the neighbours

SARAH: [gentle, reading] And Genesis says, "Noah found favor in the sight of the Lord."

ETHAN: [warm, quiet] Grace first. Then righteous.

## 4. A box with no rudder, 5. Hungry gods

## 7. A god, or a man of the soil, and a note on 9:25

## Part 3 — Floods around the world: the claim, and a tour
<!-- ~650 words: "every culture" stated accurately; Frazer on Africa; Egypt's red beer; Manu and the horned fish; Deucalion's stones; Yu drains it; the Maya wooden people; Tata and Nene's smoke; the muskrat; the frog and the dancing eel; the pelican; Fiji's bird and canoes; "the same coat". -->

SARAH: [curious, a little skeptical] Okay, the other argument. "Every culture has a flood story." Is that true?

## Part 3 — What the chart shows, and why there are so many
<!-- ~800 words: the outline rows vs Genesis's rows; Lauren's fair objection and the last band; what it can and cannot show; the close matches come late (Hawaii 1823 to 1878; Philo and Justin); "it will happen again"; three answers, each with its hard question; we believe it because Jesus and the apostles speak of it; the outline was common property; the question for the group. -->

ETHAN: [easy, explaining] Outside the Near East, those only turn up in late Greek versions that borrowed from the east, or in stories written down after missionaries arrived. SARAH: [quietly amused, reading] And here's the line: "they said they had never before heard of a ship, or of Noah."

ETHAN: [warm, a little amused] And by eighteen seventy-eight, the version a Hawaiian convert wrote down had a houseboat, eight survivors, a sacrifice, and a rainbow.

SARAH: [dry, amused] So in fifty-five years, it… grew an ark.

ETHAN: [chuckling, warm] He was reading his people's story and Scripture as one story.

SARAH: [thoughtful] And here's what got me. A bunch of these peoples expected the flood to come back. Plato's Egyptian priest. The later Hindu cycles. The Aztecs. The Mapuche in Chile. Fiji, with the canoes.

ETHAN: [thoughtful] So "never again"—

SARAH: [jumping in, quietly] —is the unusual part.

ETHAN: [quiet] In that company, it is.

SARAH: [curious] So why are there so many flood stories?

ETHAN: [clear, even-handed] The handout gives three answers, and you can combine them. One: there was one flood, and people remember it. SARAH: [fair, laying it out] And the problem with the wide version is that the close matches come late, or after missionaries. And Africa, where Genesis ten sends Ham's sons, has almost none. ETHAN: [easy, then thoughtful] And Egypt, where the Nile rose nice and gently, has no flood story at all. Though then you have to ask why so many of them reach for all of humankind. And three: stories travel. In the ancient world, and with the Bible.

SARAH: [skeptical, fair] Except the Maya. Their art from before the conquest seems to show a flood ending an earlier world.

ETHAN: [quiet, measured] So none of the three settles whether Noah's flood happened.

SARAH: [thoughtful, sincere] We believe it did because Jesus and the apostles talk about it. Not because of how many peoples tell flood stories.

ETHAN: [warm, even-handed] And flood stories prove less than both sides have claimed. They don't show that Genesis copied a myth. And on their own, they don't prove Genesis true.

SARAH: [quiet] The outline was common property.

ETHAN: [warm] The details weren't.

SARAH: [warm, thoughtful] Okay, one for the group. If you had to explain to a friend what Genesis says about God, using only the rows where Genesis stands alone, what would you say? And what do everybody else's rows say about what they hoped for, and what they were afraid of?

## A note before we begin

Two warnings, both kind ones.

**First:** today's subject is shame, and shame is not an abstraction for anybody. Somewhere in this handout you will probably meet a sentence that lands closer to home than you expected. If it does, that is the material working, not you failing. Nothing here requires you to say anything out loud that you would rather not.

**Second:** last session was heavy machinery — seven problems, one at a time. Today is built differently: **one thread, pulled all the way through.** Twenty-one of Session 3's twenty-eight problems live in Genesis 2–3, and they come off the list as we go — but the list is not the plan. The thread is the plan.

## Aim of this session

The thread is **nakedness**.

The story puts that word at its exact center *(problem 18)*, and uses it as the hinge of nearly everything that matters: it is the last thing said about the world before it breaks, the first thing known after, the subject of God's sharpest question, and the problem God personally solves on the way out the door.

And it turns out to be the way into the question this class has been asking: **what does the snake actually represent?** Because the snake is *made of* this word. He is named with it, he trades on it, and it is what he leaves behind when he goes.

Four movements: what nakedness was **before**, **who** the snake is, **how** he works — a repeatable technique, running today — and **what he actually delivers**, which is not what he advertises.

## Part 1 — Naked, and not ashamed

> The man and his wife were both naked, but they were not ashamed. *(Genesis 2:25)*

Of everything the narrator could have told us about unfallen humanity, he picks this *(problem 10)*. The strangeness is the point: the sentence is a hinge, closing the creation of the woman and opening the arrival of the snake — the only verse that faces both directions.

It is not describing innocence as ignorance. It is describing the **absence of a gap**: nothing about them requires managing, editing, or covering — no part of the self held back from the other, and none held back from God.

Put the two halves together and you get a combination none of us has experienced for long. **Completely exposed. Entirely unafraid.** We know exposure and safety as a *trade* — the more of one, the less of the other. Genesis opens with a world where they are the same condition, and calls it *home*.

**And "naked" is not a neutral word in this building.** Last session ended with the claim that the cosmos is a temple. Genesis 2 walks you inside, and the garden is the inner room. The text keeps saying so in the technical vocabulary:

- The man is put there **"to care for it and to maintain it"** (2:15) — *ʿāḇaḏ* and *šāmar*, the exact verb pair Numbers uses for the duty of the **Levites** in the sanctuary (Numbers 3:7–8).
- God is heard **"moving about in the orchard"** (3:8) — *mithallēk*, the form used for God's presence **in the camp and the tabernacle** (Leviticus 26:12).
- **Cherubim** guard the way at the end (3:24) — woven into the tabernacle veil, and their verb is *lišmōr*, **to guard**: the man's own job from 2:15, reassigned to angels after he fails it.

And **Jubilees** (c. **160–150 BCE**) applies the purity laws for entering the *sanctuary* to entering *Eden*, on the grounds that **"it is holier than all the earth besides"** ([3:12–19](texts/second-temple-sources.md)).

That changes what 2:25 is saying. **In a sanctuary, nakedness is not a wardrobe issue. It is a vestment issue.** Priests may not climb the altar steps *"so that your nakedness is not exposed"* (Exodus 20:26). And one law states Genesis 3:8–10 in advance:

> Since the LORD your God **walks about** in the middle of your camp… your camp is to be holy, so that he does not see **anything indecent** among you and turn away from you. *(Deuteronomy 23:14 — "anything indecent" is* ʿerwat dāḇār, *"the nakedness of a thing")*

**Walking. Nakedness. Turning away.** That is the vocabulary of Genesis 3 in the form of a statute.

So Genesis is not telling us they hadn't invented clothes. It is saying that in the one place where being uncovered is a problem, **they had no problem** — something was already covering them. The earliest readers all reached for the same answer: **glory.**

- **Apocalypse of Moses 20** (first century CE), Eve: *"I knew that I was bare of the righteousness with which I had been clothed… thou hast **deprived me of the glory with which I was clothed**."*
- **Rabbi Meir** (second century) is reported to have had a Torah scroll reading, at 3:21, not *kotnôt ʿôr*, garments of **skin**, but *kotnôt ʾôr* — **garments of light** (*Genesis Rabbah* 20:12). One letter apart in Hebrew.

None of that is in Genesis. All of it is what its first readers thought it meant ([two more say the same](texts/second-temple-sources.md)). When independent witnesses fill a silence the same way, the silence is worth respecting.

<div class="discuss" markdown="1">
**Talk about it.** "Completely exposed, entirely unafraid." Name one relationship where you get closest to that — and one place where you spend real energy on the covering. What does the energy cost you?
</div>

## Part 2 — The one who is *ʿārûm*

Now the sentence that follows — in Hebrew the two sit in consecutive breaths *(problem 19)*:

> They were both **naked** *(ʿărummîm)*… Now the serpent was **shrewder** *(ʿārûm)* than any of the wild animals. *(2:25–3:1)*

English cannot show it. The storyteller has rhymed their **condition** with his **character** — and the first thing to ask is why.

**Because it is not a bad word.** *ʿārûm* appears eleven times in the Hebrew Bible; eight are in **Proverbs**, and in every one it is a compliment. The *ʿārûm* is the **prudent** person, who sees trouble coming and takes cover (22:3) and *"ponders their course"* while the simple believe anything (14:15). This is a wisdom virtue — what Proverbs is *for*.

So the villain of Genesis 3 is not stupidity, and not obvious malice. **He is prudence** — the quality the wisdom books tell you to cultivate, run in reverse. Proverbs supplies the reverse in so many words:

> A fool's annoyance is known at once, but **one who covers over shame** *(kōseh qālôn)* **is shrewd** *(ʿārûm)*. *(Proverbs 12:16)*

The *ʿārûm* of Proverbs **covers shame**. The *ʿārûm* of Genesis 3 **strips it bare**. Same word, exact inversion — and if you want a one-line answer to what the snake represents, that is a serious candidate: **the gift of discernment, unhooked from trust, aimed at the exposure of someone else.**

**So who is he?** The honest sequence, in order of date.

**1. Genesis says: an animal.** *"One of the wild animals **that the LORD God had made**."* That is a loud demotion. Every neighbor Israel had kept a cosmic serpent — the chaos-monster the storm god must kill before the world can hold still. Isaiah still speaks the language (*"Leviathan the fleeing serpent,"* 27:1 — word for word the Ugaritic **Lotan**, enemy of Baal); in *Gilgamesh* a snake steals the plant of rejuvenation, and that is how humanity loses immortality. Snakes meant **wisdom, immortality, chaos, and royal power** — and Genesis files the whole portfolio under *animals, field*, on the inventory the man named in 2:20. The loudest thing the story says about the snake is how little it says.

**2. Second Temple Judaism starts to name him.** *"By the **envy of the devil**, death came into the world"* (Wisdom of Solomon 2:24, first century BCE) — and **3. the New Testament states it flatly**: *"The ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan"* (Revelation 12:9).

**4. The rabbis mostly went a third way**, the one most useful on a Tuesday: the snake is the ***yetzer ha-raʿ***, the **evil inclination** — not a creature outside you but a pull inside you. **Reish Lakish** (third century) collapses the cast into one — *"Satan, the evil inclination, and the Angel of Death **are one**"* — the voice that *"seduces people and then accuses them"* ([*Bava Batra* 16a](texts/rabbinic-sources.md)).

These are frames, not rivals — but notice what each costs. If the snake is only an enemy *outside* you, this story is about **security**, and you will spend your life watching the perimeter. If he is also the voice you carry, it is about **you**.

**And here is the tell.** In Hebrew the snake speaks **twenty-six words**, in two short speeches, and never speaks again. He never touches the fruit. He asks one question, contradicts one sentence, supplies one motive — **and exits before verse six.**

Everything after that is human: the seeing, the wanting, the taking, the giving, the sewing, the hiding, the blaming. Whoever the snake is, **the story is not about him** — and a class that spends its energy identifying him has been drawn off exactly the way the man was.

<div class="discuss" markdown="1">
**Talk about it.** Genesis had every serpent myth in the ancient world available and chose to call this one a field animal God made. What changes in how you carry this story if the snake is *not* a rival power — and what do you lose?
</div>

## Part 3 — The con, in four moves

This is the practical heart of the session, because the technique is reproducible and it is being run on you this week.

### Move 1 — Quote the generosity, and reverse it

God's command in 2:16 is extravagant in Hebrew: *mikkōl ʿēṣ-haggān **ʾākōl tōʾkēl*** — *"from **every** tree of the garden, **eating you shall eat**."* The doubled verb is the Hebrew way of leaning on a word: freely, certainly, without limit. One tree is off the list. **The rest of the world is a yes.**

Now the snake's opening line. He takes that exact phrase and puts a **"not"** in front of it:

> Is it really true that God said, **'You must not eat from any tree of the orchard'**? *(3:1)*

He has invented nothing. **He has quoted God's single most generous sentence with the polarity reversed.** Ten thousand yeses, restated as one no.

That is the engine of the whole thing: **the con is never "here is something evil." It is "look at what you are missing."** It works by subtraction, and by getting you to run the audit yourself.

### Move 2 — Make her say it

Notice that he asks rather than asserts. The damage is not in his question; it is in **her answer**:

| God said (2:16–17) | She reports (3:2–3) |
|---|---|
| from **every** tree… **eating you shall eat** | *"We may eat of the fruit from the trees"* — the *every* is gone |
| *(nothing about touching)* | *"and you must not **touch** it"* — added |
| **dying you shall die** | *"**or else** you will die"* — softened to a maybe |
{: .ff-table .ff-wrap}

Three edits in one sentence. **The gift shrinks, the fence grows, the warning goes vague** — and he has not made an argument yet. He has simply gotten her talking.

The rabbis went straight at the added clause *(problem 7)*. ***Genesis Rabbah* 19:3**, quoting **Rabbi Ḥiyya** (early third century): you should *"not make the fence taller than the main object being protected"* — and then, *"when it saw her passing before the tree, **it took hold of her and pushed her onto it**… 'See, you did not die. Just as you did not die from touching it, so you will not die from eating of it.'"*

**The fence became the lever.** And ***Avot de-Rabbi Natan*** names who built it, answering Session 3's question about how she learned the rule at all *(problem 6)*: **Adam** *"was unwilling to tell this to Eve exactly as the Holy One… had commanded him; he therefore **made a fence to his words**."* The result:

> Eve then began to think to herself, *"**All that my master has commanded me from the beginning is false**"*… Now what brought about the act of touching? **The answer is the fence which Adam had made to his words.**

Read that twice. She does not conclude *the fence was wrong*. She concludes **everything was**. When you cannot tell your fence from God's word, the day the fence fails, the word falls with it. The Talmud fixes it in a maxim: *"anyone who **adds**, **subtracts**"* (*Sanhedrin* 29a).

### Move 3 — Give God a motive

Only now does he argue — and look at what he attacks. Not the rule. **The rule-giver.**

> For **God knows** that when you eat from it your eyes will open and you will be like God. *(3:5)*

The claim is that the command is not protection but **self-protection**: behind the "no" is a rival guarding his position. The midrash spells it out in the snake's own voice (*Genesis Rabbah* 19:4, quoting **Rabbi Levi**, third century) — he *"began to **slander its Creator**,"* saying God forbids the tree *"so you will not create other worlds. For **every person hates the craftsman that competes with him**."* And then, a few lines on:

> **"Hurry and eat now, before He creates additional worlds and they will dominate you."**

*Hurry. Before.* That is a fifth-century Jewish commentary describing, with total precision, the fear of missing out — a manufactured scarcity, a closing window, and a Giver recast as a competitor.

### Move 4 — Stand back and let desire finish the job

Then he stops talking, and the Hebrew of 3:6 does something we should not walk past.

Back in 2:9 the narrator already told us what the garden's trees were like: every tree ***neḥmāḏ*** to look at and ***ṭôḇ*** for food — **desirable** and **good**. The whole garden was desirable. That was never in short supply.

Now 3:6 lists three things about **this** tree: *ṭôḇ* for food, a ***taʾăwâ*** to the eyes (a **craving**), and ***neḥmāḏ*** — desirable — **to make one wise**.

So the famous "third attraction" *(problem 8)* is not a new adjective. It is the **same desire with a new object**. She is not looking at a piece of fruit differently. **She is looking at a promotion.**

And those two words are not neutral: Deuteronomy's tenth commandment uses both — *lōʾ **taḥmōḏ**… wĕlōʾ **tiṯʾawweh***, do not **covet**, do not **crave** (5:21). **The first sin in the Bible is narrated in the exact vocabulary the tenth commandment will use.** Coveting is the desire for a thing *because it is not yours* — fear of missing out with an older and more honest name.

Then the verbs turn *(problem 17)*: she **saw** that it was **good**… and **took**. That is God's inspection formula from Genesis 1, in her hands.

**And check what he never did.** He never touched the fruit, offered it, or picked it. He asked a question, reversed a sentence, and named a motive. **The rest she did herself, and that is the design.**

### The scoreboard *(problems 2 and 25)*

Here is the difficulty Session 3 left standing. Score the predictions and the snake appears to win: their eyes *do* open (3:7), they *do* become "like God, knowing good and evil" — and it is **God who says so** (3:22) — and they do not drop dead that day.

So was he telling the truth? No, and the place it breaks is the death. ***Môṯ tāmûṯ***, "dying you shall die," is a **sentencing formula**, not a medical prediction. Solomon warns Shimei that *"on the day"* he crosses the Kidron he will *"surely die"* (1 Kings 2:37). Shimei crosses; he is executed **three years later** — and the king quotes his own words back as having been kept exactly. Nobody in that story thinks Solomon lied. *On the day you eat, you are under sentence.* And the sentence is carried out: the presence lost, the tree of life barred, dust at the end of the road.

Which leaves the real finding. **The snake did not lie about the outcome. He lied about the Giver** — and about what the outcome would be worth.

## Part 4 — What he actually delivers

> Then the eyes of both of them opened, and they knew **they were naked**. *(3:7)*

Promised: opened eyes, godlikeness, the wisdom of the tree. Delivered: **a mirror** *(problem 11)*.

He was not wrong that their eyes would open. **He simply never mentioned what they would see.** And what they see is not the cosmos, or good and evil in the abstract, or anything like a promotion. They see **themselves** — and the sight is unbearable.

The Hebrew registers it in the spelling. In 2:25 they are *ʿărummîm*, the form that rhymes with the snake's own name for himself. In 3:7 the narrator switches to *ʿêrummîm*, the form Deuteronomy and Ezekiel use for the nakedness of the **destitute and the exiled**. **The pun dies at the moment the thing it described is lost.**

**Then two coverings, for two audiences.** Fig leaves are for each other; trees are for God (3:7–8). Nothing has changed about their bodies. What has changed is what it now costs **to be seen**.

**And notice what they never say.** God asks where the man is, and this is his answer:

> I heard you moving about in the orchard, and **I was afraid because I was naked**, so I hid. *(3:10)*

He does not say *I ate*. He files a wardrobe report. And that distinction is the most usable thing in this session:

> **Guilt** says *I did something bad.* Its grammar is **confession**, and confession has an address.
> **Shame** says *I am something bad.* Its grammar is **hiding**, and hiding has nowhere to go.

The first human response to sin in the Bible is not repentance. **It is concealment.** Then the blame, running two directions at once: *"The woman **whom you gave me**, she gave me some fruit"* (3:12). She is guilty — and so, in the same breath, is the one who gave her. Shame cannot bear its own weight for a single verse; it hands it to the nearest person, and then upward.

**Which brings us to the three questions** *(problem 27)*.

**"Where are you?"** — God is not lost. *Genesis Rabbah* 19:9 hears the consonants of *ʾayyekkāh* and notices they spell ***ʾêkāh***: *how…?* — the first word of **Lamentations**. God's first sentence after the fall is not a search but a **lament**, the same one he will sing over the exile of his people. Not *I can't find you.* **How did it come to this.**

**"Who told you that you were naked?"** — take this one home. It presupposes something enormous: **shame is not native. It was installed.** They had been naked all along and it had never once been a problem, so the arrival of the idea has a source, and a source has a voice. God is asking Adam to **trace the sentence back** — and the unspoken second half is: *because it was not mine.*

**"What is this you have done?"** — the only one of the three that hands him a confession, ready-made, if he will take it. He does not.

**And then God does two things, and the order matters.**

He does not lift the exile — 3:23–24 stands, the consequence is real and the door is guarded. And then *(problem 12)*: **he makes them clothes.** Their fig-leaf *ḥăgōrōṯ* are **loincloths**. What God makes are *kotnôṯ* — **tunics** — and *kuttōneṯ* is the priest's word, the garment Aaron and his sons are given *"for dignity and adornment"* (Exodus 28:39–40). **The last thing God does before the exile is dress them like priests.**

Their covering worked with each other and failed in the presence. His works in the presence, which is the only place it was ever needed.

**And one line before they go** *(problem 13)*. In the middle of a death sentence, having just been told he is dust, the man turns and names his wife **Eve — "mother of all the living"** (3:20). It is the first act of hope in the Bible, and it happens **inside the curse**.

<div class="discuss" markdown="1">
**Talk about it.** *"Who told you that you were naked?"* Pick a sentence you actually carry about yourself — the kind that starts *"I am…"* Don't debate whether it's true. Just ask the question God asked: **who told you that?** And is that a voice you would let speak to someone you love?
</div>

## Part 5 — Where the tradition goes somewhere else

It would be dishonest to quote only the sources that agree with us. Four places the tradition parts company with this reading — [all of them at length in the reference version](reference.html):

- **The earliest reading we possess blames the woman, and Genesis does not.** ***Ben Sira* 25:24** (c. **180 BCE**, older than any rabbi or the New Testament): *"From the woman came the beginning of sin, and by her we all die."* The text does not support it — she gave the fruit *"to her husband **who was with her**"* (*ʿimmāh*, standing right there, silent, the whole conversation), and God's first question goes to **him**. Paul puts it on **Adam** (Romans 5:12–19). When the earliest reading and the text disagree, take the text.
- **On the snake's motive, the rabbis go where we did not.** *Genesis Rabbah* 18:6 says he saw them together and **desired her**; *Avot de-Rabbi Natan* makes it ambition — *"I will slay Adam and marry his wife; then I will be king."* Both make him a rival suitor rather than a con artist.
- **Philo of Alexandria** (a contemporary of Jesus) reads it as pure allegory: the serpent is **pleasure**, the woman sense-perception, the man the mind. Not our reading — but notice that a first-century Jew already assumed the snake was a picture of something **inside** a person.
- **And one that is simply better than anything we said.** *Targum Pseudo-Jonathan* on 3:21 renders the garments as *"vestures of honour **from the skin of the serpent, which he had cast from him**."* The tempter's own shed skin, made into the covering of the people he shamed.

**A warning the tradition aims at us.** *Genesis Rabbah* 19:1 opens on the snake not with the snake but with Ecclesiastes — *"he who increases knowledge increases pain"* — and then, in the name of **Rabbi Meir**: *"The downfall of the serpent was in accordance with its greatness — **the more cunning, the more cursed**."* The midrash's warning about this chapter is pointed at the **clever**, not the foolish. Which is to say: at a group that has spent five sessions getting good at this.

## Part 6 — So what?

Five things. They are all versions of one thing.

**1. Count the yeses out loud.** The con opens by getting you to audit what you lack, and it always disguises the subtraction as a question. The counter-move is arithmetic, said out loud, before the argument starts: *from every tree of the garden you may freely eat.* Gratitude is not a mood here. It is **accurate accounting**, and it is the only thing that was ever going to make the one tree look small.

**2. Name the voice.** *"Who told you that you were naked?"* is a tool, not just a line. When a sentence arrives that begins *you are* — behind, worthless, too much, not enough, unforgivable — the useful question is not *is this true* (shame is very good at that argument). It is **who said that**, and **when did I start letting them**. God's own diagnostic in the garden is source-tracing.

**3. Mark your fences.** Fences are not the problem — Judaism builds them deliberately. The problem is an **unmarked** fence: a preference remembered as a commandment. Say which is which, out loud, to your kids especially — because the day your fence fails, whatever you failed to distinguish from it goes down at the same time. Eve's conclusion was not *the fence was wrong*. It was *all of it was false.*

**4. Answer guilt and shame differently, because they are different.** Guilt has a remedy and the remedy is short: **say what you did**, to someone with the standing to hear it. Shame has no such remedy, because it is not about a deed — which is exactly why it sends you off to sew. Better fig leaves are not the answer to shame. **Being found is.**

**5. Let yourself be dressed.** The fig leaves were not wicked. They were just not adequate to the room. And the last thing God does inside the garden is make a covering that is — one that costs something, and that they did not make.

**And that thread runs the length of the Bible.** God finds Israel *"naked and bare,"* covers her, and calls the covering a **covenant** (Ezekiel 16:7–8). An accuser stands up against a high priest in filthy clothes, and the answer is not a counter-argument but a **change of clothes** (Zechariah 3:1–5). A father calls for the **best robe** before his son's rehearsed confession is finished (Luke 15:22). And Paul, refusing to let any of this become an escape from the body, says the hope is *"not that we want to be **unclothed**, but **clothed**"* (2 Corinthians 5:4).

**And then the inversion that ends it.** Philippians 2:6 says of Christ that he *"did not regard **equality with God** as something to be **grasped**."* That is Genesis 3:5, run backwards, in the same words. The snake offered godlikeness **by seizure** to two people who had already been given the image. The second Adam, who was *in the form of God*, declined the grasp — *"emptied himself,"* and was **stripped**, and died with nothing on, in public.

**Naked, so that the ones who grabbed could be clothed.**

**One last honesty.** Genesis will not tell you the snake's name, and we should not pretend it did. What it tells you is his **method** — reverse the generosity, get you talking, indict the Giver, then stand back — and what he actually pays out, which is never the promotion and always the mirror. That is enough to recognize him without knowing what he is. **You are not asked to identify the voice. You are asked to notice that it is not God's.**

<div class="discuss" markdown="1">
**Talk about it — to close.** *First:* where is something being sold to you right now on the strength of what you'd be missing? Name it out loud; the con mostly survives on not being named. *Second, and concretely:* this week, one of two things. Either **count the yeses** — write down what is actually already yours before you look at what isn't. Or **let yourself be found** by one person: say the thing you have been sewing leaves over, to someone who will not hand you a mirror.
</div>

# The rabbis on Genesis 3

A source sheet for **Session 5**. Everything here comes from the Jewish
interpretive tradition. For the readings that are older than this literature —
Ben Sira, Jubilees, Josephus and the rest — see the companion sheet,
**[the earliest readings](second-temple-sources.md)**.

**On dates, since Session 5 keeps insisting on them.** These documents were
compiled centuries after the sages they quote, and they quote them by name.
Both facts matter. Where a passage is attributed to a named rabbi, we give
that rabbi's own century as well as the document's, so you can see how far the
tradition is reaching back — and so you can hold the attribution for what it
is: **tradition, not transcript.**

| Document | Compiled | Attributed voices reach back to |
|---|---|---|
| **Mishnah** | c. 200 CE | 1st century BCE onward |
| **Genesis Rabbah** | c. 400–450 CE, Roman Palestine | 2nd–4th century sages |
| **Babylonian Talmud** | c. 500–600 CE | 1st–5th century sages |
| ***Avot de-Rabbi Natan*** | debated: 3rd–9th century, on tannaitic material | 1st–3rd century |
| **Targum Pseudo-Jonathan** | final form c. 7th–8th century or later | earlier Palestinian traditions |
{: .ff-table .ff-wrap}

Each entry is marked **→ toward the session's reading** or **⇢ a different
direction**. Both are useful. Where the tradition agrees with us it is a very
old witness that we did not invent this; where it diverges it is a reminder
that a good text carries more than one true reading.

---

## 1. The serpent, and why the word is *ʿārûm*

### Genesis Rabbah 19:1 → *toward the session's reading — and a warning aimed at us*

The midrash opens its treatment of the snake not with the snake but with
Ecclesiastes — reading the whole episode as a story about **wisdom going
wrong**.

> "The serpent was…cunning" – it is written: "For with great wisdom is great
> anger, and he who increases knowledge increases pain" (Ecclesiastes 1:18).
> By increasing wisdom a person increases anger against himself; by increasing
> knowledge he brings pain upon himself… Have you ever heard anyone say:
> 'This donkey is afflicted by a cold?'… **Where is suffering found? It is
> among human beings.**… Rabbi Yishmael taught: **In accordance with the
> camel, so is its burden.**… It is taught in the name of **Rabbi Meir**: The
> downfall of the serpent was in accordance with its greatness – **the more
> cunning, the more cursed.** **Rabbi Hoshaya Rabba** said: It stood erect
> like a reed, **and it had legs**… **Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar** said: It was
> like a camel. **The world lost a great benefit**, as were it not so, man
> would have been able to send merchandise with it and it would have been able
> to come and go by itself.

*Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar: 2nd century. Rabbi Hoshaya Rabba:
early 3rd century.*

Three things worth noticing. **(1)** The warning of the passage is pointed at
the **capable**, not the foolish — the bigger the camel, the heavier the load.
**(2)** Rabbi Hoshaya answers Session 3's problem 3 (*so how was the snake
getting around before?*) exactly as Josephus does three centuries earlier: it
had legs. **(3)** Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar's line is unexpectedly sad. He reads
the curse as a loss to **everyone** — the world is poorer for the snake's
ruin.

---

### Genesis Rabbah 18:6 ⇢ *a different direction*

> "The serpent was…cunning" – … **Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korḥa** said: It is to
> inform you **what misdeed prompted that evil one to assail them**. It is
> because it saw them engaging in the way of nature in the open, and **it
> lusted for her**.

*Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korḥa: 2nd century.*

The snake as a rival suitor rather than a con artist — a motive of **desire**,
not slander. *Avot de-Rabbi Natan* (below) makes it ambition instead. Both
answer a question Genesis pointedly refuses to answer: *why does he bother?*

The same passage also contains a small piece of Hebrew wordplay on our theme.
Reading *"and they were not ashamed"* (*ve-lo yitboshashu*) as though it
contained *shesh*, "six," **Rabbi Eliezer** finds in it that Adam did not
spend even **six hours** in peace — a lovely bit of gallows humour about how
short the unashamed life turned out to be.

## 2. The fence — and how it became a lever

### Genesis Rabbah 19:3 → *toward the session's reading*

> "…nor shall you touch it" – that is what is written: "**Do not add to His
> words**, lest He rebuke you, and you will be found to be false"
> (Proverbs 30:6). **Rabbi Ḥiyya** taught: This teaches that you should
> **not make the fence taller than the main object being protected**, so it
> should not fall and sever the saplings. So, the Holy One blessed be He said
> merely: "As on the day that you eat of it you shall die" (Genesis 2:17). But
> she did not say that, rather she added on to it and said: "God said: You
> shall not eat of it, **nor shall you touch it**." **When it saw her passing
> before the tree, it took hold of her and pushed her onto it.** It said to
> her: '**See, you did not die.** Just as you did not die from touching it, so
> you will not die from eating of it.'

*Rabbi Ḥiyya: early 3rd century.*

This is the single most-quoted rabbinic reading of Genesis 3, and it is a
piece of close reading of exactly the kind Session 3 taught: the rabbis
noticed that **the woman quotes a rule God never gave**, and built the whole
mechanism of the fall out of that one added clause.

---

### *Avot de-Rabbi Natan* 1 → *toward the session's reading*

The same observation, developed at length — and here the fence is **Adam's**,
which answers Session 3's problem 6 (*how did she learn the command at all?*).
The chapter is a commentary on the Mishnah's own instruction to *"make a fence
around the Torah"* (*Avot* 1:1), which makes its conclusion all the more
striking.

> What fence did Adam, the first man, make to his words?… **Adam was unwilling
> to tell this to Eve exactly as the Holy One, blessed be He, had commanded
> him**; he therefore made a fence to his words, over and above the words
> which the Holy One, blessed be He, had spoken to him, and this is what he
> said to her, "But of the fruit of the tree which is in the midst of the
> garden, God hath said: Ye shall not eat of it, **neither shall ye touch it**,
> lest ye die."… At that moment the wicked serpent reasoned to himself
> saying, "Since I cannot bring about the fall of Adam, I will bring about the
> fall of Eve."… He said, "**If you think it is the touching of the tree that
> the Holy One, blessed be He, has forbidden us, behold I will touch it and
> not die**; so you, too, will not die if you touch it."… **Eve then began to
> think to herself, "All that my master has commanded me from the beginning is
> false"**… Now what brought about the act of touching? **The answer is the
> fence which Adam had made to his words.** Hence the Sages said: **When a man
> makes a fence to his words, he is not always able to stand by his words.**
> They further said: **Hence let not a man add to the words which he has
> heard.** Rabbi Jose said: **A firm wall of ten hand-breadths is better than
> a tottering wall of a hundred cubits.**

*Core material tannaitic (1st–3rd century); the document's redaction is
debated.*

Eve's conclusion is the sentence to keep: not *the fence was wrong* but
**"all of it is false."** An unmarked fence takes the commandment down with it
when it falls.

The same chapter gives the serpent a motive of pure ambition — *"I will go,
I will slay Adam and marry his wife; then I will be king over the whole world.
I shall also walk erect and eat all the dainties of the world"* — and then
reads the three curses of Genesis 3:14–15 as three exact answers to those
three ambitions.

---

### Babylonian Talmud, *Sanhedrin* 29a → *toward the session's reading*

The same insight compressed into a maxim, and turned into a general rule of
interpretation:

> **Ḥizkiyya** says: From where is it derived that **anyone who adds,
> subtracts**? It is derived from a verse, as it is stated that Eve said:
> "God has said: You shall not eat of it, neither shall you touch it"
> (Genesis 3:3), whereas God had actually rendered prohibited only eating from
> the tree but not touching it… **Because Eve added that there was a
> prohibition against touching the tree, the snake showed her that touching it
> does not cause her to die, and she consequently sinned by eating from it as
> well.**

*Ḥizkiyya: 3rd century.*

---

## 3. The pitch: slander, and the hurry

### Genesis Rabbah 19:4 → *toward the session's reading — the FOMO passage*

> **Rabbi Yehoshua of Sikhnin said in the name of Rabbi Levi**: It began to
> **slander its Creator**. It said: 'He ate from this tree and then created
> the world, so He says to you: Do not eat from it **so you will not create
> other worlds**. For **every person hates the craftsman that competes with
> him**.'
>
> **Rabbi Yehuda ben Rabbi Simon** said: The serpent said: 'Everything that
> was created after its counterpart dominates that counterpart… Man was
> created after everything in order to dominate everything. **Hurry and eat
> the fruit now, before He creates additional worlds and they will dominate
> you.**' That is what is written: "The woman saw that the tree was good…"
> (Genesis 3:6) – **she saw the arguments of the serpent**.

*Rabbi Levi: 3rd century. Rabbi Yehoshua of Sikhnin and Rabbi Yehuda ben Rabbi
Simon: 4th century.*

Two moves, and Session 5 is built on both. **First**, the accusation is not
that the command is wrong but that **the commander has an interest** — God as
a competing craftsman guarding his trade. **Second**, *"hurry and eat now,
before"* is a manufactured **closing window**. A fifth-century document
describing, exactly, the fear of missing out.

And the closing line is a piece of very sharp reading: what she "saw" in 3:6
was not a tree. **It was his argument.**

---

### Genesis Rabbah 19:5 → *toward the session's reading*

> **Rabbi Yosei bar Zimra** said: **Three matters were stated regarding that
> tree**: that it was good for eating, pleasing to the eye, and suited for
> increasing wisdom – and the three of them were stated in one verse.

*Rabbi Yosei bar Zimra: 3rd century.*

The rabbis counted the three attractions of 3:6 — Session 3's problem 8 —
seventeen centuries before we did.

The passage continues into territory Genesis leaves blank: **Rabbi Simlai**
(3rd century) supplies the speech Eve gives Adam, arguing that if she dies
alone God will simply make another Eve — *"there is nothing new under the
sun."* Genesis records no such speech. The midrash is filling a silence, and
knows it.

---

## 4. Opened eyes, and what they saw

### Genesis Rabbah 19:6 → *toward the session's reading*

> "The eyes of both of them were opened" – **Had they been blind?** … This is
> analogous to a **villager who was passing by a glazier's shop** which had a
> basket filled with goblets and cut glass in front of it, and he grabbed them
> with his cane and shattered them. He got up and grabbed him. He said to him:
> 'I know that I will not be able to get any benefit out of you, **but come
> with me and I will show you how much goodness you have destroyed**.' So,
> too, He showed them how many generations they had destroyed.
>
> "They knew that they were naked" – **they had been stripped bare of even
> that one commandment that they had received.**
>
> "They sewed fig leaves" – **Rabbi Shimon ben Yoḥai** said: It is the leaf
> that brought **grief** to the world.

*Rabbi Yoḥanan ben Zakkai: 1st century. Rabbi Akiva and Rabbi Shimon ben
Yoḥai: 2nd century. Rabbi Yudan and Rabbi Berekhya, who transmit the parable:
4th century.*

The glazier parable answers the anticlimax of 3:7 (Session 3's problem 11):
the opened eyes are not a revelation of the cosmos but a tour of the damage.
And *"stripped bare of even that one commandment"* is the earliest rabbinic
form of the idea the [Second Temple sheet](second-temple-sources.md) states
outright: what they lost when they were "naked" was a **covering** they had
not known they were wearing.

The fig-leaf pun — *teʾenah*, fig, against *toʾanah*, grief — is untranslatable
and entirely characteristic.

---

### Genesis Rabbah 19:9 → *toward the session's reading*

> "The Lord God called to the man and said to him: **Where are you** *[ayeka]*?"
> – **how** *[eikh]* **have you become like this?** Yesterday you were loyal
> to My will, but now to the will of the serpent. Yesterday you extended from
> one end of the world to the other end, but now you fit "among the trees of
> the garden"?…
>
> [God speaking:] I put him into the Garden of Eden and commanded him, but he
> violated the command, and I sentenced him to banishment and expulsion, and
> **I lamented over him: 'How could it be [eikha]?'**… So it was with His
> descendants: I brought them into the Land of Israel, I commanded them and
> they violated the command, I sentenced them to banishment and expulsion, and
> **I lamented over them: Eikha**, as it is stated: "**How [eikha] does…it
> sit**" (Lamentations 1:1).

*Rabbi Abahu, in the name of Rabbi Ḥanina: 3rd–4th century.*

The consonants of *ʾayyekkāh*, "where are you," are the consonants of
*ʾêkāh*, "how…?" — the first word of the book of **Lamentations**, the book
Jews read on the night the Temple burned. On this reading God's first question
after the fall is not a search but a **funeral lament**, and it is the same
lament he will sing over the exile of his people. Adam's expulsion and
Israel's exile are told as one story.

---

### Genesis Rabbah 19:11 → *toward the session's reading*

> "The man said: The woman whom You gave to be with me…" – there are four
> people whom the Holy One blessed be He **tapped on their jug** and found
> that they were **jugs filled with urine**. They are: Adam, Cain, Bilam, and
> Hezekiah… In all these cases, the people should have realized that **God's
> question was actually an implied accusation, and they should have
> immediately admitted their guilt**, rather than attempt to actually answer
> the question. But Ezekiel, He found him more perceptive than all of them,
> as it is stated: "Son of man, will these bones come to life? And I said:
> Lord God, **You know**" (Ezekiel 37:3).

You tap a jug to hear whether anything is inside. God's questions in Genesis 3
are knocks, not requests for information — and the four men who answer them
literally all ring hollow.

---

## 5. The clothing

### Genesis Rabbah 20:12 → *toward the session's reading*

> "The Lord God made for Adam and for his wife **garments of hide** *[or,
> with an ayin]*, and clothed them" (Genesis 3:21). **In Rabbi Meir's Torah,
> they found that "garments of *or*" was written** *[with an alef, meaning
> **light**]*… **Rabbi Shimon ben Lakish** said: **Radiant hides.** And **the
> firstborn sons would use them** *[for religious rites, which was their
> purview, until they were replaced by the Levites]*.
>
> **Rabbi Levi** said: The Torah teaches you proper conduct: Eat according to
> your means, wear clothing that is less than your means… Eat according to
> your means – "**From every tree of the garden you may eat**" (Genesis 2:16).

*Rabbi Meir: 2nd century. Reish Lakish (Shimon ben Lakish) and Rabbi Levi:
3rd century.*

**Garments of light.** In Hebrew, *ʿôr* (hide) and *ʾôr* (light) differ by a
single letter, and the tradition preserved a report that **Rabbi Meir's own
Torah scroll had the other one**. Whatever the textual history, the reading
tells you what the second century thought had been lost.

And Reish Lakish's addition is the one that matters most for Session 5: the
garments God made were **used for priestly service**. That is the midrash
arriving independently at what the Hebrew of 3:21 already suggests — the word
God uses for what he makes them, *kuttōneṯ*, is the word for the **priest's
tunic** (Exodus 28:39–40).

Note the small grace in Rabbi Levi's closing comment, too: he quotes God's
generosity in 2:16 as the model for how a person should eat.

---

### Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Genesis 3:7 and 3:21 → *toward the session's reading (but late)*

An Aramaic paraphrase read alongside the Hebrew in synagogue. Its final form
is late — 7th–8th century or after — so treat it as tradition of uncertain
age, not early evidence. Both verses are transformed:

> And the eyes of both were enlightened, and they knew that they were naked,
> **divested of the purple robe in which they had been created**. And they saw
> the sight of their shame, and sewed to themselves the leaves of figs, and
> made to them cinctures.
> — Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Genesis 3:7

> And the Lord God made to Adam and to his wife **vestures of honour from the
> skin of the serpent, which he had cast from him**, upon the skin of their
> flesh, instead of that adornment which had been cast away; and He clothed
> them.
> — Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Genesis 3:21

The second is the most arresting image in the whole tradition. **The tempter's
own shed skin becomes the covering of the people he shamed** — and the phrase
*"instead of that adornment which had been cast away"* makes the exchange
explicit: one lost garment for another given.

## 6. The snake as the *yetzer ha-raʿ* — the enemy you carry

The rabbis largely declined to identify the snake with Satan. They did
something more uncomfortable instead.

### Babylonian Talmud, *Bava Batra* 16a ⇢ *a different direction, and the most important one*

> **Reish Lakish** says: **Satan, the evil inclination, and the Angel of Death
> are one**, that is, they are three aspects of the same essence. He is the
> Satan who seduces people and then accuses them… He is also the evil
> inclination, as it is written: "The impulse of the thoughts of his heart was
> only evil continuously" (Genesis 6:5)… He is also the Angel of Death.

*Reish Lakish: 3rd century.*

**Seduces, then accuses.** That is the shape of Session 5's whole argument
about the snake — the same voice sells you the fruit and then hands you the
mirror — stated in one clause by a third-century rabbi.

---

### Babylonian Talmud, *Shabbat* 105b → *toward the session's reading*

> …that is **the craft of the evil inclination**. **Today it tells him do
> this, and tomorrow it tells him do that**, until eventually, when he no
> longer controls himself, it tells him worship idols and he goes and worships
> idols. **Rabbi Avin** said: What verse alludes to this? "There shall not be
> a strange god within you" (Psalms 81:10). **What is the strange god that is
> within a person's body? Say that it is the evil inclination.**

*Rabbi Avin: 4th century.*

---

### Babylonian Talmud, *Sukkah* 52a → *toward the session's reading*

> **Rav Asi** said: Initially, when it begins to entice someone, the evil
> inclination is **like a strand of a spider's web**; and ultimately it is
> **like the thick ropes of a wagon**, as it is stated: "Woe unto them that
> draw iniquity with cords of vanity, and sin as if it were with a wagon rope"
> (Isaiah 5:18).

*Rav Asi: 3rd century.*

---

### And one from Session 4's sheet, which belongs here too

*Genesis Rabbah* 9:7 reads *"behold, it was **very** good"* as a verdict on
the **evil inclination** — because without it *"a man would never build a
house, would never marry a wife, would never beget children, and would never
engage in commerce."* The tradition is not squeamish about desire. It thinks
desire is load-bearing. What Genesis 3 shows is desire redirected, not desire
condemned.

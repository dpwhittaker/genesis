# The earliest readings of Genesis 3

A source sheet for **Session 5**. Everything here was written **before the
rabbinic literature** — between roughly **180 BCE and 100 CE** — which makes
these the oldest interpretations of the garden story that exist in any
language. Several of them are older than the New Testament. Full provenance
and licensing at the foot of the page.

Two things to keep in mind as you read.

**These are not commentaries.** Nobody in this period wrote verse-by-verse
notes on Genesis. They **retold** it — and the retellings show you what the
first readers thought the story meant, because every addition, omission and
smoothing is an interpretation with the seams showing.

**Early does not mean right.** The oldest reading on this sheet (Ben Sira) is
the one that departs furthest from what Genesis actually says. Age is
evidence about what people *thought*, not a guarantee that they were correct.

Each entry is dated, and marked **→ toward the session's reading** or
**⇢ a different direction**.

---

## 1. Ben Sira 25:24 — *c. 180 BCE* ⇢ *a different direction*

The oldest surviving interpretation of Genesis 3. Written in Hebrew in
Jerusalem by Yeshua ben Eleazar ben Sira, roughly two centuries before the
New Testament, and translated into Greek by his grandson.

> From the woman came the beginning of sin, and by her we all die.

**This is where that idea comes from — and it is not from Genesis.** The text
of Genesis has the man standing beside her for the whole conversation
(*"she also gave some of it to her husband **who was with her**,"* 3:6 — Hebrew
*ʿimmāh*), has God address the man first (3:9), and never assigns the blame
this way. Paul, writing in a world where Ben Sira was widely read, puts it on
**Adam** (Romans 5:12–19).

Note also the company the verse keeps. Ben Sira 25 is a chapter of sustained
invective against difficult wives; verse 24 arrives as the theological
capstone of an argument that began as domestic complaint. That context is
part of the evidence.

## 2. Jubilees 3 — *c. 160–150 BCE* → *toward the session's reading*

A Hebrew retelling of Genesis and Exodus, found among the Dead Sea Scrolls in
more copies than most books of the Bible. It survives complete only in
Ge'ez (Ethiopic).

### Eden is a sanctuary

Jubilees explains the purity laws of Leviticus 12 — how long a woman is
excluded from the sanctuary after childbirth — by grounding them in **when
Adam and Eve entered the garden**:

> And after Adam had completed forty days in the land where he had been
> created, we brought him into the Garden of Eden to till and keep it, but
> his wife they brought in on the eightieth day, and after this she entered
> into the Garden of Eden. And for this reason the commandment is written on
> the heavenly tables in regard to her that giveth birth: "if she beareth a
> male, she shall remain in her uncleanness seven days… and thirty and three
> days shall she remain in the blood of her purifying, and **she shall not
> touch any hallowed thing, nor enter into the sanctuary**, until she
> accomplisheth these days…"
> — Jubilees 3:13–16

> And when she had completed these eighty days we brought her into the Garden
> of Eden, **for it is holier than all the earth besides, and every tree that
> is planted in it is holy**.
> — Jubilees 3:19

The argument only works if **Eden is the sanctuary**. This is the earliest
datable Jewish statement of the idea Session 4 built on and Session 5 walks
into.

### All the animals could speak

> And on that day was **closed the mouth of all beasts**, and of cattle, and
> of birds, and of whatever walketh, and of whatever moveth, **so that they
> could no longer speak: for they had all spoken one with another with one
> lip and with one tongue**.
> — Jubilees 3:28

The oldest answer on record to Session 3's first problem — *why is nobody
surprised that the snake talks?* Whether or not it is what Genesis means, it
tells you the first readers did not find the talking strange. They found the
**silence afterwards** the thing that needed explaining.

### Nakedness, and the covering as law

> And he tilled the garden, and **was naked and knew it not, and was not
> ashamed**.
> — Jubilees 3:24

> And to Adam alone did He give the wherewithal to **cover his shame**, of all
> the beasts and cattle. On this account, it is prescribed on the heavenly
> tables as touching all those who know the judgment of the law, that they
> should cover their shame, and should not uncover themselves as the Gentiles
> uncover themselves.
> — Jubilees 3:44–45

For Jubilees, God's act of clothing in 3:21 is not merely mercy — it is the
**founding of a commandment**. Also worth noting: on the day he leaves the
garden, Adam **offers incense** (3:41), which is a priest's act.

## 3. Wisdom of Solomon 2:23–24 — *1st century BCE* ⇢ *a different direction (and an important one)*

A Greek work from Alexandria. This is, arguably, the earliest text to
identify the tempter of Eden with a personal adversary:

> For God created man incorruptible, and to the image of his own likeness he
> made him. But **by the envy of the devil, death came into the world**.

Two things at once. It confirms the identification the New Testament will
state outright (Revelation 12:9), and it shows that the identification is an
**interpretation added later**, not something Genesis says. Genesis calls the
snake *"one of the wild animals that the LORD God had made."* The Greek word
here, *diabolos*, can mean "the devil" or simply "the slanderer" — which is
close to what the midrash will later say the snake was doing (see the
[rabbinic sheet](rabbinic-sources.md), *Genesis Rabbah* 19:4).

## 4. Josephus, *Antiquities* 1.1.4 §§40–51 — *c. 93 CE* → *mixed*

The Jewish historian, writing in Rome a generation after the Temple was
destroyed, retells Genesis 3 for a Greek-speaking audience.

> God therefore commanded that Adam and his wife should eat of all the rest
> of the plants, but to abstain from the tree of knowledge; and foretold to
> them, that if they touched it, it would prove their destruction. But
> **while all the living creatures had one language**, at that time the
> serpent, which then lived together with Adam and his wife, **shewed an
> envious disposition**, at his supposal of their living happily, and in
> obedience to the commands of God; and imagining, that when they disobeyed
> them, they would fall into calamities, he persuaded the woman, out of a
> malicious intention, to taste of the tree of knowledge, telling them, that
> in that tree was the knowledge of good and evil; which knowledge, when they
> should obtain, they would lead a happy life; nay, a life not inferior to
> that of a god…

> Upon this they perceived that they were become naked to one another; and
> **being ashamed thus to appear abroad**, they invented somewhat to cover
> them; for the tree sharpened their understanding; and they covered
> themselves with fig-leaves; and tying these before them, out of modesty,
> **they thought they were happier than they were before, as they had
> discovered what they were in want of**.

> But when God came into the garden, Adam, **who was wont before to come and
> converse with him**, being conscious of his wicked behavior, **went out of
> the way**… [God said:] "…for **thy silence is not the sign of thy virtue,
> but of thy evil conscience**."

> He also **deprived the serpent of speech**, out of indignation at his
> malicious disposition towards Adam. Besides this, he inserted poison under
> his tongue, and made him an enemy to men… And when he had **deprived him of
> the use of his feet**, he made him to go rolling all along, and dragging
> himself upon the ground.

Four notes. **(1)** Josephus independently gives the same explanation as
Jubilees for the unremarked talking snake: all animals spoke. **(2)** He keeps
the snake an **animal** — no devil — but assigns the motive Wisdom assigns to
the devil: **envy**. **(3)** His line about the fig leaves is a piece of
psychological observation two millennia ahead of its time: their gain was the
discovery of **what they lacked**. **(4)** He notices that Adam's silence,
not his answer, is the problem — which is exactly what Session 5 argues about
the difference between guilt and shame. Josephus also answers Session 3's
problem 3 (how the snake got around before the curse) the same way *Genesis
Rabbah* will: it had feet.

## 5. Apocalypse of Moses 20–21 — *1st century CE* → *toward the session's reading*

Part of the Jewish work known in Latin as the *Life of Adam and Eve*, this is
Eve's own retelling to her children. It is the clearest surviving statement of
the idea that the humans were **clothed in glory** before the fall.

> And in that very hour **my eyes were opened**, and forthwith I knew that I
> was **bare of the righteousness with which I had been clothed**, and I wept
> and said to him: "Why hast thou done this to me in that thou hast
> **deprived me of the glory with which I was clothed**?"… And I began to
> seek, in my nakedness, in my part for leaves to hide my shame, but I found
> none, for, as soon as I had eaten, the leaves showered down from all the
> trees in my part, **except the fig tree only**.
> — Apocalypse of Moses 20:1–5

> And your father answered and said, "I fear lest God be wroth with me." And
> I said to him, "Fear not, for as soon as thou hast eaten thou shalt know
> good and evil." And speedily I persuaded him, and he ate and straightway
> **his eyes were opened and he too knew his nakedness**. And to me he saith,
> "O wicked woman! what have I done to thee that thou hast **deprived me of
> the glory of God**?"
> — Apocalypse of Moses 21:4–6

Note that the blame-shift of Genesis 3:12 survives into the retelling, and
gets sharper. Note also the detail about the fig tree — the author has noticed
that the leaves they sewed came from the only tree that would still give them
any, and has built a small tragedy out of it.

## 6. Philo of Alexandria, *On the Creation* 157–165 — *c. 20 BCE – 50 CE* ⇢ *a different direction*

*Described here rather than quoted.* Philo — a Greek-speaking Jewish
philosopher who was a contemporary of Jesus — reads the whole garden story as
an allegory of the human interior: the serpent is **pleasure**, the woman is
**sense-perception**, and the man is **mind**. Pleasure approaches the senses,
the senses persuade the mind, and the mind falls. He argues the serpent fits
the role because it goes on its belly, feeds on earth, and carries poison in
its teeth.

This is not the reading Session 5 takes, and its Greek framework (a soul at
war with a body) is not Genesis's. But it is worth knowing that a first-century
Jew, writing in the lifetime of Jesus, already assumed **the snake was a
picture of something inside a person** — the same instinct the rabbis would
later give a Hebrew name, *yetzer ha-raʿ*.

  ours, and ellipses mark omissions.

  domain**. English: **William Whiston**, 1737 — **public domain** — via

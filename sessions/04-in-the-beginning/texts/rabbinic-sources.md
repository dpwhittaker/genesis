# The rabbis on Genesis 1

A source sheet for **Session 4**. Everything here is quoted from the Jewish
interpretive tradition: the **Mishnah** (c. 200 CE), the **Talmud** (c. 500 CE),
**Genesis Rabbah** and its sister midrashim (c. 400–600 CE), and **Rashi**
(1040–1105). Full provenance and licensing at the foot of the page.

Two things are worth saying before you read them.

**First, these are close readers, not casual ones.** Nearly every passage below
begins by noticing a *bump* — exactly the kind of bump we hunted in Session 3.
"Why is 'it was good' not written on the second day?" "'God completed on the
seventh day'? This is bewildering." The rabbis are not smoothing the text. They
are pressing on it.

**Second, they do not always go where we are going.** Session 4 reads Genesis 1
as the founding of a cosmic temple. Some of these passages point straight at that
reading; some of them take the same bump and walk off in a completely different
direction. Both are useful. Where the tradition confirms us, it is a two-thousand-year-old
witness that we are not inventing things. Where it diverges, it is a reminder that
a good text supports more than one true reading — and that our reading is a
reading, not the last word.

Each entry below is marked **→ toward temple cosmology** or **⇢ a different
direction**, so you can see at a glance which is which.

---

## 1. Creation as building — the architect's plans

### Genesis Rabbah 1:1 → *toward temple cosmology*

> Another matter, *amon* means artisan [*uman*]. The Torah is saying: 'I was the
> tool of craft of the Holy One blessed be He.' The way of the world is that when
> a flesh-and-blood king builds a palace he does not build it based on his own
> knowledge, but rather based on the knowledge of an artisan. And the artisan does
> not build it based on his own knowledge, but rather, he has [plans on] sheets and
> tablets by which to ascertain how he should build its rooms, how he should build
> its doors. So too, the Holy One blessed be He looked in the Torah and created the
> world.

The very first thing Genesis Rabbah says about the very first verse of the Bible
is that **creation is a building project.** Not a birth, not a battle, not an
emanation — a king commissioning a **palace**, with an architect, with drawings,
with rooms and doors specified in advance. The rabbis reached for that image
because it is what the text's own vocabulary suggested to them.

---

### Genesis Rabbah 1:15 → *toward temple cosmology — and an early one*

> Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel, Beit Shammai say: The heavens were created first and the earth was created thereafter. Beit Hillel says: The earth was created first and the heavens thereafter. These cite a reason for their position and those cite a reason for their position. According to the opinion of Beit Shammai, who say that the heavens were created first and the earth thereafter, **it is analogous to a king who had a throne made, and once that was made he had its footstool made.** So, the Holy One blessed be He said: "The heavens are My throne and the earth is My footstool…" (Isaiah 66:1). According to the opinion of Beit Hillel, who say that the earth was created first and the heavens thereafter, **it is analogous to a king who built a palace; first he built the lower sections and then he built the upper sections**, so, "on the day that the Lord God made earth and the heavens" (Genesis 2:4). Rabbi Yehuda bar Ilai said: This verse, too, supports Beit Hillel: "You laid the foundations of earth in times past" (Psalms 102:26), and then, "the heavens are the work of Your hands" (Psalms 102:26). Rabbi Ḥanin said: From the same place where Scripture supports Beit Shammai, from there Beit Hillel refute them: **"The earth was" (Genesis 1:2) – it already was.** Rabbi Yoḥanan said in the name of the Sages: In terms of Creation, the heavens were first, but in terms of completion, the earth was first.

This dispute is attributed to the two schools of the generation *before Jesus* — Hillel and Shammai taught roughly 30 BCE to 10 CE — which, if the attribution holds, would make it the earliest reading of Genesis 1 on this sheet that we can attach to named people. Treat that as a claim about the *tradition*, not a dated document: see the caution at the end of this entry.

And look at what the two houses reach for. To settle the meaning of the first verse of the Bible, Beit Shammai pictures **a throne with its footstool** and Beit Hillel pictures **a palace going up storey by storey**. Not biology, not chronology as such — *royal architecture.* The building instinct that this session traces through Genesis 1 is not a late rabbinic overlay and certainly not a modern one; it is the native idiom of the earliest readers we can name. And Beit Shammai's proof text is **Isaiah 66:1** — the same verse Session 4 uses for rest-as-residence.

Two more things worth marking. **The dispute is generated by the seam between the two creation accounts** — Session 3's problem 4. Beit Shammai reads "the heavens and the earth" (1:1); Beit Hillel reads "earth and the heavens" (2:4). What we are trained to see as an embarrassment is the engine of the argument. And Rabbi Ḥanin presses on problem 8 in the same breath: *"'The earth was' — it already was."*

**How much weight can it bear?** Less than it is often asked to. The passage reaches us in a document compiled centuries after the men it names, so what we have is an **attribution**, not a transcript. Two considerations support it. The dispute is preserved a second time, in different words and with different proof texts, in the Talmud (*Chagigah* 12a, quoted below) as an early tannaitic teaching — so it is not one collection's private memory. And it **has no polemical motive**: nobody scores a point against anyone by arguing whether heaven or earth came first, and content with nothing to win is the kind most likely to be genuinely old.

Against that, attributions to the two houses are precisely the material that historians of rabbinic literature treat most cautiously, and no "who would have invented this?" test can carry a firm date by itself. So do not say *the oldest named dispute about Genesis 1 in Jewish literature*, and do not present it as evidence of what was thought before 70 CE. Say what it is: a very old tradition, in the idiom of building, from the earliest readers of Genesis 1 we can put names to.

## 2. Where did the chaos come from?

### Genesis Rabbah 1:9 ⇢ *a different direction*

> A certain philosopher once asked Rabban Gamliel, saying to him: 'Your God is a
> great artist, however he found many excellent raw materials that helped him:
> Emptiness, disorder, darkness, wind, water, and depths.' He said to him: 'May the
> spirit of that man depart from him; regarding all of them, creation is written.'

The philosopher has read Genesis 1:2 carefully and asked the obvious question:
if the earth was already *tohu va-vohu* when the story opens, God did not start
from nothing — he started from *stuff*. Rabban Gamliel's answer is metaphysical:
he goes verse-hunting elsewhere in Scripture to show that the raw materials were
themselves created.

Note what has happened. The rabbis take a real bump in the text and answer it with
a **doctrine of origins** — creation out of nothing. That is a genuine question and
a defensible answer, and it later became mainstream in both Judaism and
Christianity. But it is *not* the direction temple cosmology goes. We will ask a
different question of the same verse: not *where did the chaos come from?* but
*what is the chaos doing in the story?* Both readings can be true. They are simply
answering different questions, and it is worth being honest that ours is the less
traditional one.

## 3. Light before the sun

### Genesis Rabbah 3:6 ⇢ *a different direction*

> It is taught: The light that was created during the six days of Creation cannot
> illuminate by day, because it would make the orb of the sun seem dim [by
> comparison], nor can it [illuminate] at night, as it was created to illuminate
> only during the day. Where is it, then? It was stored away, and is designated for
> the righteous for the future…
>
> Rabbi Yehuda bar Rabbi Simon said: He set it aside… for Himself. The Rabbis say:
> He set it aside for the righteous in the World to Come. This is analogous to a
> king who had a fine portion of food and set it aside for his son.

Session 3, problem 16: *days and plants before the sun.* The rabbis saw it long
before we did — and their solution is one of the most beautiful things in the
midrash. The day-one light is **not** the sun's light. It is a primordial light
too bright for this world, **hidden away** (*or ha-ganuz*) and reserved for the
righteous in the age to come.

This goes a different direction from ours, and the difference is instructive. We
read the same gap **functionally**: day one produces time, and the lamps are
fixtures hung into it later. The rabbis read it **eschatologically**: the light
comes first because it is *saved up* — creation's opening line is already a
promise about the end. Their reading goes *forward in time*; ours goes *inward,
into the building*. Neither cancels the other, and honestly, theirs is the more
moving.

### Bavli Chagigah 12a ⇢ *a different direction — the other half of the hidden light*

> The light that the Holy One, Blessed be He, created on the first day was not that
> of the sun but a different kind of light, through which man could observe from one
> end of the world to the other. But when the Holy One, Blessed be He, **looked upon
> the generation of the Flood and the generation of the Dispersion and saw that their
> ways were corrupt** and that they might misuse this light for evil, **He arose and
> concealed it from them**, as it is stated: "And from the wicked their light is
> withheld" (Job 38:15).
>
> And for whom did He conceal it? **For the righteous people in the future**, as it is
> stated: "And God saw the light, that it was good" (Genesis 1:4), and "good" is
> referring to none other than the righteous…
>
> When the light saw that it had been concealed for the righteous, **it rejoiced**, as
> it is stated: "The light for the righteous shall rejoice" (Proverbs 13:9).

Genesis Rabbah 3:6 gives the consoling half of this tradition — the light is
*stored up for the righteous*. The Talmud gives the whole of it, and the whole of
it has a hard edge. The light is hidden **because God saw what people would do with
it**. It is not only a gift held in trust; it is a gift **withheld**, and Job 38:15
is quoted to say so plainly.

Read together, the two halves are one act seen from opposite ends: light kept *for*
someone is light kept *from* someone. If you quote the beautiful half in a class,
quote this one too. It is the harder reading, it is the better attested one, and
the tradition itself does not soften it — except for the last line, which is the
only consolation offered and is given to the light rather than to us.

**Also in this passage:** the Talmud's own version of the Beit Shammai / Beit
Hillel dispute about whether heaven or earth was created first, with different
proof texts (Amos 9:6 for Hillel, Isaiah 66:1 for Shammai) — a second, independent
attestation of the tradition quoted at §1 above. And note what the page as a whole
is doing: this is the Gemara on *Mishnah Chagigah 2:1*, the mishnah that restricts
teaching the work of creation — and it expounds the work of creation for several
pages. See §9.

### Rashi on Genesis 1:1 → *toward temple cosmology*

> **בראשית ברא IN THE BEGINNING GOD CREATED** — … If, however, you wish to explain
> it in its plain sense, explain it thus: At the beginning of the Creation of heaven
> and earth when the earth was without form and void and there was darkness, God
> said, "Let there be light." **The text does not intend to point out the order of
> the acts of Creation** — to state that these (heaven and earth) were created
> first; for if it intended to point this out, it should have written… "At first God
> created etc."

The single most influential Jewish commentator on the Torah, writing in eleventh-century
France, says of Genesis 1:1 in its *plain sense* (*peshat*, the level of reading
Rashi is most famous for defending): **the text is not telling you the order in
which things were made.** If someone in your group is worried that reading Genesis 1
for something other than sequence is a modern liberty, this is the answer. It is
older than the printing press.

## 4. The second day gets no "good"

### Genesis Rabbah 4:6 → *toward temple cosmology (partly)*

> Why is "that it was good" not written regarding the second day? Rabbi Yoḥanan
> taught it in the name of Rabbi Yosei ben Rabbi Ḥalafta: It is because Gehenna
> was created on it…
>
> **Rabbi Ḥanina says: It is because division was created, as it is stated: "And
> let it divide between water and water."** Rabbi Tavyomei said: If on account of
> division that is for the improvement of the world and its settlement, there is no
> "that it was good," division that causes turmoil in the world, all the more so.
>
> Rabbi Shmuel [bar Naḥman] said: **It is because the work of creating water was
> not completed.**

Session 3, problem 14. Three rabbis, three answers, no attempt to pick a winner —
which is itself a lesson in how this literature works. Two of the three are
close cousins of the reading we will take: **something on day two is divided but
not finished**, and the missing verdict marks the unfinished thing. Rabbi Shmuel's
"the work of the water was not completed" is a hair's breadth from ours.

### Genesis Rabbah 5:4 → *toward temple cosmology*

> Rabbi Berekhya said: **The lower waters separated from the upper only with
> weeping** [*bekhiya*]. That is what is written: "He dams the depths of [*mibekhi*]
> the rivers" (Job 28:11).

The waters below **wept** at being cut off from the waters above. This is a
midrashic pun (*bekhi*, "weeping," heard inside a word for the damming of rivers),
and it is also a theological claim of real weight: the separation that makes the
world habitable is experienced, from below, as a **loss of nearness to God**.

### Rashi on Leviticus 2:13 → *toward temple cosmology*

> **מלח ברית THE SALT OF THE COVENANT** … because a covenant was established with
> the salt as far back as the six days of Creation when **the lower waters (those of
> the oceans) received an assurance that they would be offered on the altar** in the
> form of salt and also as water in the ceremony of "the libation of water" on the
> Feast of Tabernacles.

And here is the payoff — the tradition's own answer to the weeping waters, and the
single most striking piece of temple cosmology in this whole source sheet. The
lower waters were consoled with a **promise**: every grain offering in the Temple
would be salted with them, and once a year, at Sukkot, they would be carried up and
poured out on the altar.

Read that again. **The rift opened in the cosmos on day two is healed in the
Temple.** The rabbis are saying, in their own idiom, exactly what this session is
about: the sanctuary is where the world is put back together.

## 5. "Let us make humankind"

### Genesis Rabbah 8:8 ⇢ *a different direction — and a remarkable admission*

> Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥman said in the name of Rabbi Yonatan: When Moses was writing
> the Torah… when he reached this verse: "And God said: Let us make Man in our image,
> in our likeness" – he said before Him: 'Master of the universe, why do you provide
> the heretics with basis for a claim? This is bewildering.' He said to him: **'Just
> write, and if someone wants to err, let him err.'**

This may be the most extraordinary text on the sheet. The rabbis notice that "let
us make" is dangerous — it can be read as more than one divine speaker, which is
precisely how Christians read it — and they imagine Moses *objecting to God about
it while taking dictation.* And God's answer is not "you have misunderstood." God's
answer is: **write it anyway.**

Whatever else that is, it is a frank acknowledgment from inside the tradition that
the ambiguity in the verse is **deliberate** — that the Author left it there on
purpose, knowing exactly what it would provoke. That is Session 3's whole thesis,
stated by a fifth-century midrash.

The passage then gives the rabbis' own reading: God consulted **the ministering
angels**, modeling humility for the powerful. That is not where Christian readers
go, and this session will not pretend otherwise.

### Genesis Rabbah 8:9 ⇢ *a different direction*

> The heretics once asked Rabbi Samlai: 'How many deities created the world?'…
> They asked him further: 'What is this that is written: "Let us make Man in our
> image, in our likeness"?' He said to them: 'Read what is written thereafter:
> "*God created* [*vayivre'u*] man in their image" is not stated, but rather,
> "**God created** [*vayivra*] man in **His** image."'…
>
> Rabbi Samlai said [moreover]: **Everywhere you find a basis for a claim of the
> heretics, you find a refutation alongside it.**

The grammatical answer: verse 26 has a plural *"let us,"* but verse 27 immediately
answers with three singular verbs. Rabbi Samlai's closing rule is worth carrying
around — *wherever the text opens a door, it also supplies the answer next to it* —
and it is a rabbinic version of the same instinct that makes us read bumps as
designed rather than accidental.

Note honestly: the tradition here is arguing **against** the reading most Christians
have been taught. We do not have to settle that today. What both readings agree on
is that verse 26 is doing something odd on purpose.

### Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 ⇢ *a different direction (and a magnificent one)*

> Therefore but a single person was created in the world, to teach that if any man
> has caused a single life to perish from Israel, he is deemed by Scripture as if he
> had caused a whole world to perish; and anyone who saves a single soul from Israel,
> he is deemed by Scripture as if he had saved a whole world… **for humans stamp many
> coins with one seal and they are all like one another; but the King of kings, the
> Holy Blessed One, has stamped every human with the seal of the first man, yet not
> one of them are like another. Therefore everyone must say, "For my sake was the
> world created."**

Session 4 reads *image* through the ancient world's **cult statue** and **royal
image** — a figure installed in a temple to represent its owner. The Mishnah takes
the same word somewhere else entirely: to the **worth of the individual**, and to a
courtroom instruction given to witnesses in a capital case. Same word, same verse,
and a completely different — and completely indispensable — payoff.

Keep both. The temple reading tells you *what humanity is for*; Sanhedrin tells you
*what a human being is worth.* A study that gets the first and misses the second has
gone wrong.

## 6. "Very good"

### Genesis Rabbah 9:5 and 9:7 ⇢ *a different direction*

> In Rabbi Meir's Torah they found written: "And, behold, it was very [*me'od*]
> good" – and, behold, **death [*mot*] is good.**
> — Genesis Rabbah 9:5

> "Behold it was very good" – this is the good inclination; "and behold it was
> **very** good" – this is the **evil inclination**. Is the evil inclination, then,
> very good?… Were it not for the evil inclination, a man would never build a house,
> would never marry a wife, would never beget children, and would never engage in
> commerce.
> — Genesis Rabbah 9:7

Rabbi Meir's Torah scroll — one letter different, *me'od* ("very") read as *mot*
("death") — turns the crown of the creation account into a statement about
mortality. And Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥman hears in the *extra* word "very" an
endorsement of the very drive that makes people grasp.

This is the rabbis at their most daring, and it is a direction we are not taking.
Our reading of "good" is a builder's verdict — *fit for its purpose*. Theirs is
moral and anthropological. Worth noticing: they get there by pressing on exactly
the word we press on, and by treating "very" as significant rather than decorative.

## 7. The seventh day

### Genesis Rabbah 10:9 → *toward temple cosmology*

> Rabbi asked Rabbi Yishmael ben Rabbi Yosei… 'Did you hear from your father what
> is meant by: "**God completed** [*vayechal*] **on the seventh day**"? **This is
> bewildering.**'…
>
> Geniva and the Rabbis, Geniva said: This is analogous to **a king who made himself
> a wedding canopy, painted it and embossed it. What was it lacking? A bride
> [*kalla*] to enter it. So, too, what was the world lacking? Shabbat.** The Rabbis
> say: This is analogous to a king for whom they crafted a signet ring. What was
> lacking? The seal. So, too, what was the world lacking? Shabbat.
>
> This is one of the matters that they changed for King Ptolemy – [they wrote]
> "God completed **on the sixth day**…and He rested on the seventh day."…
>
> What was created on it [Shabbat] after He rested? **Tranquility, satisfaction,
> calm, and quiet.**

Four things in one passage, and every one of them matters for this session.

1. **The rabbis flag the bump and say so out loud.** *"This is bewildering."* If
   God finished on the sixth day, why does 2:2 say he finished *on the seventh*?
2. **The ancient harmonization is recorded, and recorded as a change.** The
   translators who produced the Greek Bible for King Ptolemy quietly wrote
   "sixth" — and the midrash remembers that they did. The Septuagint and the
   Samaritan Pentateuch both read "sixth" at 2:2 to this day. The instinct to
   smooth a bump is not modern; what is instructive is that the rabbis kept the
   rough reading *and* kept the record of the smoothing.
3. **Two royal parables — and both make the seventh day the thing that completes
   the building.** A wedding canopy is finished but empty until the bride enters.
   A signet ring is finished but useless until the seal is cut. *So too, what was
   the world lacking? Shabbat.* Hold that against the cosmic-temple reading: a
   house is not finished when the last stone is laid. It is finished when its
   owner moves in.
4. **Rest is something God *made*.** "What was created on it after He rested?
   Tranquility, satisfaction, calm, and quiet." Not the absence of work — a
   *creature*, the seventh day's own contribution to the inventory.

### Mishnah Tamid 7:4 → *toward temple cosmology*

> The following are the psalms that were chanted in the Temple. 1) On the first day
> they used to say, "The earth is the Lord's and the fullness thereof, the world and
> they that dwell therein" (Psalms 24). 2) On the second day they used to say: "Great
> is the Lord and highly to be praised, in the city of our God. His holy mountain"
> (Psalms 48). 3) On the third day they used to say: "God stands in the congregation
> of God, in the midst of the judges he judges" (Psalms 82). 4) On the fourth day
> they used to say: "O Lord, God to whom vengeance belongs. God to whom vengeance
> belongs, shine forth" (Psalms 94). 5) On the fifth day they used to say: "Sing
> aloud unto God our strength, shout unto the God of Jacob" (Psalms 81). 6) On the
> sixth day they used to say: "The lord reigns, he is clothed in majesty, the Lord is
> clothed, He has girded himself with strength" (Psalms 93). 7) **On Shabbat they
> used to say: "A psalm, a song for the Sabbath day" (Psalms 92). A psalm, a song for
> the time to come, for the day that will be all Shabbat and rest for everlasting
> life.**

This is not commentary. This is **what actually happened in the Temple**, every
day, for centuries: the Levites sang the creation week. Seven days, seven psalms,
in order. The creation account was not a text the priesthood studied — it was the
**liturgical calendar of the building itself.**

And look where the seventh day lands. Psalm 92 is titled "for the Sabbath day," and
the Mishnah glosses it: *a song for the time to come, for the day that will be all
Shabbat.* Session 3, problem 15 — *day seven has no evening and no morning* — and
here is the tradition's answer, sung out loud once a week in the courts of the
Temple. **The seventh day has no closing because it has not closed.**

### Babylonian Talmud, Pesachim 54a → *toward temple cosmology*

> Seven phenomena were created before the world was created, and they are: **Torah,
> and repentance, and the Garden of Eden, and Gehenna, and the Throne of Glory, and
> the Temple, and the name of Messiah.**

A list of seven things that logically precede creation — and three of them are
places in a sanctuary: **the Garden**, **the Throne**, **the Temple**. In the
rabbinic imagination, the Temple is not a late Israelite institution bolted onto
the world. It is prior to the world; the world is built around it.

## 8. The tabernacle *is* the world

These are the two texts this session is built on. They say, flatly and in detail,
what we will spend Part 2 arguing: **the sanctuary Israel built in the wilderness
was a model of the cosmos, and the cosmos was made the way a sanctuary is made.**

### Numbers Rabbah 12:13 → *toward temple cosmology*

> "*Et hamishkan*" – **as it is equal to the rest of the world**… How so?
>
> It is written: "In the beginning, God created [the heavens and the earth]"
> (Genesis 1:1). And it is written: "He spreads the heavens like a sheet" (Psalms
> 104:2). Regarding the Tabernacle it is written: "You shall make sheets of goats'
> hair as a tent over the Tabernacle…" (Exodus 26:7).
>
> On the second, it is written: "Let there be a firmament…and let it divide…"
> (Genesis 1:6). Regarding the Tabernacle it is written: "**The curtain shall divide
> for you**" (Exodus 26:33).
>
> On the third: "Let the water under the heavens [be gathered to one place]" (Genesis
> 1:9). In the Tabernacle, "You shall craft a **basin of bronze**… for washing…"
> (Exodus 30:18).
>
> On the fourth, "let there be **lights** in the firmament of the heavens" (Genesis
> 1:14). In the Tabernacle, "You shall craft a **candelabrum of pure gold**…"
> (Exodus 25:31).
>
> On the fifth, "let **birds** fly over the earth…" (Genesis 1:20). In the
> Tabernacle, "the **cherubs** shall be spreaders of wings" (Exodus 25:20).
>
> On the sixth, **man** was created. In the Tabernacle, "you, **draw Aaron your
> brother near** to you" (Exodus 28:1).
>
> On the seventh, "the heavens…were **completed**" (Genesis 2:1). In the Tabernacle,
> "all the work of the Tabernacle…was **completed**" (Exodus 39:32). In the creation
> of the world it is written: "God **blessed**" (Genesis 2:3). In the Tabernacle, "he
> **blessed** them" (Exodus 39:43). On the seventh, "God completed [*vaykhal*]"
> (Genesis 2:2). In the Tabernacle, "it was on the day that Moses **concluded**
> [*kalot*]." On the seventh, "and **sanctified** it" (Genesis 2:3). In the
> Tabernacle, "and **sanctified** it" (Numbers 7:1).

Seven days, seven pieces of the sanctuary, in order. Sky → tent curtains. The
dividing firmament → the dividing veil. Gathered waters → the bronze basin. The
lights → the lampstand. Winged things → the winged cherubim. Humanity → the high
priest. And the seventh day matched not by an object but by **four shared verbs**:
*completed*, *blessed*, *concluded*, *sanctified*.

### Midrash Tanchuma, Pekudei 2 → *toward temple cosmology*

> R. Simeon the son of Yohai said: This verse indicates that the earthly Temple is
> directly opposite the heavenly Temple, since it is said: *The place, O Lord, which
> Thou hast made for Thee to dwell in, the Sanctuary, O Lord, which Thy hands have
> established* (Exod. 15:17).
>
> R. Jacob the son of Issi asked: Why does it say: *I love the habitation of Thy
> house, and the place where Thy glory dwelleth?* **Because the Tabernacle is equal
> to the creation of the world itself.** How is that so? Concerning the first day, it
> is written: *In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth* (Gen. 1:1), and
> it is written elsewhere: *Who stretched out the heavens like a curtain* (Ps. 104:2),
> and concerning the Tabernacle it is written: *And thou shalt make curtains of goats'
> hair* (Exod. 26:7)…

The same correspondence, from a different collection — and with the extra claim
that the earthly sanctuary stands **directly opposite** a heavenly one.

One caution against overclaiming here: this is best described as the same
tradition in a second dress, not as a second independent witness. The later
portions of Numbers Rabbah and the Tanchuma material are closely related bodies of
midrash, and they are not testifying separately. What they show is that the
correspondence was **widespread and settled** in the midrashic tradition — which is
plenty — rather than that two unconnected sources arrived at it on their own.

## 9. A caution the tradition itself gives

### Mishnah Chagigah 2:1 ⇢ *a different direction*

> They may not expound upon the subject of forbidden relations in the presence of
> three. **Nor the work of creation in the presence of two.** Nor [the work of] the
> chariot in the presence of one, unless he is a sage and understands of his own
> knowledge. Whoever speculates upon four things, it would have been better had he
> not come into the world: what is above, what is beneath, what came before, and what
> came after.

An honest closing note, because it cuts against what we are doing. For the rabbis,
*ma'aseh bereshit* — "the work of creation" — was **restricted teaching**, expounded
to no more than one student at a time, and speculation about what lies above, below,
before, and after was flatly discouraged. Here we are, a whole group, doing it out
loud.

The difference is real, and it is worth naming rather than hiding. But two things
keep this from being a door closed in our faces.

**First, the tradition did not keep its own fence.** The Gemara on this very
mishnah goes straight on to expound the work of creation at length, in public, and
in considerable detail — the ten things made on the first day, what *tohu* and
*vohu* are, what the firmament is made of, how far the world expanded before God
said "enough," and whether heaven or earth came first (*Chagigah* 11b–16a). Both
the hidden-light passage and the Beit Shammai / Beit Hillel dispute quoted above
sit inside that discussion. A restriction the rabbis themselves work around on the
next page is a rule about *how* to teach the subject, not a prohibition on the
subject.

**Second, look at what is actually restricted.** The four forbidden speculations
are the esoteric ones — what is above, what is beneath, what came before, what came
after: the secrets behind the veil, not the plain sense of the verses. Later Jewish
law reads the fence that narrowly, which is why Jewish children have always been
*started* on Bereshit rather than kept away from it.

What the rabbis were guarding against was **speculative cosmology** — using
Genesis 1 as a launchpad for theories about the machinery of the universe. That
warning is well aimed, and this session is not exempt from it. What we are actually
doing is closer to the Mishnah's *other* mode — reading Genesis 1 as **liturgy**,
the way Tamid 7:4 does, as a text about what a world is *for* rather than how it is
wired. Keep Chagigah's warning in the room anyway. When a study of Genesis 1 starts
producing confident diagrams of things no one has seen, the rabbis told us where
that ends.

## 10. Where the thesis stopped being an argument and became a practice

The two passages below are the strongest material on this sheet, and they are the
easiest to miss because neither is a commentary on Genesis 1. They are **law**. If
the cosmos is a sanctuary, that ought to change what a person does on a Saturday
and what they do before eating an apple — and in Judaism it did, for two thousand
years, whether or not anyone was making the argument this session makes.

### Bavli Shabbat 49b → *toward temple cosmology*

> That which we learned in the mishna: The primary categories of labor, which are
> prohibited by Torah law on Shabbat, are forty-less-one; **to what does this number
> correspond?**…
>
> Rabbi Ḥanina bar Ḥama said to them: **They correspond to the labors in the
> Tabernacle.** All types of labor that were performed in the Tabernacle are
> enumerated as primary categories of labor with respect to Shabbat. However, other
> labors, even if they are significant, are not enumerated among the primary
> categories of labor since they were not performed in the Tabernacle…
>
> As it was taught in a baraita: **One is only liable for performing a labor to which
> there was a corresponding labor in the Tabernacle.** They sowed in order to grow
> dyes for the Tabernacle, and therefore you may not sow on Shabbat. They reaped, and
> therefore you may not reap on Shabbat.

The thirty-nine categories of forbidden work are listed at *Mishnah Shabbat* 7:2 —
sowing, weaving, dyeing, tying a knot, writing two letters, kindling a fire — and
the list looks arbitrary until you ask where it came from. Here is the answer, and
it is the whole of Session 4 in a single sentence of Talmud: **the work that stops
on the seventh day is the work that builds a sanctuary.**

The derivation is made from the same juxtaposition this session traces in Part 2:
the seventh of the tabernacle speeches (Exodus 31:12–17) is the Sabbath command,
which places the ceasing of sanctuary-labor exactly where Genesis 1 places the
seventh day. Note what this is and is not. It is not a midrash *about* Genesis 1;
it is a body of practice that assumes the connection and legislates from it. For a
class, that is worth more than another commentary — it is the reading in load-bearing
use.

### Bavli Berakhot 35a → *toward temple cosmology*

> One is forbidden to derive benefit from this world, which is the property of God,
> without reciting a blessing beforehand. And anyone who derives benefit from this
> world without a blessing, **it is as if he is guilty of misuse of a consecrated
> object**…
>
> Rav Yehuda said that Shmuel said: One who derives benefit from this world without a
> blessing, **it is as if he enjoyed objects consecrated to the heavens**, as it is
> stated: "The earth and all it contains is the Lord's, the world and all those who
> live in it" (Psalms 24:1). Rabbi Levi… raised a contradiction: It is written: "The
> earth and all it contains is the Lord's," and it is written elsewhere: "The heavens
> are the Lord's and the earth He has given over to mankind" (Psalms 115:16)…
> **Here, the verse that says that the earth is the Lord's refers to the situation
> before a blessing is recited** [and the other, after].

The technical charge is *me'ilah* — misappropriation of consecrated property, the
liability you incurred by eating from an offering by mistake. The rabbis apply it
to a piece of fruit. Behind it stands exactly the claim Part 4 makes: **there is no
neutral material.** Everything is temple property, so everything must be released
for ordinary use before you can use it.

Rabbi Levi's resolution is the elegant part. The two psalms are not in conflict;
they describe **before and after**. Before the blessing, the world is God's; after
it, it is yours. A blessing is therefore not a thank-you note attached to a meal you
already owned — it is the **transfer** that makes the bite yours to eat.

And notice the proof text. Psalm 24 — *"the earth is the Lord's and the fullness
thereof"* — is the psalm the Levites sang **on the first day**, tracking the first
day of creation (Tamid 7:4, §7 above). The verse that makes your breakfast temple
property is the verse the Temple sang over day one.

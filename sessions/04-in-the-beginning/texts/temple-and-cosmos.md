# Temple and cosmos

A source sheet for **Session 4**, gathering the non-biblical evidence that in the
ancient world a **temple was a small cosmos** and the **cosmos was a great
temple** — and that a god's "rest" was not a nap but a **residence**.

Two texts, a thousand years and several hundred miles apart:

1. **Gudea, Cylinder B** (Sumerian, c. 2100 BCE) — a ruler builds a house for his
   god, and the god moves in. This is what a temple dedication *looked like*, five
   centuries before Abraham.
2. **Josephus, *Antiquities of the Jews*** (Greek, c. 93 CE) — a Jewish priest
   explains to a Roman audience that Israel's tabernacle was built "in way of
   imitation and representation of the universe."

Full provenance and licensing at the foot of the page.

## 1. Gudea builds the E-ninnu (Sumerian, c. 2100 BCE)

Gudea was the ruler (*ensi*) of Lagaš, a Sumerian city-state, around 2100 BCE —
roughly the world Abraham's family came out of. Two enormous clay cylinders record,
in some 1,300 lines, how he built the temple **E-ninnu** for the warrior-god
**Ninĝirsu**. It is the longest Sumerian literary text we have, and it is
essentially a **building and dedication liturgy**: the god requests the house in a
dream, the plans are given, the bricks are moulded, the temple rises, and then the
god **enters it**.

Read these excerpts asking one question: *what happens at the end of a build?*

### The god enters his house

> **910–924.** Warrior Ninĝirsu **entered the house, the owner of the house had
> arrived.** He was an eagle raising its eyes toward a wild bull. The warrior's
> entering his house was a storm roaring into battle. Ninĝirsu entered his house
> and it became the shrine of the abzu when there is a festival. The owner came out
> of his house and he was Utu rising over the land of Lagaš.

*"The owner of the house had arrived."* The building has been finished for some
time. It is not truly a temple until the one it was built for walks in.

### He takes his seat

> **1182–1202.** The temple towered upwards in full grandeur, unparallelled in
> fearsomeness and radiance… Its owner, the warrior Ninĝirsu, came out as the
> daylight on the dais of Ĝir-nun… **Its throne, standing in the guena hall, was An's
> holy seat which is sat upon joyfully.** Its bed, standing in the bedroom, was a
> young cow kneeling down in its sleeping place. On its holy quilt (?), strewn with
> fresh herbs, **Mother Bau was resting comfortably with Lord Ninĝirsu.**

Note the two pieces of furniture the poem lingers over: a **throne** and a **bed**.
The god's occupation of his finished house is described simultaneously as
**enthronement** and as **rest** — and in this text those are not two different
things. To "rest" in your temple is to sit down and rule from it.

### The seven days after

> **1211–1223.** He had everything function as it should in his city. Gudea had
> built the E-ninnu, made its powers perfect… **He had debts remitted and made all
> hands clear. When his master entered the house, for seven days the slave woman was
> allowed to became equal to her mistress and the slave was allowed to walk side by
> side with his master.** But the ritually unclean ones could sleep only at the
> border of his city. He silenced the evil-speaking tongue and locked up evil.

This one deserves a long pause. The dedication of the temple runs **seven days**;
during them **debts are cancelled** and **the social order is suspended** — slave
and master walk side by side, servant and mistress are equal.

Every element of Israel's Sabbath is already lying here in pieces, a thousand years
early: seven days, release from debt, and rest that reaches the household servant.
What Genesis does is not invent these ideas. It does something stranger and more
radical: it takes a **one-off festival** at the dedication of *one* building and
writes it into the **structure of the world** — every seventh day, forever, for
everyone (Exodus 20:8–11; Deuteronomy 5:12–15). Compare Session 1's table on where
sevens and rest come from.

## 2. Josephus: the tabernacle imitates the universe (c. 93 CE)

**Flavius Josephus** (c. 37 – c. 100 CE) was a Jewish priest of aristocratic
Jerusalem stock who served as a commander in the revolt against Rome, was captured,
and spent the rest of his life in Rome writing Jewish history for a Greek-speaking
Roman readership. He had **served in the Second Temple** before its destruction in
70 CE. When he explains what the sanctuary *meant*, he is not speculating from the
outside; he is a priest describing his own building.

In *Antiquities* Book 3 he answers the charge that the Jews are irreligious, and
his defence is this: look at the tabernacle, and you will see it is a **model of
the universe**.

> **7.** Now here one may wonder at the ill-will which men bear to us, and which
> they profess to bear on account of our despising that Deity which they pretend to
> honor; for if any one do but consider the fabric of the tabernacle, and take a view
> of the garments of the high priest, and of those vessels which we make use of in
> our sacred ministration, he will find that our legislator was a divine man, and
> that we are unjustly reproached by others; for if any one do without prejudice, and
> with judgment, look upon these things, he will find **they were every one made in
> way of imitation and representation of the universe.**
>
> **When Moses distinguished the tabernacle into three parts, and allowed two of
> them to the priests, as a place accessible and common, he denoted the land and the
> sea, these being of general access to all; but he set apart the third division for
> God, because heaven is inaccessible to men.**
>
> And when he ordered **twelve loaves** to be set on the table, he denoted **the
> year**, as distinguished into so many months. By branching out the candlestick into
> seventy parts, he secretly intimated the Decani, or seventy divisions of the
> planets; and as to **the seven lamps upon the candlesticks, they referred to the
> course of the planets**, of which that is the number.
>
> **The veils, too, which were composed of four things, they declared the four
> elements**; for the fine linen was proper to signify the earth, because the flax
> grows out of the earth; the purple signified the sea, because that color is dyed by
> the blood of a sea shell-fish; the blue is fit to signify the air; and the scarlet
> will naturally be an indication of fire.
>
> Now the vestment of the high priest being made of linen, signified the earth; the
> blue denoted the sky, being like lightning in its pomegranates, and in the noise of
> the bells resembling thunder. And for the ephod, it showed that **God had made the
> universe of four elements**… He also appointed the breastplate to be placed in the
> middle of the ephod, **to resemble the earth, for that has the very middle place of
> the world**. And **the girdle which encompassed the high priest round, signified the
> ocean, for that goes round about and includes the universe.** Each of the sardonyxes
> declares to us the sun and the moon… And for the twelve stones, whether we
> understand by them the months, or whether we understand the like number of the
> signs of that circle which the Greeks call the Zodiac, we shall not be mistaken in
> their meaning. And for the mitre, which was of a blue color, it seems to me to mean
> heaven; for how otherwise could the name of God be inscribed upon it?
> — *Antiquities of the Jews* 3.7.7 (§§179–187), trans. William Whiston

Some of Josephus's specific decodings are Greek philosophy in Jewish dress — the
four elements and the seven planets are Hellenistic furniture, not Mosaic. Do not
lean on the details.

Lean on the **premise**, which he states as something obvious and uncontested:
*the sanctuary is a scale model of the world.* Three zones of the tabernacle, three
zones of the cosmos. The lampstand is the sky's lights. The high priest wears the
universe on his body. A first-century Jerusalem priest thought this was simply what
the building was — and he expected an educated outsider to find it persuasive
rather than eccentric.

Turn that around, and you have this session's thesis: if the sanctuary is a small
world, then the world is a large sanctuary. **Philo of Alexandria**, Josephus's
older contemporary, says the same thing from the other end — that the whole cosmos
is God's temple and the true high priest is the Logos (*On the Special Laws* 1.66;
*On Dreams* 1.215) — but Josephus is the cleaner witness because he served in the
building he is describing.

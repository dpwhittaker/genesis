# Where the wives came from

A source sheet for **Session 6**, on the question Genesis 4 raises in five
words and never answers: **"Cain was intimate with his wife"** (4:17).

This is one of the oldest questions asked of the Bible, and it has been
answered in more different ways than almost any other. What follows is the
full range, **earliest first** — because the readings closest to the text in
time are also, as it happens, the ones least embarrassed by it.

Two things worth settling before we start.

**It is a fair question.** It is not a gotcha and it is not new. Jewish
readers were working on it two centuries before the New Testament. Anyone who
tells you it is a frivolous question has not read the people who took it most
seriously.

**And it is a question about a silence.** Genesis does not say Cain's wife was
a stranger, or a sister, or anything else. It does not name her. The whole
discussion below is about what belongs in a gap — which is exactly the kind of
reading this class has been practising since Session 3.

---

## 1. What the text actually gives us

Three data points, and that is all.

> **17** Cain was intimate with **his wife**, and she became pregnant and gave
> birth to Enoch. Cain was building a **city**…
> — Genesis 4:17

She arrives with no name, no father, no origin, and no introduction. The
sentence assumes we will not ask.

> **14** …I will be a homeless wanderer on the earth; **whoever finds me will
> kill me!**
> — Genesis 4:14

The same problem in a different key, three verses earlier. Cain expects to
meet people. So does the text — he builds a **city**, which is not a thing one
does with a wife and a son.

> **4** The length of time Adam lived after he became the father of Seth was
> 800 years; during this time he had **other sons and daughters**.
> — Genesis 5:4

The only relevant datum Genesis ever supplies — and it arrives a chapter
later, in a different genealogy, about a different son.

---

## 2. The women Genesis 4 *does* name

There are three, and none of them is Cain's wife.

| Name | Hebrew | Root | Sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Adah** — Lamech's wife (4:19) | *ʿāḏâ* | *ʿ-d-h* | ornament, adornment |
| **Zillah** — Lamech's wife (4:19) | *ṣillâ* | *ṣ-l-l* | shadow, shade |
| **Naamah** — Tubal-Cain's sister (4:22) | *naʿămâ* | *n-ʿ-m* | pleasant, lovely |
{: .ff-table .ff-wrap}

All three are ordinary, transparent **Hebrew** words. And notice what they are
attached to: Adah bears the **herdsmen**, Zillah bears the **metalworker**,
and Naamah is named and then does nothing at all — she is given a name and no
sentence.

**Cain's own name is the clearest case of what is going on here.** Hebrew
*qayin* is a Semitic word for a **smith** or metalworker — compare Arabic
*qayn* and Aramaic/Syriac *qaynāyā*, both "metalworker" — and it is the very
same word as **Kenite** (*qênî*), the name of the metalworking clan Moses
marries into (Numbers 24:22; Judges 4:11). Cain's line runs seven generations
and terminates in **Tubal-Cain**, "who heated metal and shaped all kinds of
tools made of bronze and iron."

But Genesis 4:1 gives the name a *different* explanation:

> …and gave birth to Cain *(qayin)*. Then she said, "I have **created**
> *(qānîtî)* a man just as the LORD did!"

*Qānîtî* is from a different root, *q-n-h*, "to acquire, to create." This is a
**pun**, not an etymology — the kind of sound-play Genesis loves and uses
constantly (*ʾādām* / *ʾădāmâ*, *ḥawwâ* / *ḥay*, *bābel* / *bālal*). Eve names
her son for what she feels, and the narrator lets the other meaning stand
underneath it.

**Which is the useful finding.** The names in this chapter are doing **jobs**
— they carry puns, professions and themes. They are not functioning as a
family register. That is worth holding on to before we ask what the *unnamed*
wife's name might have been.

## 3. The names later traditions gave her

Genesis never names Cain's wife. Other books did — and they do not agree.

| Name | Source | Date | Says she is |
|---|---|---|---|
| **'Âwân** | *Jubilees* 4:1, 4:9 | c. 160–150 BCE | Cain's **sister** |
| **Themech** | Pseudo-Philo, *LAB* 2:1 | 1st century CE | (not stated) |
| **Lebhudha** | *Cave of Treasures* | Syriac, c. 6th century CE | Cain's **twin** |
| **Luluwa** | *Conflict of Adam and Eve* / *First Book of Adam and Eve* | Arabic & Ethiopic recensions, later | Cain's **twin** |
{: .ff-table .ff-wrap}

(Abel's twin sister, where one is named, is **Kelimath** or **Aklia**.)

> And **Cain took 'Âwân his sister to be his wife** and she bare him Enoch at
> the close of the fourth jubilee.
> — *Jubilees* 4:9

> But Cain dwelt in the earth trembling, according as God appointed unto him
> after he slew Abel his brother; and **the name of his wife was Themech**.
> — Pseudo-Philo, *Biblical Antiquities* 2:1

### So — has anyone traced these names to a particular people?

This is the question the class asked, and it has a real answer. Two, in fact.

**First: the names in the Bible are ordinary West Semitic, and they turn up on
women of several different peoples.**

- **Adah** is also the name of **Esau's Hittite wife**, "the daughter of Elon
  the Hittite" (Genesis 36:2).
- **Naamah** is also the name of the **Ammonite** mother of King Rehoboam
  (1 Kings 14:21) — and of a town in Judah (Joshua 15:41).

So these names were not distinctively Israelite. They circulated across the
whole Northwest Semitic world, worn by Hittite-, Ammonite- and
Israelite-associated women alike. That is a genuine result — but notice which
way it cuts: it tells us these are **common regional names**, not that anyone
in Genesis 4 belonged to a particular nation.

**Second — and this is the more interesting one: the extra-biblical names have
been traced, and they lead back to the language of the book that supplies
them.**

- ***Âwân*** is commonly connected with the Hebrew ʾāwen, "iniquity, trouble" —
  in a **Hebrew** book. (Treat this as a proposed etymology rather than a
  settled one; *Jubilees* survives complete only in Ge'ez, and the vowels are
  not secure.)
- ***Azûrâ***, whom the same book gives to **Seth**, is likewise usually linked
  to the Hebrew root *ʿ-z-r*, "to help" — the root of *ʿēzer*, the word for the
  woman in Genesis 2:18.
- ***Luluwa*** appears in an **Arabic and Ethiopic** work — and Arabic *luʾluʾa*
  means **"pearl."** The text itself glosses the name for us:

  > But **Luluwa means "beautiful,"** because she was more beautiful than her
  > mother.
  > — *First Book of Adam and Eve* 74:8

  The same chapter explains Cain's name as meaning **"hater"** — which is not
  what *qayin* means in any Semitic language. This author is explaining names
  in his own idiom, not preserving old ones.

**The pattern is the finding.** A Hebrew book gives her a Hebrew name. A
Syriac book gives her a Syriac name. An Arabic book gives her an Arabic name
meaning *pearl*, with a folk etymology attached. **Each community named her in
its own language, for its own reasons** — which is precisely what you see when
names are being *supplied*, and not what you see when they are being
*transmitted*.

So the honest answer to "have the wives' names been traced to different people
groups?" is: **the trail leads to the authors, not to the women.** There is no
onomastic road out of Genesis 4 to some other nation, because Genesis 4
contains no name to put on that road.

## 4. The explanations, earliest first

### 1 — She was his sister *(the earliest and dominant Jewish answer)*

This is what the oldest readers say, and they say it without a flicker of
discomfort.

- ***Jubilees* 4:9** (c. 160–150 BCE) names her, calls her his sister, and moves
  on. The same book supplies sister-wives for Seth, Enos and Kenan too.
- **Josephus** (c. 93 CE) says of Adam and Eve simply: "**They had also
  daughters.**"
- ***Genesis Rabbah* 22:2–3** derives it from the grammar of 4:1–2 — Rabbi
  Yehoshua ben Korḥa: "**Two entered the bed and seven descended**," i.e. twin
  sisters were born alongside Cain and Abel.
- ***Genesis Rabbah* 22:7** turns the sisters into the **motive for the murder**:
  "An extra twin sister was born with Abel. This one said: 'I will take her, as
  I am the firstborn.' That one said: 'I will take her, as she was born with
  me.'"
- The **Syriac and Arabic traditions** name both twins outright.

**On the obvious objection.** Leviticus 18, which forbids marrying a sister,
is written centuries after these events are set and does not claim to be
retroactive — and none of the ancient readers above raises the difficulty at
all. It is worth noticing that Genesis itself has **Abraham married to his
half-sister** long after the flood (Genesis 20:12), and reports it without
comment. The objection is real for us; it does not appear to have been real
for them.

### 2 — A niece, or someone further down

Genesis 5:4 gives Adam 800 years of further children. Genesis 4 is not a
chronology — it compresses seven generations into six verses and never dates
anything. Cain's **city** already presupposes a population, so the text is not
imagining the small family we picture. On this reading the wife is simply
someone born later, and no twins are required.

### 3 — People outside the garden

That Genesis 1 and Genesis 2 describe different things: a humanity at large,
and then a particular couple chosen out of it.

- **Isaac La Peyrère**, *Prae-Adamitae* (1655), gave this its first systematic
  form and applied it directly to our question — Cain's wife and the citizens
  of his city were, for him, people who were never descended from Adam. **This
  theory's history has to be told with it:** it fed directly into polygenism
  and was used to argue for racial hierarchy. That is part of its record and
  should not be quietly dropped.
- **S. Joshua Swamidass**, *The Genealogical Adam and Eve* (IVP, 2019), is the
  modern, explicitly anti-polygenist version. His distinction is between
  *genealogical* and *genetic* ancestry: a couple living relatively recently
  could be genealogical ancestors of every person alive without being the sole
  genetic source of humanity. Cain's wife is one of his opening examples. If
  someone in the group has met this through BibleProject, this is the book.

### 4 — The narrator is not answering this question

The reading this class has been building toward since Session 3.

Genesis 4 is not a population report. It is an account of **how violence and
civilisation grew up together** — and it is told at speed, in a form that
compresses generations, names people for their functions, and mentions a wife
only because the line has to reach a city. The chapter leaves a hole here for
the same reason it leaves Cain's words blank in verse 8 and the mark
unspecified in verse 15: because those are not what it is about.

That is not a dodge, and it does not make the question illegitimate. It is a
claim about **genre** — and it is testable. Ask of each gap: does filling it
change what the story is doing? For Cain's wife, the honest answer is no. For
the missing speech in 4:8, the ancient readers thought it was yes — which is
why they filled *that* one, at length. (See
[the rabbis on Genesis 4](rabbinic-sources.html#2-the-missing-speech-in-48).)

---

## 5. One more thing the tradition did with these women

Having married Cain's line off, the rabbis then married it **back in**:

> "And the sister of Tuval Cain was Naama." Rabbi Abba bar Kahana said:
> **Naama was Noah's wife.**
> — *Genesis Rabbah* 23:3

And Pseudo-Philo has Cain's son Enoch take "a wife **of the daughters of
Seth**." Both traditions refuse to let Cain's line be sealed off. Whatever the
chapter says about where violence comes from, the ancient readers would not
let it become a story about two kinds of people.

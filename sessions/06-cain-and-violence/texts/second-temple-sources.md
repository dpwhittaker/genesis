# The earliest readings of Genesis 4

A source sheet for **Session 6**. Everything here was written **before the
rabbinic literature** — roughly **200 BCE to 100 CE** — which makes these the
oldest surviving interpretations of the Cain story in any language. Several
are older than the New Testament. For the rabbis, see the companion sheet,
**[the rabbis on Genesis 4](rabbinic-sources.html)**. For the specific question
of Cain's wife, see **[where the wives came from](the-wives.html)**.

**Why these matter more than usual here.** Genesis 4 is unusually silent. It
never says *why* the offering was refused, never says what the mark looked
like, never explains where Cain's wife came from, and drops Cain's own words
in verse 8 entirely. Every reader listed below is standing in front of those
same gaps with no more information than we have. What they do with them is the
best evidence we have of how the story was *heard* in the centuries around the
New Testament.

**Early does not mean right.** Watch how often a reader supplies a motive the
text withholds, and then how quickly that supplied motive becomes "what the
story says." That is the mechanism worth learning, and it is still running.

Each entry is marked **→ toward the session's reading** or **⇢ a different
direction**.

---

## 1. *Jubilees* 4:1–12 — *c. 160–150 BCE* → *toward the session's reading*

A Hebrew retelling of Genesis and Exodus, found among the Dead Sea Scrolls in
more copies than most books of the Bible. It survives complete only in Ge'ez
(Ethiopic). This is the **oldest surviving retelling of Genesis 4**.

Notice what it does and does not explain:

> And in the third week in the second jubilee she gave birth to Cain, and in
> the fourth she gave birth to Abel, and in the fifth she gave birth to her
> daughter **'Âwân**.
>
> And in the first (year) of the third jubilee, **Cain slew Abel because
> (God) accepted the sacrifice of Abel, and did not accept the offering of
> Cain.**
> — *Jubilees* 4:1–2

**That is the whole explanation.** Jubilees calls one a *sacrifice* and the
other an *offering* and never says what the difference was. The oldest reader
we have declines to answer the question — which is worth knowing before we
spend an hour on it.

What Jubilees *does* fill in is the family:

> And Adam and his wife mourned for Abel **four weeks of years**… And in the
> sixth week he begat his daughter **'Azûrâ**. And **Cain took 'Âwân his
> sister to be his wife** and she bare him Enoch at the close of the fourth
> jubilee.
> — *Jubilees* 4:8–11

Twenty-eight years of mourning for Abel, and a sister named for Cain to marry
— stated flatly, with no defensiveness at all. Jubilees goes on to name a
sister-wife for Seth ('Azûrâ), for Enos (Nôâm), and for Kenan (Mûalêleth),
and then — from Mahalalel onward — quietly switches to **cousins**: "the
daughter of his father's brother." The author is working out a marriage
schema, and you can watch him tighten it as the population grows.

## 2. Philo of Alexandria, *On the Sacrifices of Abel and Cain* — *c. 20 BCE – 50 CE* ⇢ *a different direction*

A Greek-speaking Jewish philosopher in Alexandria, a contemporary of Jesus,
who devoted an entire treatise to these two brothers. Philo reads from the
**Greek** Bible, not the Hebrew, and that matters.

He finds **two** accusations against Cain, and he builds both out of silences:

> And it came to pass **after some days** that Cain brought of the fruits of
> the earth as an offering to the Lord. Here are two accusations against the
> self-loving man; one that he showed his gratitude to God **after some days,
> and not at once**, the other that he made his offering **from the fruits,
> and not from the first fruits**…
> — *De Sacrificiis* 52

And his summary, later in the same treatise:

> These now, and such as these, are the accusations brought against Cain, who
> after some days offered sacrifice; but Abel did not bring the same
> offerings, nor did he bring his offerings in the same manner; but **instead
> of inanimate things he brought living sacrifices**, and instead of younger
> things, worthy only of the second place, he offered what was older and of
> the first consideration, and **instead of what was weak he offered what was
> strong and fat**…
> — *De Sacrificiis* 88

**Watch the move.** Genesis never says Cain withheld his firstfruits. It
simply doesn't use the word. Philo reads the *absence* of "firstfruits" as an
accusation — and this becomes, by way of the rabbis and the Reformers, the
single most widely taught answer in Christian history. It may well be right.
But it is an argument from silence, and Philo is where it starts.

## 3. Josephus, *Antiquities of the Jews* 1.2.1–2 — *c. 93 CE* ⇢ *a different direction*

A Jewish priest and historian writing for a Roman audience. He is the earliest
witness to a completely different answer.

### On the offerings

> Now Cain brought the fruits of the earth, and of his husbandry: but Abel
> brought milk, and the first fruits of his flock. But **God was more delighted
> with the latter oblation, when he was honoured with what grew naturally of
> its own accord, than he was with what was the invention of a covetous man,
> and gotten by forcing the ground.**

Not *late* and not *second-best* — **violent**. For Josephus the problem is
agriculture itself: Cain "first contrived to plough the ground," and ploughing
is a way of *forcing* things out of the earth. Note too that Josephus reads the
Hebrew consonants *ḥlb* as *ḥālāb*, **milk**, where our Bibles read *ḥēleb*,
**fat**. Same letters, different vowels, different story.

### On why Cain was not executed

> God therefore did not inflict the punishment [of death] upon him, **on
> account of his offering sacrifice, and thereby making supplication to him
> not to be extreme in his wrath to him**: but he made him accursed, and
> threatned his posterity in the seventh generation. He also cast him,
> together with his wife, out of that land. And when he was afraid, that in
> wandring about he should fall among wild beasts, and by that means perish;
> **God bid him not to entertain such a melancholy suspicion**… and setting a
> mark upon him, that he might be known, he commanded him to depart.

**This is the earliest reading of the mark as an answer to a plea.** Cain
supplicates; God relents from death; God reassures him; God marks him. Josephus
also, in passing, has Cain already married at the moment of exile — "together
with his wife" — and never explains her.

### On what Cain became

> He also introduced a change in that way of simplicity wherein men lived
> before; and **was the author of measures and weights**. And whereas they
> lived innocently and generously while they knew nothing of such arts, **he
> changed the world into cunning craftiness**. He first of all **set
> boundaries about lands**: he built a city, and fortified it with walls…

Josephus reads Cain's line as the invention of **civilisation as a system of
grasping** — measures, weights, property lines, walls. It is a hostile reading,
and a shrewd one: Genesis 4 really does put city, herding, music and metallurgy
in the line of the murderer.

> Now Jared was the son of Enoch: whose son was Malaliel: whose son was
> Mathusela: whose son was **Lamech. Who had seventy seven children** by two
> wives, Silla and Ada.

Josephus takes the "seventy-seven" of Lamech's song as a count of his
**children**. It is a misreading, but a revealing one — even he found the
number too strange to leave where it was.

> **A note on a number you may meet.** Whiston's 1737 English edition carries a
> footnote reporting "the old tradition" that Adam had **thirty-three sons and
> twenty-three daughters**. That figure is often quoted as though Josephus
> wrote it. He did not — it is Whiston's own note, citing a later source.
> Josephus's own text says only, of Adam and Eve, "**They had also
> daughters.**"

## 4. Pseudo-Philo, *Biblical Antiquities* 1–2 — *first century CE* → *toward the session's reading*

An anonymous Jewish retelling of the Bible from Adam to David, probably
written in Hebrew in the first century, surviving only in Latin. It is
obsessed with names, and it hands out dozens that appear nowhere else.

> But Cain dwelt in the earth trembling, according as God appointed unto him
> after he slew Abel his brother; and **the name of his wife was Themech**.
> And Cain knew Themech his wife and she conceived and bare Enoch. Now Cain
> was fifteen years old when he did these things; and from that time he began
> to build cities, until he had founded **seven cities**…
> — *LAB* 2:1–3

**Themech is a different name from Jubilees' 'Âwân**, and this author does not
call her Cain's sister. Two Jewish texts, perhaps a century apart, name Cain's
wife — and they name her differently. That disagreement is itself the finding;
see **[the wives sheet](the-wives.html)**.

Pseudo-Philo also has Cain's son Enoch "take a wife **of the daughters of
Seth**" — that is, the murderer's line and the replacement line intermarry in
the second generation.

## 5. Wisdom of Solomon 10:3 — *first century BCE* ⇢ *a different direction*

A Greek Jewish work, roughly contemporary with Philo, retelling Genesis as a
series of rescues by personified Wisdom. Cain is the one man in the sequence
she does not rescue:

> But when **the unjust went away from her in his anger**, he perished by the
> fury wherewith he murdered his brother.
> — Wisdom 10:3 (Douay–Rheims; "the unjust" is Cain)

Note the diagnosis: not a wrong offering, not a wrong crop — **anger**, and a
departure. Whatever else was wrong with Cain, this reader locates the fatal
moment at Genesis 4:5, in the downcast face, and not at 4:3.

## 6. *1 Enoch* 22 — *third–first century BCE* → *toward the session's reading*

*Described here rather than quoted*, so that no modern translation is relied
upon. In Enoch's tour of the places where the dead wait, the angel Raphael
shows him a hollow where the spirit of Abel is still bringing his complaint
against Cain — Genesis 4:10's "your brother's blood is crying out to me" taken
as an ongoing action, centuries later. Elsewhere (*1 Enoch* 85) the story is
retold as a vision of a black bull goring a red one.

The point for Session 6: the earliest readers heard 4:10 as a voice that has
**not stopped**. Hebrews 12:24 assumes exactly that.

## 7. The New Testament

Genesis 4 is quoted or invoked more often in the New Testament than any other
chapter of the primeval history. Five passages, and they do not all pull the
same way.

### Abel as the first of the righteous martyrs

> …so that on you will come all the righteous blood shed on earth, **from the
> blood of righteous Abel** to the blood of Zechariah son of Barachiah…
> — Matthew 23:35

### Abel's offering, and the reason given

> **By faith** Abel offered God a **greater sacrifice** than Cain, and through
> his faith he was commended as righteous because God commended him for his
> offerings. And through his faith he still speaks, though he is dead.
> — Hebrews 11:4

**A translation note worth having.** The Greek word here for Abel's "sacrifice"
is *thysia* — but in the **Septuagint** of Genesis 4, *thysia* is the word used
for **Cain's** offering, while Abel's is called *dōra*, "gifts." The Hebrew
uses one word, *minḥâ*, for both — and *minḥâ* is the standard Old Testament
term for a **grain** offering. Any argument that Abel's gift was accepted
*because it was an animal* has to get past the fact that Genesis calls it by
the cereal-offering word.

Hebrews itself gives a different reason: **faith**.

### Cain's deeds, and the reason given

> …not like Cain who was of the evil one and brutally murdered his brother.
> And why did he murder him? **Because his deeds were evil, but his brother's
> were righteous.**
> — 1 John 3:12

> Woe to them! For they have traveled down **Cain's path**…
> — Jude 11

### Abel's blood, answered

> …and to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and to the sprinkled blood
> that **speaks of something better than Abel's does**.
> — Hebrews 12:24

Abel's blood cried from the ground **for vengeance** (Genesis 4:10). Hebrews
says there is now blood that speaks better — same verb, opposite request.

### And the number

> Then Peter came to him and said, "Lord, how many times must I forgive my
> brother who sins against me? As many as seven times?" Jesus said to him,
> "Not seven times, I tell you, but **seventy-seven times**!"
> — Matthew 18:21–22

In the Greek this is *hebdomēkontakis hepta* — **word for word the Septuagint
of Genesis 4:24**, Lamech's boast. That number occurs in exactly two places in
the Bible, and this is the second. Jesus answers Lamech in Lamech's own
arithmetic, and reverses the operation.

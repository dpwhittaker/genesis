# The rabbis on Genesis 4

A source sheet for **Session 6**. Everything here comes from the Jewish
interpretive tradition. For the readings that are older than this literature —
Jubilees, Philo, Josephus and the rest — see the companion sheet,
**[the earliest readings](second-temple-sources.html)**. For Cain's wife, see
**[where the wives came from](the-wives.html)**.

**On dates.** These documents were compiled centuries after the sages they
quote, and they quote them by name. Both facts matter. Where a passage is
attributed to a named rabbi we give that rabbi's own century as well as the
document's — so you can see how far the tradition reaches back, and hold the
attribution for what it is: **tradition, not transcript.**

| Document | Compiled | Attributed voices reach back to |
|---|---|---|
| **Genesis Rabbah** | c. 400–450 CE, Roman Palestine | 2nd–4th century sages |
| **Targum Onkelos** | c. 2nd century CE, later fixed in Babylonia | — |
| **Targum Pseudo-Jonathan** | final form c. 7th–8th century or later | earlier Palestinian traditions |
{: .ff-table .ff-wrap}

A word on the **Targums**, since they carry a lot of weight in this session.
They are Aramaic translations made for synagogues where Hebrew was no longer
the everyday language — and they translate *loosely*, folding interpretation
into the rendering. When a Targum adds a sentence, that sentence is what the
translator believed the verse **meant**. There is no better evidence of how a
passage was heard out loud.

Each entry is marked **→ toward the session's reading** or **⇢ a different
direction**. Both are useful. Where the tradition agrees with us it is an old
witness that we did not invent this; where it diverges it is a reminder that a
good text carries more than one true reading.

## 1. Why the offering was refused

### *Genesis Rabbah* 22:5 ⇢ *a different direction — the standard answer*

> "Cain brought an offering of the fruit of the ground to the Lord" — **from
> the refuse**. This is analogous to a malevolent sharecropper who eats the
> ripe fruit himself and provides the field owner with the unripe fruit.

Clean, memorable, and **not in the text**. Genesis says only "from the fruit
of the ground." The parable supplies the adjective. This is the same move
Philo made four centuries earlier (see [the earliest readings](second-temple-sources.html)),
and between them they have furnished nearly every sermon on this passage
since.

### Targum Pseudo-Jonathan 4:3 ⇢ *a different direction*

> And it was at the end of days, on the fourteenth of Nisan, that Kain brought
> of the produce of the earth, **the seed of cotton** (or flax), an oblation
> of first things before the Lord.

Two additions in one verse. The Targum **dates** the offering to Passover eve,
and it makes Cain's gift **flaxseed** — inedible, the cheapest thing a farmer
grows. Note, though, that this Targum still calls it "an oblation of **first
things**" — even while disparaging it, it will not say Cain withheld his
firstfruits.

### *Genesis Rabbah* 22:6 → *toward the session's reading*

On the exact wording of 4:4–5 — that God "turned to Abel *and* to his
offering":

> "The Lord turned to Abel and to his offering" – **He was contented with him
> [personally]**… "But to Cain and to his offering He did not turn" – **He was
> not contented with him.**

The midrash has noticed that the man is named before the gift, both times, and
concludes that the *man* is what was accepted or refused. Hebrews 11:4 and
1 John 3:12 reach the same place by a different road.

### *Genesis Rabbah* 22:6 on *śəʾēt* → *toward the session's reading*

The same section works on the strange word in 4:7 — *śəʾēt*, "lifting":

> "if you do good, it will be lifted up [se'et]" – **blessing**… "And if you
> do not do good," there will also be se'et – **a curse**… Another matter,
> "if you do good," **I will forgive you all your sins** — se'et in the sense
> of *noseh avon*, bearing iniquity.

**Hold on to that third reading.** *Nāśāʾ ʿāwōn*, "to bear iniquity," is the
ordinary Hebrew idiom for **to forgive** — and it is the same root Cain
reaches for in verse 13. God offers a *lifting* before the murder; Cain says
afterwards that his guilt cannot be *lifted*. The midrash heard the connection.

### Targum Onkelos 4:7 → *toward the session's reading*

> Is this not so—if you improve [your actions], **there is forgiveness [for
> you]**, but if you do not improve [your actions], sin rests at the opening
> [your sin will be kept for the Day of Judgement]…

The oldest Targum reads *śəʾēt* as **forgiveness**, flatly. So before the
murder, on this reading, God has already put pardon on the table.

## 2. The missing speech in 4:8

The Hebrew records that Cain spoke and **does not record what he said** (see
[the passage sheet](genesis-4.html)). The Targums fill the gap — and what they
put in it is a theological argument.

### Targum Pseudo-Jonathan 4:8 → *toward the session's reading*

> Kain answered and said to Habel, **I perceive that the world was created in
> goodness, but it is not governed according to the fruit of good works, for
> there is respect to persons in judgment; therefore it is that thy offering
> was accepted, and mine not accepted with good will.** Habel answered and
> said to Kain, In goodness was the world created, and according to the fruit
> of good works is it governed; and there is no respect of persons in
> judgment; but because the fruits of my works were better than thine, my
> oblation, before thine, hath been accepted with good will.
>
> Kain answered and said to Habel, **There is neither judgment nor Judge, nor
> another world; nor will good reward be given to the righteous, nor vengeance
> be taken of the wicked.** And Habel answered and said to Kain, There is a
> judgment, and there is a Judge; and there is another world, and a good
> reward given to the righteous, and vengeance taken of the wicked.
>
> And because of these words they had contention upon the face of the field;
> and Kain arose against Habel his brother, and drave a stone into his
> forehead, and killed him.

**This is the single most important passage on the sheet.** The ancient
synagogue heard the first murder as the end of an argument about **whether God
is just** — and the argument starts from exactly the question this session
opens with: *why was his offering accepted and not mine?* Cain reasons from
one unexplained refusal to the conclusion that there is no Judge at all, and
then acts on it.

Note what the two brothers actually disagree about. Abel's position is that
the world *is* run on merit and his works were better. Cain's is that it is
not run on anything. The Targum does not let either of them off; it just shows
you where each answer goes.

## 3. Why they quarrelled — three other guesses

### *Genesis Rabbah* 22:7 ⇢ *a different direction*

> About what were they quarreling? They said: 'Let us divide the world between
> us.' One took the land and one took the movable property. This one said:
> 'The land on which you are standing is mine.' That one said: 'What you are
> wearing is mine.' That one said: 'Take it off.' This one said: 'Fly.' As a
> result: "Cain rose up against Abel his brother and killed him."
>
> Rabbi Yehoshua of Sikhnin said in the name of Rabbi Levi: … this one said:
> '**The Temple shall be built in my domain**,' and that one said: 'The Temple
> shall be built in my domain,' as it is stated: "It was when they were **in
> the field**," and "field" is nothing other than the Temple, as it says:
> "Zion will be plowed like a field" (Micah 3:12)…
>
> Rabbi Huna said: **An extra twin sister was born with Abel.** This one
> [Cain] said: 'I will take her, as I am the firstborn.' That one [Abel] said:
> 'I will take her, as she was born with me.' As a result: "Cain rose up."

*Rabbi Levi and Rabbi Huna: third–fourth century.*

Property, religion, or a woman. The rabbis are not being frivolous — they are
enumerating what people actually kill each other over, and noticing that
Genesis has deliberately not told us which one this was. The third answer is
also the tradition's standard solution to where the wives came from; it is
followed on [the wives sheet](the-wives.html).

### *Genesis Rabbah* 22:8 ⇢ *a different direction, and worth hearing anyway*

> Rabbi Yoḥanan said: **Abel was stronger than Cain**, as the verse need not
> have stated: "rose up." Rather, it teaches that **he had been situated
> underneath him**. He [Cain] said to him: 'There are the two of us in the
> world, what will you go and say to Father?' He [Abel] **became filled with
> mercy for him** [and released him]. Immediately, he [Cain] rose up against
> him and killed him.

*Rabbi Yoḥanan: third century.*

Read from the single word "rose up," Abel wins the fight, lets his brother go
out of pity, and is killed for it. The midrash draws a hard moral — "do not do
a favour for a wicked person" — which you may or may not want. But the picture
of the victim showing mercy first is not one most of us carry, and it is very
old.

## 4. "My guilt is too great to be lifted"

### *Genesis Rabbah* 22:11 → *toward the session's reading*

> "Cain said to the Lord: 'Is my iniquity too great to bear?'" — **You bear
> the upper worlds and the lower worlds, but You are unable to bear my
> transgression?**
>
> Another matter, My iniquity is greater than Father's. Father violated a
> lesser commandment and was expelled from the Garden of Eden. This [what I
> have done] is the severe transgression of bloodshed, all the more so that my
> iniquity is great.

The Sefaria edition's own note on this passage is worth quoting: *"Cain was
not questioning the punishment. Rather, he was confessing his sin."*

### Targum Pseudo-Jonathan 4:13 → *toward the session's reading*

> And Kain said before the Lord, **More heavy is my rebellion than can be
> borne (away). Yet is there power before Thee to forgive it.**

The Targum does not merely allow the second reading of 4:13 — it **states it
outright and then adds a plea.** On this reading Cain's line is not a complaint
about his sentence at all. It is a confession, followed by an appeal to a
mercy he believes exists.

Whatever we finally decide the Hebrew means, the ancient synagogue heard a man
asking to be forgiven. And the next thing that happens in the chapter is that
God protects him.

## 5. The sign

### Targum Pseudo-Jonathan 4:15 → *toward the session's reading*

> And the Lord said to him, Behold now, any one who killeth Kain, **unto seven
> generations** vengeance shall be taken of him. And the Lord **sealed upon
> the face of Kain the mark of the Name great and honourable**, that any one
> who might find him should not kill him when he saw it upon him.

The protection is **the divine Name**. Not a brand, not a disfigurement — the
Name of God placed on a murderer so that no one will touch him.

### *Genesis Rabbah* 22:12 — four guesses and no verdict

> "The Lord placed a sign for Cain" – Rabbi Yehuda said: **He caused the orb
> of the sun to shine for him.** Rabbi Neḥemya said: Would the Holy One
> blessed be He cause the orb of the sun to shine on behalf of that wicked
> one? Rather, it teaches that **he caused leprosy to glow on him**…
> Rav said: **He gave him a dog.**

*Rabbi Yehuda and Rabbi Neḥemya: second century. Rav: third century.*
Elsewhere in the same passage, Abba Yose says God **made a horn grow on him**.

Four rabbis, four answers, no resolution — which tells you the text genuinely
does not say. Note that Rabbi Yehuda's guess works from Genesis 1:14, where the
lights are made to be "**signs**": he is reasoning from the word *ʾôt* itself,
not from anything in chapter 4.

The dog is the tenderest of the four. On this reading God's response to a man
terrified of dying alone in open country is to give him **something that will
stay with him**.

### *Genesis Rabbah* 22:12 — an argument for the defence

> Rabbi Neḥemya said: **Cain's sentence was unlike the sentence of murderers,
> as he had no one from whom he could learn.** From here on, "anyone who kills
> Cain" will be killed.

The first murderer had never seen a death, had no law, and had no precedent.
The midrash treats that as genuinely mitigating — and reads God's leniency as
**justice**, not merely mercy.

## 6. Repentance

### *Genesis Rabbah* 22:13 → *toward the session's reading*

> **Adam the first man encountered him.** He said to him: 'What became of your
> sentence?' He said to him: '**I repented and reached a settlement.**' Adam
> the first man began beating himself on the face, saying: '**Such is the
> power of repentance, and I did not know.**' Immediately, Adam the first man
> stood and said: "A psalm, a song for the Shabbat day…" (Psalms 92:1).

The first murderer walks out of his sentencing alive, meets his father on the
road, and tells him why. And the news that repentance *works* is such a shock
to the first man that he composes the Sabbath psalm on the spot.

### The same passage, the other way ⇢ *a different direction*

Honesty requires the dissent, which sits in the **same section**, immediately
before:

> Rabbi Berekhya said in the name of Rabbi Elazar ben Rabbi Shimon: He
> departed **as one who shows a split hoof**, and as one who deceives his
> Creator.

The pig displays its split hooves to look kosher while lacking the hidden
requirement. On this reading Cain's repentance is a performance. Rabbi Ḥama
adds that he left **joyfully** — having got away with it.

**The rabbis did not agree about whether Cain repented**, and the collection
preserves the disagreement side by side rather than settling it. That is worth
imitating.

### Targum Pseudo-Jonathan 4:24 → *toward the session's reading*

> For **Kain who sinned and was converted by repentance** (had protection)
> unto seven generations extended to him: and to Lemek, the son of his son,
> who hath not sinned, it is just that it shall be extended unto seventy and
> seven.

A second ancient witness, independent of the midrash, that Cain **repented**
— and that his protection was the result of it.

## 7. Lamech, and the spread of violence

### *Genesis Rabbah* 23:2 ⇢ *a different direction*

> Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi said: These are all expressions of rebellion. **Irad**
> – 'I will expel them from the world'; **Meḥuyael** – 'I will eradicate them
> from the world'; **Metushael** – 'I will uproot them from the world';
> **Lemekh** – 'What do I have to do with Lemekh and his offspring?'

*Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi: third century.* The genealogy read as a sentence of
judgement spelled out in names — the line announcing its own end.

### *Genesis Rabbah* 23:3 → *toward the session's reading*

On Tubal-Cain, the first metalworker:

> Rabbi Yehoshua of Sikhnin said in the name of Rabbi Levi: **This one improved
> upon [tibel] Cain's transgression; Cain killed, but he did not have an
> instrument with which to kill**, but this one was "a forger of every sharp
> instrument of bronze and iron."

A pun on the name, and a whole theory of technology inside it: the seventh
generation does not invent murder, it **industrialises** it. Cain had to
improvise. Tubal-Cain supplies the tool.

And on his sister:

> "And the sister of Tuval Cain was Naama." Rabbi Abba bar Kahana said:
> **Naama was Noah's wife.** Why did they call her Naama? It is because her
> actions were pleasing.

*Rabbi Abba bar Kahana: third–fourth century.* The tradition marries the last
named woman of Cain's line to the man who survives the flood — so that Cain's
line does not, after all, end.

### *Genesis Rabbah* 23:4 ⇢ *a different direction — but it turns on real Hebrew*

The English of 4:23 reads as a boast: "I have killed a man for wounding me."
Hebrew can also read the same words as a **question**, and the midrash does:

> "Have I slain a man for my wound," that wounds should befall me on his
> account?… Cain committed murder and yet [his punishment] was suspended for
> him for seven generations; **I, who did not kill anyone, all the more so**…
>
> **Rabbi says: This is a flawed a fortiori argument**: If so, from whom would
> the Holy One blessed be He ever collect His promissory note?

On this reading Lamech is not confessing a murder — he is arguing that since
even Cain got seven generations of protection, he, who has killed nobody, has
still more coming. Which is arguably worse: **he has turned God's restraint of
vengeance into a licence.** And the midrash's anonymous "Rabbi" immediately
calls the logic broken.

Either way the trajectory is the same. God set a limit at **seven**. Lamech
quotes the limit back, inflates it to **seventy-seven**, and makes a song of
it. What Jesus does with that number is on
[the earliest readings sheet](second-temple-sources.html#7-the-new-testament).

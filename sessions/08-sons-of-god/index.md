## A note before we begin

Four verses in Genesis have produced more strange books, late-night
documentaries and internet videos than any other four in the Bible. *The sons
of God saw the daughters of humankind… The Nephilim were on the earth in
those days.* Some of us were taught that the sons of God were the godly
descendants of Seth. Some of us were taught they were fallen angels. Some of
us have never heard the passage mentioned in church at all, because it is
awkward.

Christians have disagreed about these verses for eighteen hundred years,
sincerely, and no one here needs to pretend otherwise. This session makes a
case where the evidence leans, names who disagrees, and leaves you room. What
it asks is what we have asked all along: **read it the way its first hearers
would have** — people who knew their neighbours' stories about gods who
fathered heroes — and follow the words where the rest of the Bible takes
them. And then notice what Genesis itself does with the story, which is to
tell it in four verses and turn to something it cares about more.

## Aim of this session

Last time, the two family trees ran down to a man named *Rest*. Today, the
eight verses between the genealogy and the ark — the last thing Genesis tells
us about the world before the flood.

Five movements: the text, slowly; who the sons of God were; the Nephilim, and
where the giants went; how the story grew, among Israel's neighbours and in
the Jewish books of Jesus' day; and the paragraph Genesis puts right after it,
which is about two hearts.

## Part 1 — Read it slowly

> When humankind began to multiply on the face of the earth, and daughters
> were born to them, the sons of God **saw** that the daughters of humankind
> were **beautiful**. Thus they **took** wives for themselves **from any they
> chose**. So the LORD said, "My Spirit will not remain in humankind
> indefinitely, since they are mortal. They will remain for 120 more years."
> The Nephilim were on the earth in those days (and also after this) when the
> sons of God would sleep with the daughters of humankind, who gave birth to
> their children. They were the mighty heroes of old, the famous men.
> *(Genesis 6:1–4)*

**It opens with a blessing coming true.** *Began to multiply* is Genesis
1:28 — *be fruitful and multiply* — happening. Daughters are born. For one
verse, the world is doing what God said.

**Then three verbs you have heard before.** The NET says *beautiful*; the
Hebrew says ***good*** — *ṭôb*, God's word over his own work in Genesis 1.
And the sentence runs: they **saw** that they were **good**, and they
**took**. That is Genesis 3:6 exactly: *the woman **saw** that the tree was
**good** for food… and she **took**.* The first time a creature saw that
something was good and took it, it was fruit. Now it is people. Genesis
tells the sin of heaven in the grammar of the garden.

The Bible will use those three verbs again, and look at who uses them.
Pharaoh's officials *see* Sarai and she is *taken* (12:15). Shechem, *"prince
of the land,"* *sees* Dinah and *takes* her (34:2). King David *sees* a woman,
*"very good in appearance,"* and *takes* her (2 Samuel 11:2–4). After the
garden it is nearly always **rulers**, taking women because they can — and
Genesis 6:2 ends the same way: *from any they chose*. No one is asked.

**God answers with a limit.** *My Spirit will not remain in humankind
forever, since they are flesh* — the Hebrew word is *ʿōlām*, "forever," and
it is the word of Genesis 3:22: humans *must not… take also from the tree of
life and eat, and **live forever***. When humans reached up for what belonged
to heaven, God drew a line at *forever*. Now heaven has reached down, and he
draws the same line in the same place. (Whether the 120 years are a new limit
on a lifetime or the time left before the flood, Session 7 left open; both
readings are ancient.)

**And then the honour roll.** *They were the mighty heroes of old, the
famous men.* The Hebrew is *haggibbōrîm… ʾanšê haššēm* — "the warriors… **the
men of the name**." Listen to how that sounds: like a plaque in a hall of
fame. Every nation around Israel had such a plaque, and Part 4 reads some of
them. Genesis puts its version one verse before the LORD looks at the earth
and grieves.

**Notice what is not here.** No one in these four verses is called tall.

## Part 2 — Who are the sons of God?

Start with the Hebrew idiom, because it does most of the work. In Hebrew,
**"son of ___" usually means "one who belongs to the class ___."** A "son of a
year" is a year-old lamb. "The sons of the prophets" are prophets. A "son of
death" is a man who deserves to die. A "son of man" is a human being. So
*bənê hāʾĕlōhîm*, the sons of God, most naturally means **members of the
class *ʾĕlōhîm*** — divine beings — the way "sons of man" means human beings.

**And that is how the phrase is used everywhere else.** It and its close
cousins appear in eight verses of the Old Testament, and outside Genesis
every one of them is in heaven: the sons of God who *present themselves
before the LORD*, with Satan among them (Job 1:6; 2:1); who *shouted for joy*
with the morning stars when the world was made (Job 38:7); the *heavenly
beings* told to give the LORD glory (Psalm 29:1; 89:6); the *sons of the Most
High* judged in God's assembly (Psalm 82:6). The [Scripture
sheet](texts/elsewhere-in-scripture.html) prints them all.

### The heavenly court

Those verses belong to a picture the Old Testament paints again and again:
**God on a throne, with a court around him.** Micaiah sees *"the LORD sitting
on his throne, with all the heavenly assembly standing beside him on his
right and on his left,"* and hears the court discuss what to do about King
Ahab (1 Kings 22:19–22). Isaiah hears, in the throne room, *"Who will go on
**our** behalf?"* (6:8). Daniel sees *"thrones were set up… the court
convened"* (7:9–10). The court has several names — the *host* (army) *of
heaven*, the *holy ones*, the *council*, the *sons of God* — and scholars
call it **the divine council**.

Three things about this court matter for Genesis 6.

- **It is not a committee God needs.** Its members are made (*"he gave the
  command and they came into existence,"* Psalm 148:5) and they worship
  (Nehemiah 9:6). God is not one vote among many. He is a king, and a king
  has a court.
- **Its members can go wrong.** In Psalm 82, *"God stands in the assembly of
  El; in the midst of the gods he renders judgment"* — he condemns them for
  injustice and sentences them: *"you will die like mortals."*
- **It was the language of Israel's neighbours, reclaimed.** At Ugarit, a
  Canaanite city on the Syrian coast destroyed around 1185 BCE, the clay
  tablets call the gods *"the sons of El"* and *"the assembly of the gods,"*
  and number them **the seventy sons** of El's wife. (Genesis 10 counts
  seventy nations — and Deuteronomy 32:8, in the Dead Sea Scroll the NET
  follows, says God divided the nations *"according to the number of the
  heavenly assembly."*) Israel used the same words for the court of heaven
  and changed what they meant: at Ugarit the sons of El were gods who ruled;
  in Israel they are creatures who serve and can be judged. The
  [neighbours sheet](texts/the-neighbors.html) has the Ugaritic.

Genesis's own "**us**" belongs in this picture too — *"Let **us** make
humankind"* (1:26), *"the man has become like one of **us**"* (3:22), *"let
**us** go down"* (11:7). The rabbis heard God speaking to his court; the
church fathers heard the Trinity; both readings are old. Either way, notice
where the "us" speaks: at the border between heaven and earth.

### Three readings

Christians and Jews have read "the sons of God" three ways.

| | Heavenly beings | The line of Seth | Kings and nobles |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The claim** | Members of God's court left their place and took human wives | Seth's godly line married the women of Cain's line | Rulers who called themselves "sons of the gods" took any woman they wanted |
| **Oldest witnesses** | *1 Enoch* (3rd c. BCE), *Jubilees*, the Dead Sea Scrolls, Philo, Josephus; Jude, 2 Peter; Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian | Julius Africanus (c. 221), the Syriac church, then Augustine (c. 420) — the West's standard view through Luther and Calvin | The rabbis, from the 2nd century CE ("sons of the judges"); revived by Meredith Kline (1962) and John Walton |
| **Strongest point** | It is what the phrase means everywhere else | Keeps the story about human sin, which is where 6:3 and 6:5 put it | *Saw–took* is rulers' grammar; ancient kings did claim divine fathers |
| **Hardest problem** | How? — and Jesus says angels do not marry | "Sons of God" never means Sethites, and nothing in the text says "Cain" | The phrase never means kings, either |
{: .ff-wrap}

**The Seth reading** is the one many of us grew up with, and it has an
honourable history — Augustine, Chrysostom, Luther and Calvin held it. It is
not the oldest Christian reading: it first appears around 221, when Julius
Africanus offered it *"in my opinion"* — while his own Greek Bible read *"the
angels of heaven."* But it spread for reasons that still persuade: Jesus says
that in the resurrection people *"neither marry nor are given in marriage, but
are like angels in heaven"* (Matthew 22:30), and it seemed impossible that
spirits could father children. And it gets something profoundly right. As
Luther put it, *"the flood came not because the generation of Cain was
corrupt, but because the generation of the righteous… now had lapsed"* —
Genesis is not blaming the flood on anyone but us.

**The king reading** notices something real too: the grammar of 6:2 is the
grammar of Pharaoh and David, and the oldest Jewish comment on the passage —
Ben Sira's, around 180 BCE — calls the heroes of 6:4 *"the princes of old."*
The rabbis took it up for their own reasons ([one of them cursed anyone who
said "sons of God"](texts/rabbinic-sources.html)), and good evangelical
scholars hold it today.

**But we read with the New Testament, and the New Testament knows this
story.** Jude and 2 Peter do not quote Genesis 6. They do something more
telling: they retell it the way the Jewish books of their own day told it.

> You also know that **the angels who did not keep within their proper
> domain but abandoned their own place of residence**, he has kept in eternal
> chains in utter darkness, locked up for the judgment of the great Day. So
> also Sodom and Gomorrah… since they indulged in sexual immorality and
> pursued unnatural desire **in a way similar to these angels**… *(Jude 6–7)*

Angels who *left their place*; kept *in chains in darkness until the
judgment*; set beside Sodom's going after *"other flesh"* (Jude 7, literally)
*in a way similar to these angels*. Second Peter puts the same angels
**immediately before Noah and the flood** (2:4–5), and 1 Peter speaks of
*"spirits in prison… disobedient long ago… in the days of Noah"* (3:19–20).
Every one of those details is in *1 Enoch*'s telling of Genesis 6, and a few
lines later **Jude quotes *1 Enoch*** word for word (vv. 14–15). Most scholars
since the mid-twentieth century, evangelicals included, read Jude 6 and 2 Peter
2:4 this way, as does the NET Bible's own study note on Genesis 6:2. Readers
who hold the Seth view answer that these verses describe an earlier fall of
Satan's angels — a fair reply, but the harder reading of Jude. Even the rabbis
never fully let the angels go: Nachmanides conceded that the fallen-angel
reading *"fits into the language of the verse more than all other
interpretations."* As with Enoch last time, **Genesis leaves the door ajar, and
the New Testament walks through it**: the sons of God were heavenly beings who
left their place. The angels Jesus says do not marry are angels *in heaven*;
Jude's point is that these left. *How*, Genesis does not say — it tells us very
little on purpose.

## Part 3 — The Nephilim, and where the giants went

**The word.** *Nəpilîm* looks like it comes from *nāpal*, "to fall," and the
old guesses start there: *the fallen ones*, or *those who fall upon others*.
When Jews in Alexandria translated Genesis into Greek, around 250 BCE, they
wrote ***gigantes*** — and through the Latin that reached the King James as
***"There were giants in the earth in those days."***

But in Greek, *gigantes* did not mean "tall people." The Giants of Greek
myth were **the children of the Earth who made war on the gods of heaven**.
The translators reached for their world's name for beings who crossed the
line between heaven and earth. The size came later.

**Where size enters: a report the Bible calls evil.** The Nephilim appear by
name exactly once after the flood — in the mouths of the ten spies:

> "All the people we saw there are of great stature. **We even saw the
> Nephilim there** (the descendants of Anak came from the Nephilim), and **we
> seemed like grasshoppers** both to ourselves and to them." *(Numbers
> 13:32–33)*

Numbers calls this *dibbâ* — a "bad," "evil" report — and the men who gave it
*"died by the plague before the LORD"* (14:36–37). (The parenthesis linking
the Anakim to the Nephilim is not in the ancient Greek translation, and may be
an early scribe's explanation.) And the last clause turned out to be wrong. Forty
years later a woman in Jericho told two other spies what the Canaanites had
actually felt: *"We are absolutely terrified of you… we lost our courage"*
(Joshua 2:9–11). The spies saw giants and felt like grasshoppers; the people
of the land saw Israel and felt the same.

### Follow the thread

The Bible never loses track of the giants after that. It follows them to
their end.

- **The Anakim**, tall people around **Hebron** (Numbers 13:22; Deuteronomy
  9:2). Caleb — the one spy besides Joshua who did not bring the evil report
  — asks for their city at eighty-five: *"assuming the LORD is with me, I
  will conquer them"* (Joshua 14:12).
- **The Rephaim**, Deuteronomy's name for the old tall peoples of the land —
  *"the Moabites call them Emites… the Ammonites call them Zamzummites"*
  (Deuteronomy 2:10–11, 20–21). *"Only King Og of Bashan was left of the
  remaining Rephaites"* — with his famous iron "bed," thirteen and a half feet
  long (3:11).
- **Gath.** The last Anakim *"remained in Gaza, Gath, and Ashdod"* (Joshua
  11:22). Goliath is *"from Gath"* (1 Samuel 17:4). And 2 Samuel 21 lists four
  more giants of Gath, *"descendants of Rapha,"* one with six fingers on each
  hand — *"killed by David and his soldiers."*

After David, the giants are gone from the story. The thread that started in
Genesis 6 ends with a shepherd boy and his men.

**How tall was Goliath?** Your Bible may differ, and both are honest. The
traditional Hebrew text says *"six cubits and a span"* — about nine and a half
feet (KJV, NIV, ESV). A Dead Sea Scroll copy of Samuel, the Greek Bible and
Josephus say *"four cubits and a span"* — about six foot nine — and the NET
follows them: *"close to seven feet tall."* Either way, he was a giant to the
men who had to face him.

### Og, the last of the Rephaim

Here archaeology does something striking. The Hebrew word *rəpāʾîm* has a
**second meaning**: it is also the Bible's word for **the dead** — the shades
in Sheol. *"It rouses the spirits of the dead (rəpāʾîm) for you, all the
former leaders of the earth"* (Isaiah 14:9). Why would one word mean both a
giant people and the ghosts of kings?

The tablets from **Ugarit** answer it. There the *rpum* are **the royal
dead** — deified ancestors, summoned in the liturgy for a king's funeral. Two
Phoenician kings of Sidon (c. 500 BCE) had curses carved on their stone
coffins against grave-robbers: *"may they have no resting-place with the
Shades"* — the word is *rpʾm*, **Rephaim**. And one Ugaritic tablet blesses
*Rapiʾu*, "the eternal king," *"the god who dwells in ʿAṯtarat, the god who
rules in Hdrʿy."* Now read Joshua 12:4: Og *"lived in **Ashtaroth and
Edrei**."* If those are the same two towns — many scholars think so, others
read the line as divine names — then Og's cities were where the Canaanites
enthroned the king of the dead.

And Og's land of Bashan, the Golan today, is covered with **about five
thousand dolmens** — great stone tables of basalt, prehistoric tombs, found
almost nowhere west of the Jordan. His "iron bed," which the NET calls a
*sarcophagus*, may have been a basalt coffin (Arabs east of the Jordan still
called basalt *iron* in the 1890s), or one of those dolmens, or a relic bed
overlaid with iron. To an Israelite, Og was the last king of a
legendary race whose ghosts ruled below, in a land that looked like a
graveyard of giants. ([The neighbours sheet](texts/the-neighbors.html) has the
texts.)

### "Were there really giants?"

It is a fair question, and the ancient world thought it had evidence. An
Egyptian scribe of Ramesses II's day, mocking a colleague who did not know the
roads of Canaan, warns of Shasu nomads in a mountain pass, *"some of them… of
four cubits or of five cubits, from head to foot, fierce of face"* (*Papyrus
Anastasi I*) — seven or eight feet. People saw **enormous walls** they could
not imagine men building: at Gath, Goliath's city, the Philistine walls were
four metres thick, of stones one to two metres long, and they stood in ruins
for centuries — the excavator thinks they helped the giant stories along. And
they found **enormous bones**. Josephus says the giants' bones at Hebron were
*"still shewn to this very day"*; the emperor Augustus decorated his villa on
Capri with *"the monstrous bones of huge sea monsters and wild beasts, called
the 'bones of the giants'"*; Augustine saw *"on the shore at Utica a man's
molar tooth of such a size, that if it were cut down into teeth such as we
have, a hundred, I fancy, could have been made out of it."* We now know these were the fossils of mammoths and
other great animals, and ancient people read them the only way they could.
(The photographs of giant skeletons you may have seen online began as a 2002
Photoshop-contest entry built on a picture of a **mastodon** dig; *National
Geographic* traced the hoax in 2007.)

So what does the Bible actually claim? **Soberly: that there were very tall
warrior peoples in Canaan, remembered as the heirs of an older legend — and
that they fell.** To a man of eighty-five, and to a boy with a sling.

**And how did Nephilim survive the flood?** The rabbis said **Og** did. One
Aramaic translation makes him *"the one who escaped"* of Genesis 14:13 — *"Og…
who had been spared from the giants that died in the deluge, and had ridden
protected upon the top of the ark"* — and an eighth-century midrash tells it
like a folk tale: Og *"sat down on a piece of wood under the gutter of the ark,"*
swore to be Noah's servant, and Noah *"bored an aperture in the ark, and he put
(through it) his food daily for him."* Others read *"and also after this"* (6:4) to mean the sons of God did it
again. Others notice that the only post-flood witness is the evil report:
the spies reached for the most frightening word they knew, the way we might
call a big man *a Goliath*. Genesis does not say, and you may hold any of
these.

## Part 4 — How the story grew

### The neighbours' honour roll

*The mighty heroes of old, the men of the name.* To Israel's first hearers,
that line described a whole shelf of famous stories, because **in the ancient
world, a divine parent was how you explained a hero.**

**Gilgamesh**, the most famous hero of Mesopotamia, was a king of Uruk
after the flood — *"Two-thirds of him are divine,"* says the epic — and the
Sumerian King List calls his father a *lillu*, a phantom. A copy found at Ugarit
makes him **eleven cubits tall**. The epic opens with his people crying to
the gods that he *"leaveth no maid to her mother… nor a spouse to a
husband"*; an older tablet says the king takes the bride **first, and the
bridegroom afterwards**. (Hear Genesis 6:2: *they took wives for themselves
from any they chose.*) And when he sets out to kill the monster Humbaba, he
says why: *"If I fall, I will establish my name."* **A man of the name.**

The Greeks told it too. The poet **Hesiod**, around 700 BCE, remembers *"a
god-like race of hero-men who are called demi-gods, the race before our
own,"* destroyed by *"grim war and dread battle."* And a papyrus fragment of the
*Catalogue of Women*, a poem long credited to him, says that Zeus resolved *"to destroy the lives of the demi-gods,
that the children of the gods should not mate with wretched mortals"* — and
sent storm over the earth. Set that beside Genesis 6:3. In both, the high God
draws a line so that heaven and earth stop mixing. (The difference is as
plain: Zeus wants the gods left apart; the LORD, two verses later, grieves.)

Where the nations saw the founders of their cities and the fathers of their
kings, **Genesis sees the last straw before the flood.** It keeps the plaque
and hangs it on the wall of a house about to be washed away.

### The Watchers

In the centuries before Jesus, Jews told the Genesis 6 story at length. The
oldest version is the **"Book of the Watchers,"** the first part of ***1
Enoch***, written in Aramaic in the third century BCE — the book Jude quotes.
The angels are called **Watchers**, from the word Daniel uses for the *holy
sentinel* who *came down from heaven* (Daniel 4:13).

> And the angels, the children of the heaven, saw and lusted after them, and
> said to one another: 'Come, let us choose us wives from among the children
> of men and beget us children.'… And they were in all two hundred; who
> **descended in the days of Jared on the summit of Mount Hermon**, and they
> called it Mount Hermon, because they had sworn and bound themselves by
> mutual imprecations upon it. *(1 Enoch 6:2, 6)*

Two Hebrew puns: they came down in the days of **Jared** because *yārad*
means *to come down*, and swore on **Hermon** because *ḥērem* means *a curse*.
Hermon is the great mountain at the head of the Jordan — **Og's mountain**, as
Joshua 12:5 says. The angels take wives, and they **teach**:

> And Azâzêl taught men to make **swords, and knives, and shields**, and
> breastplates, and made known to them **the metals of the earth and the art
> of working them**, and bracelets, and ornaments, and the use of antimony,
> and **the beautifying of the eyelids**… Semjâzâ taught **enchantments**,
> and root-cuttings… Barâqîjâl (taught) **astrology**… *(1 Enoch 8:1, 3)*

Their children are *"great giants, whose height was three thousand ells"* —
nearly a mile. They devour everything, then people, until *"the earth laid
accusation against the lawless ones."* God has the archangels **bind** the
Watchers *"in the darkness… till the day of their judgement,"* and sets the
giants against each other. And their spirits become **the evil spirits** that
trouble the earth — which is where many Jews of Jesus' day thought demons came
from. Read Jude 6 and 2 Peter 2:4 again after this, and you can hear it.

Two things to notice. First, what the Watchers teach is **the Babylonian
sages' curriculum** from last session — metalwork, the arts, the signs of the
sky. Babylon said civilisation was a gift brought by sages from beyond; *1
Enoch* agrees that it came from beyond, and says it was **stolen goods**.
Second, among the Dead Sea Scrolls is a companion work, the **Book of
Giants**, and its giants have names. Two of them are **Gilgamesh** and
**Hobabish** — Humbaba, the monster Gilgamesh killed (fragments 4Q203, 4Q530
and 4Q531). Jewish scribes two centuries before Christ put Mesopotamia's greatest hero on
the list of the Nephilim. They heard *"the men of the name"* exactly the way
this session suggests the first hearers did.

### What Genesis leaves out

Now set *1 Enoch*'s thirty chapters beside Genesis's four verses. Genesis
gives the sons of God **no names, no leader, no mountain, no oath, no
teaching, no size, no punishment and no demons.** It tells one sentence of
what they did, one sentence of what God said, one sentence of who their
children were — and then it turns, in verse 5, to *humankind*.

That is the deepest difference. ***1 Enoch* explains the world's evil as
something done *to* humanity from above**: angels came down, seduced, taught,
corrupted. **Genesis will not let that be the explanation.** Its verdict
(6:3) falls on *humankind*, its diagnosis (6:5) on *the human heart*. The New
Testament uses that story; Genesis refuses to make it an excuse.

(Both traditions later backed away from the angels — the rabbis in the
second century, setting the Enoch books aside just as Christians were taking
them up, and making the sons of God *"sons of the judges"*; the church from
the third, as the Seth reading spread and Augustine ruled the Enoch books out
of Scripture. The [rabbinic](texts/rabbinic-sources.html) and
[church](texts/church-fathers.html) sheets have both.)

something goes wrong — in the world, or in us? Why is the first so

## Part 5 — Two hearts

> But the LORD **saw** that the wickedness of humankind had become great on
> the earth. Every inclination of the thoughts of their **minds** was only
> evil all the time. The LORD **regretted** that he had made humankind on the
> earth, and **he was highly offended**. So the LORD said, "I will wipe
> humankind, whom I have created, from the face of the earth—everything from
> humankind to animals, including creatures that move on the ground and birds
> of the air, for I regret that I have made them." But Noah found **favor** in
> the sight of the LORD. *(Genesis 6:5–8)*

**Count who has been seeing.** *God saw all that he had made — and it was
very good* (1:31). *The sons of God saw that the daughters of humankind were
good, and took* (6:2). *The LORD saw that the wickedness of humankind had
become great* (6:5). Three looks at the same world.

**The human heart.** The NET's *minds* is the Hebrew *heart* — where you
think and choose, not only where you feel. And *inclination* is *yēṣer*, a
noun from the potter's verb of Genesis 2:7, *"the LORD God **formed** the
man."* The creature God formed is now forming things of its own, and every
shape it makes is *only evil, all the day*. The rabbis built their teaching
of the *yēṣer hāraʿ*, the evil inclination, on this verse.

**God's heart.** Verse 6 ends on the same word as verse 5: *his heart*. The
NET's *he was highly offended* hides the verb. It is *wayyitʿaṣṣēb*, from
***ʿeṣeb***, **pain** — *it pained him to his heart.* And now listen back:

- *"With **pain** you will give birth… in **painful toil** you will eat"*
  (3:16–17). The curse.
- *"This one will bring us **comfort** from… the **painful toil** of our
  hands"* (5:29). Lamech, naming Noah.
- *"The LORD **regretted**… it **pained** him to his heart"* (6:6).

*Regretted* and *comfort* are the same Hebrew verb, *niḥam*. Lamech hoped
his son would bring *comfort* from the *pain* of the cursed ground. The next
time Genesis uses both words, **the one feeling them is God.** The pain the
curse laid on the woman and the man, God takes into his own heart. Isaiah
says it of Israel in the wilderness — *"they rebelled and **grieved** his Holy
Spirit"* (63:10, the same root) — and Paul echoes Isaiah: *"do not grieve the
Holy Spirit of God"* (Ephesians 4:30).

**Can God regret?** First Samuel 15 answers with both hands. *"I regret that
I have made Saul king,"* says the LORD (15:11); *"the Preeminent One of
Israel does not… change his mind, for he is not a human being"* (15:29); and
*"the LORD regretted that he had made Saul king"* (15:35). The same chapter
holds both on purpose: God does not waver the way we do, and God is not
unmoved. A second-century rabbi was asked the sharp form of the question — *if
God knows the future, how can Scripture say he was grieved?* — and answered
with a question of his own: when your son was born, did you rejoice? *"But did
you not know that he would ultimately die?"* The answer came back: *"At a time
of rejoicing there is rejoicing; at a time of mourning there is mourning."* A
later rabbi finished the thought: *"Seven days the Holy One blessed be He
mourned His world before the Flood"* — and proved it from the same verb used
when David wept for Absalom: *"The king is **grieved** over his son"* (2 Samuel
19:2). The grief of Genesis 6:6 is **a father's grief over a son**
(*Genesis Rabbah* 27:4).

**Wiped from the ground.** *I will wipe* is the verb for wiping a dish. The
*ʾādām* taken from the *ʾădāmâ* is to be wiped off the face of it, and the
list — humans, animals, creeping things, birds — runs backward through the
days of Genesis 1. This is creation being taken down.

**"But Noah."** In Hebrew, *Noah* is two letters, נח, and *favor* — *ḥēn* — is
the same two letters reversed, חן. And this is **the first time the word
*grace* appears in the Bible.** Notice the order. Verse 8: *Noah found
grace*. Only verse 9: *Noah was a godly man… he walked with God.* The first
time Scripture names grace, it comes before anything is said about the man
who receives it. The rabbis saw it: *"even Noah… it is not that he was
worthy, but rather that he found favor"* (*Genesis Rabbah* 28:9). Hebrews
reads Noah the same way: he *"became an heir of the righteousness that comes
by faith"* (11:7).

Then verse 9 begins a new heading and tells why the flood came a second time,
in other words — *God* rather than *the LORD*, *violence* rather than the
*heart*. That doubled telling is where Session 9 begins.

## So what?

**1. Genesis will not let the monsters take the blame.** It tells its
neighbours' most glorious story — gods fathering heroes, the men of the name
— in four verses, and then puts the problem where it belongs: *every
inclination of the human heart*. The later books made the flood the angels'
fault. Genesis made it ours.

**2. The line between heaven and earth is God's to cross — and he crosses it
the other way.** In Genesis 6, beings from heaven come down and *take*; their
children are heroes *of the name*. In the Gospel, *"God sent out his Son,
**born of a woman**"* (Galatians 4:4) — heaven comes down not to take a wife
but to be born of one — *"to all who have received him… he has given the
right to become God's children"* (John 1:12). And the one who *"did not
regard equality with God as something to be grasped"* is the one God *"gave
the name that is above every name"* (Philippians 2:6, 9). *(That line of
thought is ours; the texts are the Bible's.)*

**3. The first thing God feels about a world gone wrong is pain.** Not
irritation, not surprise — *ʿeṣeb*, the pain word of the curse, *to his
heart*. Judgment in Genesis comes from a God who hurts.

**4. Grace is named before righteousness.** The first time the Bible says
*favor*, the man who finds it has not yet been described.

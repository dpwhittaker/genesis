# The neighbours: Ugarit, Gilgamesh, Hesiod, and the ground itself

A source sheet for **Session 8**. The biblical passages are on **[the
Scripture sheet](elsewhere-in-scripture.html)**; the Jewish retellings on
**[the earliest readings](second-temple-sources.html)**. This sheet gathers
what Israel's **neighbours** said — in Canaan, Mesopotamia, Egypt and Greece —
and what has come **out of the ground**.

**Why these matter here.** Genesis 6:1–4 uses words its first hearers already
knew from other people's stories: a divine assembly of "sons of God"; heroes
born of gods and women; the *Rephaim*; "the men of the name." Reading the
neighbours shows what those words meant in the air Israel breathed — and how
differently Genesis uses them. And the archaeology answers a question every
class asks: *why did ancient people believe in giants?*

A word on dates. Ugarit's tablets are **older than any book of the Bible**
(13th century BCE). The Gilgamesh traditions are older still. The Greek
poets are roughly contemporary with Israel's monarchy. None of this tells us
who borrowed from whom; it tells us what the words sounded like.

---

## 1. The divine assembly at Ugarit

**Ugarit** was a Canaanite port on the Syrian coast, destroyed around 1185 BCE.
In 1928 a farmer's plough broke into a tomb there, and the excavations since
have recovered thousands of clay tablets in a Semitic language close to Hebrew.
Their myths describe the god **El** — the same word as Hebrew *ʾēl*, "God" —
presiding over a council of gods who are his **sons**.

The Ugaritic phrases, with the standard text numbers (KTU) and **our working
translation**:

| Ugaritic | Meaning | Where | Compare |
|---|---|---|---|
| *bn il*, *bn ilm* | "the sons of El," "the sons of the gods" | KTU 1.16 V; 1.40; 1.65 | *bənê hāʾĕlōhîm*, Gen 6:2; Job 1:6 |
| *pḫr bn ilm* | "the assembly of the sons of El" | KTU 1.4 III 14 | "the heavenly assembly," Ps 89:5 |
| *pḫr mʿd* | "the assembly of meeting" | KTU 1.2 I 14, 15, 20, 31 | "the mountain of assembly," Isa 14:13 |
| *ʿdt ilm* | "the assembly of El / of the gods" | KTU 1.15 II 7, 11 | *ʿădat-ʾēl*, "the assembly of El," Ps 82:1 |
| *dr bn il* | "the circle of the sons of El" | KTU 1.40 | "all who surround him," Ps 89:7 |
| *pḫr kkbm* | "the assembly of the stars" | KTU 1.10 I 4 | "the morning stars… all the sons of God," Job 38:7 |
{: .ff-wrap}

And one number. When Baal's palace is finished he throws a feast, and the
guests are his brothers, **"the seventy sons of Athirat"** (*šbʿm bn aṯrt*,
KTU 1.4 VI 46) — the sons of El and his wife, the whole second tier of gods.
Genesis 10's Table of Nations lists **seventy** nations. And Deuteronomy 32:8,
in the Dead Sea Scroll the NET follows, says God divided the nations
*"according to the number of the heavenly assembly."* Scholars from Emanuel
Tov to Michael Heiser have drawn the line between the two.

**What Israel changed.** The vocabulary is the same; the picture is not. At
Ugarit the sons of El are **gods** — they rule, feast, fight, and die and rise.
In Israel the court is **made** (*"he gave the command and they came into
existence,"* Psalm 148:5), it **worships** (Nehemiah 9:6), and it can be
**judged** — which is exactly what Psalm 82 does, in Ugarit's own words: *"God
stands in the assembly of El; in the midst of the gods he renders judgment."*

## 2. The Rephaim: the royal dead

The Hebrew word *rəpāʾîm* means both **an ancient race of giants** (Og is
"the last of the Rephaim") and **the dead in Sheol** (Isaiah 14:9; Job 26:5).
Ugarit and Phoenicia explain the second meaning.

### At Ugarit

In the Ugaritic tablets the *rpum* are **the deified dead — especially dead
kings**. A royal funeral liturgy (KTU 1.161), from the reign of the last king
of Ugarit, summons the *rpum* of the underworld and the kings who have died to
come to the feast for the newly dead king, and calls on the sun-goddess to
find him among them. Scholars disagree over details, but that the *rpum* are
the royal dead is close to consensus.

One short tablet (KTU 1.108, 13th century BCE) begins with a blessing on
**Rapiʾu, "the eternal king"** (*rpu mlk ʿlm*), and then — on one reading —
names where he lives: **"the god who dwells in ʿAṯtarat, the god who rules in
Hdrʿy."** In 1970 Baruch Margulis pointed out that the Bible's **Og**, "one of
the few remaining Rephaites," lived **"in Ashtaroth and Edrei"** (Joshua 12:4;
Deuteronomy 1:4). If *Hdrʿy* is Edrei, then Og's two cities are the two cities
where the Canaanites enthroned the king of the dead.

**Be fair to the other side.** The match is contested. The spelling *Hdrʿy*
does not easily become *Edrei* (an *h* for an *ʾ* is not a usual change), and
the tablet has a word-divider that lets the line be read differently: *"the
god who sits with Athtart, the god who judges with Hadd the shepherd"* — divine
names, not towns. Both readings are in print; the parallel is striking but
not proven. (L. Quick, "Og, King of Bashan: Underworld Ruler or Ancient
Giant?", TheTorah.com, 2020, lays out both.)

### Phoenician coffins from Sidon

Two kings of Sidon, buried in the late sixth or early fifth century BCE, had
curses carved on their stone coffins against anyone who disturbed them. Both
use the word *rpʾm* — Rephaim — for the dead. G. A. Cooke's 1903 translation
renders it **"shades"**:

> I, Tabnith, priest of 'Ashtart, king of the Sidonians… lie in this coffin:
> My [curse be] with whatsoever man thou art that bringest forth this coffin!
> Do not, do not open me, nor disquiet me, for I have not indeed (?) silver, I
> have not indeed (?) gold, nor any jewels… And if thou do at all open me, and
> at all disquiet me, **mayest thou have no seed among the living under the
> sun nor resting-place among the shades!** — the coffin of **Tabnit**, found
> 1887 (Istanbul Archaeology Museums)

> …For every prince and every man who shall open this resting-place, or who
> shall take away the coffin of my resting-place… **may they have no
> resting-place with the Shades**, nor be buried in a grave, nor have son or
> seed in their stead. — the coffin of **Eshmunazar II**, found 1855 (the
> Louvre)

The Louvre's own French translation of the second reads *"avec les
**Rephaïm**."* To a Phoenician, the worst thing you could wish on a man was
that he never join the Rephaim.

## 3. Og's country

### Tombs everywhere

Og ruled **Bashan** — the Golan Heights and the land east of the Sea of
Galilee. It is basalt country, and it is covered with prehistoric tombs. A
2017 survey reports that in the Golan alone *"approximately 5200 structures
defined as megalithic burials were identified, most of which are dolmens"* —
great stone tables of basalt slabs, mostly from about 2350–2000 BCE — while
*"almost no dolmens have been found west of the Jordan Rift Valley"* (Sharon
et al., *PLOS ONE*, 2017). An Israelite crossing the Jordan into Og's kingdom
walked into a landscape of giant graves, built by no one he knew.

In the middle of it stands **Rujm el-Hiri**, a stone circle 156 metres across
with a great cairn at its centre, about 5,000 years old (the exact date is
disputed; a burial was added around 1400 BCE). Its Arabic name means "stone
heap of the wild cat." Modern Israelis call it *Gilgal Refaʾim*, "the wheel of
the Rephaim" — a modern name, which proves nothing about what the ancients
thought, but shows how naturally the land invites the idea.

### Og's "iron bed"

> …Only King Og of Bashan was left of the remaining Rephaites. (It is
> noteworthy that his sarcophagus was made of iron. Does it not, indeed, still
> remain in Rabbath of the Ammonites? It is 13½ feet long and 6 feet wide
> according to standard measure.) — *Deuteronomy 3:11* (NET)

The Hebrew says ***bed*** (*ʿereś*) — "a bed of iron." Three serious
explanations have been offered, and none is provable:

- **A basalt coffin.** S. R. Driver (1895): *"ancient sarcophagi of black
  basalt are found in great numbers in the country E. of Jordan… By iron is
  meant probably the black basalt of the country, which actually contains a
  proportion of iron (about 20 per cent.), and, as Pliny remarked, has the
  colour and hardness of iron."* He adds that *"the Arabs on the east of Jordan
  still call basalt iron."*
- **A dolmen** — one of those stone tables, which does look like a giant's
  bed (Timo Veijola).
- **A real bed overlaid with iron** — in the Bronze Age iron was rare and
  precious and used as decoration, and an ancient royal bed kept as a relic
  would be exactly such a thing (Alan Millard, "King Og's Bed and Other
  Ancient Ironmongery," 1988). *(Session 7's question about Tubal-Cain's iron
  comes round again.)*

### Mount Hermon

Joshua says Og *"ruled over Mount Hermon"* (12:5; also 13:11–12) — the
mountain where *1 Enoch* 6 lands the two hundred Watchers. Hermon was a holy
mountain long before and long after. Near the ruined temple on its summit,
Charles Warren found in 1869 a stone with a rough Greek inscription, now in the
British Museum, from about the third century CE. Charles Clermont-Ganneau
(1903) read it as: *"By order of the most great and holy god, those who take
the oath — from here!"* (our English from his French). He was the first to
connect it with *1 Enoch*, where the Watchers *"swore and bound themselves by
mutual imprecations"* on Hermon. The stone is five centuries later than *1
Enoch* and names no Watchers; what it shows is that Hermon's summit was **a
place of oaths**.

## 4. Heroes born of gods

### Gilgamesh

The hero of the most famous epic of the ancient world was a king of **Uruk**
(the biblical **Erech**, Genesis 10:10) who, the poem says, was more than a
man. R. Campbell Thompson's translation (1928):

> **Two-thirds of him are divine, and [one-third of him human,]**
> — *Gilgamesh*, Tablet I (Standard Babylonian version)

His mother was the goddess Ninsun. The **Sumerian King List** gives him a
stranger father:

> **Gilgameš, whose father was a phantom (?)**, the lord of Kulaba, ruled for
> 126 years. — ETCSL 2.1.1, lines 112–115

The word is *lil-la*, a ghost or demon. And he was a **giant**: a copy of the
epic found at Ugarit (c. 1200 BCE) says he was **eleven cubits tall** —
around sixteen feet — with a chest four cubits wide (the lines have no
public-domain translation; A. R. George, *Aula Orientalis* 25, 2007).

**He took whatever he chose.** The epic opens with the people of Uruk crying
out to the gods against their king:

> Gilgamish leaveth no son to [his] father, [his] arrogance swelling
> (Each) day and [night]…
> **[Gilgamish] leaveth no [maid to her mother, nor] daughter to [hero],
> [(Nay), nor a spouse to a husband]** — Tablet I, col. II (Thompson 1928)

An older tablet of the epic (the Old Babylonian "Pennsylvania tablet," c. 1800
BCE) is sharper. The public-domain translations of 1917 and 1920 misread its
key lines; modern editions read them as a man on his way to a wedding
explaining that the king will lie with the bride **first, and the bridegroom
afterwards** — by divine decree (A. R. George, *The Babylonian Gilgamesh
Epic*, 2003; electronic Babylonian Library, 2022). Set that beside Genesis 6:2,
*"they took wives for themselves from any they chose,"* and beside Rabbi
Yudan's comment on it: *"the prominent man would enter and consort with her
first"* (*Genesis Rabbah* 26:5, on [the rabbinic sheet](rabbinic-sources.html)).
This is the strongest ancient evidence for the "kings" reading of the sons of
God.

**And he wanted a name.** When Gilgamesh sets out to kill the monster
**Humbaba** (Huwawa), his friend is afraid, and he answers — in Jastrow and
Clay's 1920 translation of the Old Babylonian Yale tablet:

> Wherever terror is to be faced,
> Thou, forsooth, art in fear of death.
> Thy prowess lacks strength.
> I will go before thee.
> Though thy mouth calls to me; "thou art afraid to approach."
> **If I fall, I will establish my name.** — lines 143–148

And a few lines later: *"I will, indeed, establish my name"* (160); *"A name I
will establish"* (187). The Akkadian of the later lines is *šuma šaṭarû*, "a
written name" — a name that lasts. Jastrow and Clay noted *"the interesting
phrase, 'writing a name' in the sense of acquiring 'fame.'"*

Two-thirds god, a giant, a king who takes the brides of his city, who goes to
war *to make a name*. That is what *"the mighty heroes of old, the men of the
name"* sounded like — and, two centuries before Christ, the Dead Sea Scrolls'
**Book of Giants** put **Gilgamesh and Ḥobabish (Humbaba)** among the
Nephilim by name (see [the earliest readings](second-temple-sources.html)).

### The sages of mixed descent

Session 7 met the seven ***apkallu***, the sages who brought civilisation to
the kings before the flood. A Babylonian ritual text, *Bīt mēseri*, goes on to
list four sages **after** the flood who were **"of human descent"** — and the
last of them, Lu-Nanna, was **"two-thirds a sage,"** the same fraction as
Gilgamesh's divinity. Two of the four **angered the gods**. And in the *Erra
Epic* the god Marduk says that at the flood he **sent the sages down to the
Apsû**, the deep, and did not let them come up. Amar Annus ("On the Origin of
Watchers," *Journal for the Study of the Pseudepigrapha*, 2010) and Anne
Kilmer (1987) argue that the Watchers of *1 Enoch* are a Jewish answer to
these sages: beings from beyond who taught forbidden knowledge, fathered a
half-divine line, and were **bound in the deep**. It is a scholarly proposal,
well argued, not a consensus. (No open translation of these texts exists; they
are described, not quoted.)

### The Greeks: demigods, and a god who ended them

The Greek poet **Hesiod** (c. 700 BCE) tells of five races of humankind. The
fourth, just before our own, was the race of heroes. H. G. Evelyn-White's
translation (1914):

> But when earth had covered this generation also, Zeus the son of Cronos made
> yet another, the fourth, upon the fruitful earth, which was nobler and more
> righteous, **a god-like race of hero-men who are called demi-gods**, the race
> before our own, throughout the boundless earth. **Grim war and dread battle
> destroyed a part of them**… But to the others father Zeus the son of Cronos
> gave a living and an abode apart from men, and made them dwell at the ends of
> earth. — *Works and Days* 156–169

And in a papyrus fragment of the *Catalogue of Women* (6th century BCE),
Zeus decides to end them — and says why:

> Now all the gods were divided through strife; for at that very time Zeus who
> thunders on high was meditating marvellous deeds, even to mingle storm and
> tempest over the boundless earth, and already he was hastening to make an
> utter end of the race of mortal men, **declaring that he would destroy the
> lives of the demi-gods, that the children of the gods should not mate with
> wretched mortals**, seeing their fate with their own eyes; but that **the
> blessed gods henceforth even as aforetime should have their living and their
> habitations apart from men.** — *Catalogue of Women*, fr. 68

Set that beside Genesis 6:3: *"My Spirit will not remain in humankind
forever."* In both, the high God draws a line so that heaven and earth **stop
mixing** — and a great destruction follows. The differences are just as
plain. Zeus's reason is to keep the gods comfortable and apart; the LORD's,
in the next verses, is grief over what humankind has become.

### What "giant" meant in Greek

When the Greek Bible put *gigantes* in Genesis 6:4, a Greek reader did not
hear "tall people." In Hesiod the Giants are born of **Earth**, from the blood
of the sky-god Ouranos — *"the great Giants with gleaming armour, holding long
spears in their hands"* (*Theogony* 185). The old Liddell–Scott lexicon defines
them as *"the Giants, a savage race destroyed by the gods… the sons of Gaia."*
And in the famous war of the Giants against the gods, Apollodorus says,
*"Earth, vexed on account of the Titans, brought forth the giants… And they
darted rocks and burning oaks at the sky"* (*Library* 1.6.1, trans. J. G.
Frazer, 1921). A *gigas* was an **earth-born rebel against heaven** — a
fitting word, as it happens, for Genesis 6. (The Greek Bible also uses
*gigantes* for the *Rephaim* — both the old peoples and, in Isaiah 14:9 and
Job 26:5, the dead.)

### Tartarus

Hesiod also describes where the gods of heaven chained the **Titans** who
fought them:

> …and buried them beneath the wide-pathed earth, **and bound them in bitter
> chains** when they had conquered them by their strength for all their great
> spirit, as far beneath the earth to Tartarus. For a brazen anvil falling down
> from heaven nine nights and days would reach the earth upon the tenth… There
> by the counsel of Zeus who drives the clouds **the Titan gods are hidden
> under misty gloom**, in a dank place where are the ends of the huge earth.
> — *Theogony* 717–731

That is the word 2 Peter 2:4 uses for the angels who sinned — God
***"cast them into Tartarus"*** and *"locked them up in chains in utter
darkness."* Peter took his readers' word for the prison of heaven's rebels
and filled it with a different story.

## 5. Giants in Canaan: what the texts and the ground say

### An Egyptian scribe meets the Shasu

**Papyrus Anastasi I** is a satirical letter from the reign of Ramesses II (13th
century BCE), in which one Egyptian scribe mocks another's ignorance of the
roads of Canaan. Describing a dangerous pass, he writes (A. H. Gardiner,
1911):

> The(?) narrow defile is infested (?) with Shosu concealed beneath the bushes;
> **some of them are of four cubits or of five cubits, from head(??) to
> foot(?), fierce of face**, their heart is not mild, and they hearken not to
> coaxing. — *Papyrus Anastasi I* 23,7–24,1

Gardiner's note: *"i. e. 6 foot 10 inches to 8 foot 6 inches… as with due
allowance for Egyptian exaggeration it well may do."* The Shasu were
semi-nomads of the Canaanite hills. An Egyptian, like the ten spies, came home
with a story about very tall men.

### *ʿAnaq* in Egyptian curses?

Egyptian **Execration Texts** (20th–18th centuries BCE) — names of enemies
written on pottery and figurines that were then smashed — curse a people
called ***Iy-ʿnq*** and three of its rulers. Since W. F. Albright (1928) some
scholars have connected them with the **Anakim**. The connection is possible,
not firm; the *Encyclopaedia Judaica* says plainly that *"it is not known"*
whether they are related.

### Goliath and Gath

- **His height.** The traditional Hebrew text of 1 Samuel 17:4 says *"six
  cubits and a span"* (about 9'9"); the oldest Hebrew manuscript of the verse
  (4QSam<sup>a</sup>, 1st century BCE), the Greek Bible and Josephus say
  *"four cubits and a span"* (about 6'9"). The NET follows the older reading:
  *"close to seven feet tall."* (J. D. Hays, *JETS* 48, 2005; a reply defending
  the taller figure, C. Billington, *JETS* 50, 2007.)
- **Goliath-like names at Gath.** In 2005 excavators at **Tell eṣ-Ṣâfī**,
  biblical Gath, found a potsherd from about the 10th century BCE scratched with
  two names, ***ʾLWT*** and ***WLT*** — not Goliath's name, as the newspapers
  said, but names of the same foreign type as *Golyat*, current in Goliath's
  city in roughly his period (Maeir et al., *BASOR* 351, 2008).
- **Walls that only giants could build.** Excavations at Gath in 2019 exposed
  the 11th-century BCE Philistine city's walls: about **four metres thick**,
  built of stones one to two metres long, twice the thickness of the later
  walls. The excavator, Aren Maeir, argues that these ruins — still standing
  long after Gath was destroyed — *"may very likely"* have fed the stories of
  giants from Gath (*"Memories, Myths, and Megalithics: Reconsidering the
  Giants of Gath,"* *Journal of Biblical Literature* 139, 2020). A colleague,
  Jeffrey Chadwick, has noticed that the city gate's walls are 2.38 m thick —
  exactly **"four cubits and a span"** on a 54-cm cubit (paper to ASOR, 2020).
  An intriguing suggestion, and no more than that.

## 6. Bones from the ground

Ancient people did not only tell stories about giants. They **found** them.
The historian of science Adrienne Mayor (*The First Fossil Hunters*,
Princeton, 2000) has shown that the Greeks and Romans regularly dug up the
enormous bones of extinct animals — mammoths, mastodons, prehistoric
rhinoceroses — and, having no other category for them, took them for the
bones of giants and heroes.

> I wanted to dig a well in the courtyard here, and in my digging I hit upon a
> coffin twelve feet long. **I could not believe that there had ever been men
> taller than now, so I opened it and saw that the corpse was just as long as
> the coffin.** — a smith at Tegea, in Herodotus 1.68 (trans. A. D. Godley,
> 1920); the Spartans took the bones to be those of the hero Orestes. (The
> Greek says "a coffin of seven cubits.")

> His own villas… he decorated not so much with handsome statues and pictures
> as with terraces, groves, and objects noteworthy for their antiquity and
> rarity; for example, at Capreae **the monstrous bones of huge sea monsters
> and wild beasts, called the "bones of the giants,"** and the weapons of the
> heroes. — Suetonius, *Augustus* 72 (trans. J. C. Rolfe, 1913)

The Jewish and Christian writers saw the same things. Josephus says of the
giants at Hebron that *"the bones of these men are still shewn to this very
day"* (*Antiquities* 5.2.3); the *Clementine Recognitions* speak of giants
*"whose bones, of enormous size, are still shown in some places for
confirmation"* (1.29); and Augustine saw on the shore at Utica *"a man's molar
tooth of such a size, that if it were cut down into teeth such as we have, a
hundred, I fancy, could have been made out of it"* (*City of God* 15.9). A
tooth a hundred times the size of a human molar fits an elephant or a mammoth.
The ancients were honest observers with the wrong category.

### And the photographs online

You may have seen photographs of archaeologists uncovering an enormous human
skeleton. The best known was made in **2002** by an artist using the name
"IronKite" for a Photoshop contest on the website Worth1000, titled
*"Archaeological Anomalies 2,"* which asked entrants to create a fake
archaeological discovery; it won third place. He started from an aerial photo
of a **mastodon** dig in New York. *National Geographic* traced and explained
the hoax in 2007 (J. Owen, "'Giant Skeleton' Photo Hoax," 14 December 2007).
The image and its copies have been circulating ever since.

**So where does that leave us?** The Bible's own claim is sober: very tall
warrior peoples in Canaan, remembered as the heirs of an older legend, whom
Israel feared and whom the LORD brought down. The ancient world had real
things in front of it that seemed to confirm giants — megalithic tombs,
colossal walls, huge bones. We know more about those things now. That does
not make the Bible's story smaller. Its point was never the height of the
giants; it was **who brought them down**.

# The church on Genesis 6:1–4

A source sheet for **Session 8**. The Jewish readings are on **[the earliest
readings](second-temple-sources.html)** and **[the rabbis](rabbinic-sources.html)**;
this sheet follows the **Christian** ones, from the second century to the
present.

**Why this sheet matters here.** Many of us were taught that the "sons of
God" were the godly descendants of Seth. That reading has a long and honourable
history — Augustine, Luther and Calvin all held it — but it is **not the
oldest Christian reading**. For roughly the first 150 years after the New
Testament, Christian writers who mention the passage read it as angels, and
several of them say plainly that they are following the Book of Enoch. The
Seth reading appears around 221, spreads through the Syriac church in the
fourth century, and becomes the standard reading of the West with Augustine
around 420. In the last century, many evangelical scholars have gone back to
the older view. Here is the evidence, in the writers' own words.

| When | Who | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| c. 150–160 | Justin Martyr | angels; their children are the demons |
| c. 177 | Athenagoras | angels; giants |
| c. 180–190 | Irenaeus | angels (citing Enoch) |
| c. 200–213 | Tertullian | angels; defends Enoch as Scripture |
| **c. 221** | **Julius Africanus** | **first Christian statement of the Seth reading** — with angels allowed as an alternative |
| c. 303–311 | Lactantius | angels, sent as guardians, who fell |
| 4th century | the Syriac church (Ephrem, d. 373); Chrysostom (c. 386) | Seth |
| c. 403 | Sulpicius Severus | angels — the West still divided |
| **c. 420** | **Augustine** | **Seth**; Enoch not Scripture — the West's standard view from here on |
| 1536; 1554 | Luther; Calvin | Seth; the Nephilim were tyrants |
| 1860s; 1888 | Keil; Delitzsch | Keil: Seth · Delitzsch: angels |
| 1962; 2001 | Meredith Kline; John Walton | kings claiming divine sonship |
| 1983 – today | Bauckham, Wenham, Schreiner, Heiser, the NET notes | angels / divine beings |
{: .ff-wrap}

Each entry is marked **→ toward the session's reading** (the sons of God as
heavenly beings, read with Jude and 2 Peter) or **⇢ a different direction**.

## 1. The first Christian readers: angels

### Justin Martyr, *Second Apology* 5 — *c. 150–160* →

Justin, a philosopher who became a Christian and was martyred in Rome,
explains to the emperor where demons come from:

> God, when He had made the whole world, and subjected things earthly to man…
> committed the care of men and of all things under heaven to angels whom He
> appointed over them. **But the angels transgressed this appointment, and were
> captivated by love of women, and begat children who are those that are
> called demons**; and besides, they afterwards subdued the human race to
> themselves, partly by magical writings, and partly by fears and the
> punishments they occasioned, and partly by teaching them to offer sacrifices,
> and incense, and libations… and among men they sowed murders, wars,
> adulteries, intemperate deeds, and all wickedness.

This is *1 Enoch*'s account (the giants' spirits become demons), told to a
Roman emperor as plain Christian teaching.

### Athenagoras, *A Plea for the Christians* 24 — *c. 177* →

> …these fell into impure love of virgins, and were subjugated by the flesh…
> **Of these lovers of virgins, therefore, were begotten those who are called
> giants.**

### Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 4.36.4 and *Demonstration* 18 — *c. 180–190* →

Irenaeus, bishop of Lyons and a student of a student of the apostle John:

> …and in the days of Noah He justly brought on the deluge for the purpose of
> extinguishing that most infamous race of men then existent, who could not
> bring forth fruit to God, **since the angels that sinned had commingled with
> them**… — *Against Heresies* 4.36.4

> …and illicit unions took place upon the earth, **since angels were united
> with the daughters of the race of mankind; and they bore to them sons who for
> their exceeding greatness were called giants.** And the angels brought as
> presents to their wives teachings of wickedness, in that they brought them
> the virtues of roots and herbs, dyeing in colours and cosmetics, the
> discovery of rare substances, love-potions… and all sorcery and idolatry
> hateful to God… — *Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching* 18

His translator's note (1920) says simply: *"This is from the Book of Enoch."*
Compare the list with *1 Enoch* 8:1 on [the earliest readings](second-temple-sources.html).

### Tertullian — *c. 200–213* →

Tertullian of Carthage, the first great Latin theologian, is the frankest of
all. On what the fallen angels taught:

> …those angels, to wit, **who rushed from heaven on the daughters of men**…
> if it is true that they had laid bare the operations of metallurgy, and had
> divulged the natural properties of herbs, and had promulgated the powers of
> enchantments, and had traced out every curious art, even to the
> interpretation of the stars… — *On the Apparel of Women* 1.2

On whether the Book of Enoch is Scripture — he thinks it is, and says why:

> I am aware that the Scripture of Enoch, which has assigned this order (of
> action) to angels, is not received by some, because it is not admitted into
> the Jewish canon either… **To these considerations is added the fact that
> Enoch possesses a testimony in the Apostle Jude.** — *On the Apparel of
> Women* 1.3

And on Paul's hard verse, *"a woman should have a symbol of authority on her
head, because of the angels"* (1 Corinthians 11:10):

> For if (it is) on account of the angels—**those, to wit, whom we read of as
> having fallen from God and heaven on account of concupiscence after
> females**—who can presume that it was bodies already defiled… which such
> angels yearned after… — *On the Veiling of Virgins* 7

(The church did not follow Tertullian on Enoch. Augustine, below, argues from
the same verse in Jude to the opposite conclusion.)

### Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* 2.15 — *c. 303–311* →

Lactantius, tutor to the son of Constantine, has the angels sent **as
guardians**, like *Jubilees*' Watchers:

> …God in His forethought, lest the devil… should by his subtilty either
> corrupt or destroy men, as he had done at first, **sent angels for the
> protection and improvement of the human race**… Therefore, while they abode
> among men, that most deceitful ruler of the earth, by his very association,
> gradually enticed them to vices, and polluted them by intercourse with
> women… But they who were born from these, because they were neither angels
> nor men, but bearing a kind of mixed nature, were not admitted into hell, as
> their fathers were not into heaven. **Thus there came to be two kinds of
> demons; one of heaven, the other of the earth.**

### The *Clementine Homilies* 8.15 — *c. 300* → *(a sectarian novel, not a church father)*

One line is worth having because it sets the Christian giants against the
Greek ones:

> But from their unhallowed intercourse spurious men sprang, much greater in
> stature than ordinary men, whom they afterwards called giants; **not those
> dragon-footed giants who waged war against God, as those blasphemous myths
> of the Greeks do sing**, but wild in manners, and greater than men in size,
> inasmuch as they were sprung of angels; yet less than angels, as they were
> born of women.

## 2. The turn to Seth

### Julius Africanus, *Chronography* — *c. 221* ⇢ *the first Christian statement of the Seth reading*

Africanus, a Christian scholar who wrote the first Christian world history,
is the earliest writer we have who proposes the Seth reading. Here is the
whole fragment. Notice that **his own Greek Bible said "angels"** — "sons of
God" was the variant he found "in some copies":

> When men multiplied on the earth, **the angels of heaven came together with
> the daughters of men. In some copies I found "the sons of God."** What is
> meant by the Spirit, in my opinion, is that **the descendants of Seth are
> called the sons of God on account of the righteous men and patriarchs who
> have sprung from him**, even down to the Saviour Himself; but that the
> descendants of Cain are named the seed of men, as having nothing divine in
> them, on account of the wickedness of their race… **But if it is thought
> that these refer to angels, we must take them to be those who deal with magic
> and jugglery, who taught the women the motions of the stars and the knowledge
> of things celestial, by whose power they conceived the giants as their
> children**, by whom wickedness came to its height on the earth, until God
> decreed that the whole race of the living should perish in their impiety by
> the deluge.

"In my opinion" — and then, in fairness, the angel reading as a live
alternative. The Seth reading entered the church as one scholar's suggestion.

### The *Clementine Recognitions* 1.29 — *c. 400, Latin* ⇢

A sister text to the *Homilies*, surviving in Latin, turns the angels into
good men — and adds a detail about bones:

> …in the eighth generation, **righteous men, who had lived the life of
> angels**, being allured by the beauty of women, fell into promiscuous and
> illicit connections with these… In the ninth generation are born the giants,
> so called from of old, not dragon-footed, as the fables of the Greeks relate,
> but men of immense bodies, **whose bones, of enormous size, are still shown in
> some places for confirmation.**

### The Syriac church and Chrysostom — *4th century* ⇢ *(described, not quoted)*

The Seth reading took hold first in the Syriac-speaking church. **Ephrem the
Syrian** (d. 373) calls Seth's descendants "sons of God" and makes the
"daughters of men" the daughters of Cain. The later Syriac *Cave of
Treasures* tradition adds the vivid picture that many of us were taught: the
sons of Seth lived on a holy mountain near Paradise, and **came down** to the
daughters of Cain in the plain. **John Chrysostom**, preaching in Antioch
around 386 (*Homilies on Genesis* 22), reads the sons of God as Seth's line
and rejects the angel reading; **Cyril of Alexandria** and **Theodoret** in the
fifth century follow, and Theodoret calls the angel reading's defenders
foolish. (No public-domain English translations of these exist; see the
citations below.)

### Augustine, *City of God* 15.22–23 — *c. 420* ⇢ *the reading that won*

Augustine knew the angel reading well, and his Bible, too, sometimes said
"angels of God":

> For in the same Scripture in which the sons of God are said to have loved
> the daughters of men, **they are also called angels of God; whence many
> suppose that they were not men but angels.** — 15.22

He is careful not to deny too much. He admits that stories of spirits
molesting women are widely told — but not of God's holy angels, and not, he
thinks, in 2 Peter:

> …I dare not determine whether there be some spirits embodied in an aerial
> substance… and who are capable of lust and of mingling sensibly with women;
> **but certainly I could by no means believe that God's holy angels could at
> that time have so fallen, nor can I think that it is of them the Apostle
> Peter said, "For if God spared not the angels that sinned…"** I think he
> rather speaks of these who first apostatized from God, along with their
> chief the devil… — 15.23

His positive argument is from verse 3 — God calls them *flesh*:

> …it was immediately added, "And the Lord God said, My Spirit shall not
> always strive with these men, for that they also are flesh." **For by the
> Spirit of God they had been made angels of God, and sons of God; but
> declining towards lower things, they are called men, a name of nature, not of
> grace**… — 15.23

And on the Book of Enoch:

> We cannot deny that Enoch, the seventh from Adam, left some divine writings,
> for this is asserted by the Apostle Jude in his canonical epistle. But it is
> not without reason that these writings have no place in that canon of
> Scripture… So that the writings which are produced under his name, and which
> contain these fables about the giants, saying that their fathers were not
> men, are properly judged by prudent men to be not genuine… **the sons of God,
> who were according to the flesh the sons of Seth, sunk into this community
> when they forsook righteousness.** — 15.23

Augustine's reading became the standard in the West for more than a thousand
years.

### Augustine on the giants themselves, *City of God* 15.9 — *toward the handout's Part 3*

Augustine did believe in giants — and he had seen the evidence:

> **I myself, along with some others, saw on the shore at Utica a man's molar
> tooth of such a size, that if it were cut down into teeth such as we have, a
> hundred, I fancy, could have been made out of it. But that, I believe,
> belonged to some giant.** For though the bodies of ordinary men were then
> larger than ours, the giants surpassed all in stature.

A tooth a hundred times the size of a human molar is not a human tooth. It
fits an elephant's or a mammoth's — the [neighbours
sheet](the-neighbors.html) has more of these.

### John Cassian, *Conferences* 8.21 — *c. 426–429* ⇢

A monk in southern Gaul gives the argument that has persuaded most Sethites
since:

> **We cannot possibly believe that spiritual existences can have carnal
> intercourse with women.**… As long then as there continued that separation
> of the lines between them, the seed of Seth, as it sprang from an excellent
> root, was by reason of its sanctity termed "angels of God," or as some copies
> have it "sons of God;" and on the contrary the others… were termed "children
> of men."

## 3. The Reformers

### Martin Luther, *Lectures on Genesis* — *c. 1536* ⇢

> He tersely calls the sons of the patriarchs the "sons of God," since to them
> was given the promise of the blessed seed and they constituted the true
> Church.

> In reference to this point the Jews fancy foolish things. They interpret the
> sons of God to signify demon-lechers by whom that impious generation was
> begotten… The more moderate ones, however, refute such folly and represent
> the sons of the mighty. This has been aptly disproved by Lyra; for the
> punishment of the deluge befell, not alone the mighty, but all flesh…

> **The flood came not because the generation of Cain was corrupt, but because
> the generation of the righteous who had believed God, had obeyed his Word,
> and had possessed the true worship, now had lapsed** into idolatry,
> disobedience to parents, sensuality, oppression.

That last sentence is Luther at his best, and it is the heart of the Seth
reading: **the tragedy of Genesis 6 is the fall of the church.** On the
Nephilim, he agrees with this session's Part 3:

> I have no other answer than that **the text says nothing of stature in this
> place.**… this passage and other, similar ones prompt me to understand
> "nephilim" to designate not bulk of body, but **tyranny and oppression**,
> inasmuch as they domineered by force…

### John Calvin, *Commentary on Genesis* 6:1–4 — *1554* ⇢

> It was, therefore, base ingratitude in the posterity of Seth, to mingle
> themselves with the children of Cain, and with other profane races; because
> they voluntarily deprived themselves of the inestimable grace of God.

> **That ancient figment, concerning the intercourse of angels with women, is
> abundantly refuted by its own absurdity; and it is surprising that learned
> men should formerly have been fascinated by ravings so gross and
> prodigious.** The opinion also of the Chaldean paraphrase is frigid; namely,
> that promiscuous marriages between the sons of nobles, and the daughters of
> plebeians, is condemned.

Calvin dismisses both of the other readings in two sentences — the angels as
a "figment," the rabbis' nobles as "frigid." On the giants he is close to
Luther:

> **Moses does not indeed say, that they were of extraordinary stature, but
> only that they were robust.** Elsewhere, I acknowledge, the same word denotes
> vastness of stature, which was formidable to those who explored the land of
> Canaan, (Numbers 13:33.) But Moses does not distinguish those of whom he
> speaks in this place, from other men, so much by the size of their bodies,
> as by their robberies and their lust of dominion… **And this was the first
> nobility of the world.**

*"The first nobility of the world"* — Calvin, too, heard 6:4 as an honour roll,
and knew what it was worth.

## 4. The nineteenth century: one commentary, two views

The famous nineteenth-century commentary "Keil and Delitzsch" hides a
disagreement. **C. F. Keil**, who wrote the Genesis volume, held the Seth
reading — and conceded, first, what the words most naturally mean:

> Three different views have been entertained from the very earliest times:
> the "sons of God" being regarded as (a) the sons of princes, (b) angels, (c)
> the Sethites or godly men… Of these three views, the first, although it has
> become the traditional one in orthodox rabbinical Judaism, may be dismissed
> at once as not warranted by the usages of the language, and as altogether
> unscriptural.

> **Apart from the context and tenor of the passage, these two points would
> lead us most naturally to regard the "sons of God" as angels**, in
> distinction from men and the daughters of men.

His main argument against it:

> Now אשּׁה לקח (to take a wife) is a standing expression throughout the whole
> of the Old Testament for the marriage relation established by God at the
> creation, and is never applied to πορνεία, or the simple act of physical
> connection. This is quite sufficient of itself to exclude any reference to
> angels. **For Christ Himself distinctly states that the angels cannot marry
> (Matt. xxii. 30; Mark xii. 25; cf. Luke xx. 34 sqq.).**

And on the New Testament: *"Nor do 2 Pet. ii. 4 and Jude 6 furnish any
evidence of angel marriages. Peter is merely speaking of sinning angels in
general…"*

His colleague **Franz Delitzsch**, in his own later commentary (1888),
disagreed:

> …it is most obvious to think here of angels… Hence it seems that we must
> really assume… that a sexual intercourse of angels with women is here
> related. **It was thus that Jude in his Epistle, ver. 6 sq., in agreement with
> the book of Henoch, understands the matter**…

The whole modern debate is in those two men: Keil arguing from Jesus's words
about angels and from the ordinary Hebrew for marriage; Delitzsch from the
meaning of the phrase and from Jude.

## 5. Today

Evangelical scholarship is divided, and the division runs roughly where it ran
between Keil and Delitzsch. *(Positions checked against the works or reliable
reviews of them; cited, not quoted.)*

- **Heavenly beings.** Gordon Wenham, *Genesis 1–15* (Word Biblical
  Commentary, 1987), who notes that it is both the oldest view and the view of
  most modern commentators; Tremper Longman III, *Genesis* (2016); Michael S.
  Heiser, *The Unseen Realm* (2015), who reads them as rebel members of the
  divine council; and the **NET Bible's own study note** on 6:2, which weighs
  all three views and favours this one. On the New Testament side, Richard
  Bauckham, *Jude, 2 Peter* (Word, 1983), and Thomas R. Schreiner, *1, 2 Peter,
  Jude* (New American Commentary, 2003), both read Jude 6 and 2 Peter 2:4 as
  referring to the angels of Genesis 6 as *1 Enoch* tells it.
- **The line of Seth.** H. C. Leupold, *Exposition of Genesis* (1942); John
  Murray, *Principles of Conduct* (1957); Kenneth A. Mathews, *Genesis 1–11:26*
  (New American Commentary, 1996).
- **Kings claiming divine sonship.** Meredith G. Kline, "Divine Kingship and
  Genesis 6:1–4," *Westminster Theological Journal* 24 (1962); John H. Walton,
  *Genesis* (NIV Application Commentary, 2001); Walter C. Kaiser Jr., *Hard
  Sayings of the Bible* (1996). Kline's key sentence: the phrase was taken from
  *"the titulary of this pagan ideology of divine kingship"* — the way ancient
  kings styled themselves *sons of the gods*.
- **On Jude and 2 Peter** the current state of the question is summed up by
  Nicholas J. Moore, "Jude and the Watchers in the Early Church," *Novum
  Testamentum* 68 (2026): since the mid-twentieth century, scholarship has
  identified the referent of Jude 6 and 2 Peter 2:4 as the Watchers story. A
  recent dissent is Kim Papaioannou, "The Sin of the Angels in 2 Peter 2:4 and
  Jude 6," *Journal of Biblical Literature* 140 (2021). And on 1 Peter 3:19,
  the main evangelical alternative to "imprisoned fallen angels" is
  Augustine's reading, revived by Wayne Grudem: Christ preached *through Noah*
  to the people of Noah's day, who are now "spirits in prison" (*Trinity
  Journal*, 1986).

# The rabbis on Genesis 6:1–8

A source sheet for **Session 8**. Everything here comes from the Jewish
interpretive tradition, from the synagogue translations of the second century
to Nachmanides in the thirteenth. For the readings that are older than this
literature — *1 Enoch*, *Jubilees*, the Dead Sea Scrolls, Philo, Josephus —
see the companion sheet, **[the earliest readings](second-temple-sources.html)**.

**Why this sheet matters here.** The oldest Jewish readers of Genesis 6 were
nearly unanimous: the sons of God were angels. Then, in the second century
CE, the rabbis turned — and one of the most revered of them **cursed** anyone
who called the sons of God "sons of God." This sheet shows the turn, and
shows that the older reading never quite went away: it survives in a Targum,
in the Talmud, in a medieval midrash, as Rashi's second explanation, and in
Nachmanides' admission that it *"fits the language of the verse more than all
other interpretations."*

**On dates.** These documents were compiled centuries after the sages they
quote, and they quote them by name. Where a passage is attributed to a named
rabbi we give that rabbi's own century as well as the document's — so you can
see how far the tradition reaches back, and hold the attribution for what it
is: **tradition, not transcript.**

| Document | Compiled | Attributed voices reach back to |
|---|---|---|
| **Targum Onkelos** | c. 2nd–3rd century CE, later fixed in Babylonia | — |
| **Genesis Rabbah** | c. 400–450 CE, Roman Palestine | 2nd–4th century sages |
| **Talmud Bavli** | c. 500–600 CE, Babylonia | 1st–5th century sages |
| **Targum Pseudo-Jonathan** | final form c. 7th–8th century or later | earlier Palestinian traditions |
| **Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer** | 8th–9th century | attributed to R. Eliezer (c. 100 CE); draws on Enoch traditions |
| **Rashi** | 1040–1105, northern France | quotes Genesis Rabbah throughout |
| **Ibn Ezra** | 1089–1167, Spain and abroad | — |
| **Nachmanides** (Ramban) | c. 1260, Spain | — |

Each entry is marked **→ toward the session's reading** or **⇢ a different
direction**. Both are useful. Where the tradition agrees with us it is an old
witness that we did not invent this; where it diverges it is a reminder that a
good text carries more than one true reading.

## 1. The turn: "sons of the judges"

### *Genesis Rabbah* 26:5 ⇢ *a different direction*

Rabbi Shimon bar Yoḥai taught in the middle of the second century, a
generation after the failed revolt against Rome. The English edition prints
the verse the way he read it — "the children of the great men":

> "The children of the great men [benei haelohim] saw," **Rabbi Shimon ben
> Yoḥai called them [when translating this verse into Aramaic] "the children
> of judges." Rabbi Shimon ben Yoḥai used to curse anyone who would call them
> [translating the words literally, as] "children of god."** Rabbi Shimon ben
> Yoḥai taught: Any breach [in morals] that is not [begun] by prominent men is
> not a breach. [The saying goes:] If the priests steal the idols, who will
> ever take an oath in their names, or who will ever sacrifice to them?

His Aramaic is *bənê dayyānayyā*, "sons of the judges," and the curse falls
on anyone who says *bənê ʾĕlāhayyā*, "sons of God." The text never mentions
angels; but scholars have long read the curse as aimed at the angel reading,
which by then was everywhere — in *1 Enoch*, in *Jubilees*, and among the
Christians. His reason is worth hearing on its own terms: **corruption that
starts at the top is the kind that spreads.**

The same section, two centuries later, has a detail that belongs beside the
session's point about rulers who *see* and *take* (Part 1):

> "That they were fair [tovot]" – **Rabbi Yudan** said: The word is written
> tovot [without vavs]. **When they would adorn a woman for her [marriage to
> her] husband, the prominent man would enter and consort with her first,
> [before her husband].** That is what is written: "That they were fair
> [tovot]" – these are the unmarried girls. "They took for themselves wives
> from whomever they chose" – these are married women.

Rabbi Yudan (4th century) reads 6:2 as a lord's claim on every bride — the
very abuse the epic of Gilgamesh charges against its hero-king (see the
[neighbours sheet](the-neighbors.html)). → *toward the session's reading of
the grammar*, even though he reads the sons of God as men.

### Targum Onkelos 6:2, 6:4 ⇢ *a different direction*

The synagogue's official Aramaic translation says the same thing, quietly, in
the text itself:

> **The sons of the rulers** saw that the daughters of man were fair and they
> took for themselves wives from whomever they chose [wanted]. — 6:2
>
> There were **giants** on the earth in those days, and also later, when **the
> sons of the rulers** came to the daughters of man and they bore children to
> them. These were the mightiest ones who ever existed. [They were] men of
> renown. — 6:4

*Bənê rabrəbayyā*, "the sons of the great ones." And the Nephilim become
*gibbārayyā*, "mighty men, giants."

### *Talmud Bavli, Sanhedrin* 108a ⇢ *a different direction*

A second second-century voice, Rabbi Yosei, reads 6:2 as the sin of the
**eye**:

> Rabbi Yosei says: The generation of the flood became haughty due only to
> **the covetousness of the eyeball, which is similar to water**, as it is
> stated: "And the sons of the prominent men saw that the daughters of men
> were fair; and they took for themselves wives from anyone they chose"
> (Genesis 6:2). Consequently, God punished them with water, which is similar
> to an eyeball, as it is stated: "All the fountains [mayenot] of the great
> deep were breached…" (Genesis 7:11).

The pun is on *ʿayin*, which means both **eye** and **spring**. They sinned by
the eye; they were judged by the springs. It is the *saw… took* of Part 1,
read as a moral.

### Rashi on 6:2 → *and* ⇢ — *both readings, side by side*

Rashi, the great eleventh-century commentator, gives the rabbis' reading
first — and then, without apology, the older one:

> THE SONS OF ELOHIM — The sons of princes and rulers (Genesis Rabbah 26:15).
> **Another explanation of בני האלהים is that these were princely angels who
> came as messengers from God: they, too, intermingled with them** (the
> daughters of men). Wherever the word אלהים occurs in the Scriptures it
> signifies authority…

*(The Hebrew says "princes **and judges**"; "Genesis Rabbah 26:15" is the
translator's reference to 26:5.)* The rabbinic curse did not erase the
angels. Rashi kept them as his second word.

### Ibn Ezra on 6:2 ⇢ *a different direction* — *and the Seth reading, as hearsay*

> THE SONS OF GOD. The term bene Elohim (the sons of god) refers to the sons
> of the judges who dispensed God's (Elohim) justice. Others say that Elohim
> refers to God Himself and the sons of God are holy ones (saints) living on
> earth. They are called sons of God in the sense of Ye are the children of
> the Lord your God (Deut. 14:1). **Others say that the sons of God refers to
> the children of Seth and that the daughters of man refers to women of
> Cain's family.** However, it appears to me that the sons of God refers to
> those who know the will of the most High. These men chose women who matched
> them astrologically and physically. Hence they produced mighty men. **It is
> possible that they took women even against their will.**

Four readings, and not one of them angels. Notice the third: the **Seth and
Cain** reading that most Christians know shows up here only as *"others
say."* It was the church's standard reading by Ibn Ezra's day, not the
synagogue's. (See [the
church on Genesis 6](church-fathers.html).)

### Nachmanides (Ramban) on 6:4 → *toward the session's reading*

Nachmanides, in thirteenth-century Spain, first reports Rashi, then offers his
own theory — Adam, Seth and Enosh, fathered directly in God's likeness, were
the "sons of God," and their children were *nəpilîm*, "inferior ones" — and
then says this:

> This is a fitting explanation of this chapter. **But the Midrash of Rabbi
> Eliezer the Great, in the chapters concerning the angels that fell from
> their place of holiness in heaven — as is mentioned in the Gemara of Tractate
> Yoma — fits into the language of the verse more than all other
> interpretations.** But it would necessitate delving at length into the
> secret of this subject.

One of the greatest rabbinic interpreters, a thousand years after Rabbi
Shimon's curse, concedes that **the fallen-angel reading fits the words
best** — and declines to discuss it further because it belongs to the
mystical tradition. That is the midrash in section 2.

## 2. The angels who would not go away

### Targum Pseudo-Jonathan 6:4 → *toward the session's reading*

This Aramaic translation reads 6:2 as the rabbis did — "the sons of the
great" — and then, two verses later, lets the older story in by name:

> **Schamchazai and Uzziel, who fell from heaven, were on the earth in those
> days;** and also, after the sons of the Great had gone in with the daughters
> of men, they bare to them: and these are they who are called men who are of
> the world, men of names.

The Aramaic is a pun: the *nəpilîm* are *nəpîlîn min šəmayyā*, "**the ones
who fell from heaven.**" *Shamḥazai* is *1 Enoch*'s Shemiḥazah, leader of the
Watchers. Pseudo-Jonathan is the only Targum where the angels survive in the
text itself.

### *Talmud Bavli, Yoma* 67b → *toward the session's reading*

On the Day of Atonement one goat was sent into the wilderness *"for Azazel"*
(Leviticus 16:8–10). Who is Azazel? A second-century school gives an answer
that only makes sense if you know *1 Enoch*:

> The school of Rabbi Yishmael taught: **Azazel is so called because it atones
> for the actions of Uzza and Azael.**

That is the whole of the Talmud's line. (The English edition adds that these
were "sons of God" who sinned with "daughters of men" — true to the tradition,
but the commentator's explanation, not the Talmud's words.) In *1 Enoch*,
**Asael** is the Watcher who taught weapons and cosmetics, and he is bound in
the wilderness under jagged rocks — see [the earliest
readings](second-temple-sources.html).

### *Talmud Bavli, Niddah* 61a → *toward the session's reading*

> Now, Sihon and Og were brothers, as the Master said: **Sihon and Og were
> sons of Ahijah, son of Shamhazai.**

An anonymous tradition, stated in passing as if everyone knew it: the two
Amorite kings Israel defeated east of the Jordan were **grandsons of the
fallen Watcher**.

### *Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer* 22 → *toward the session's reading* — *with a twist*

This eighth-century retelling of Genesis is "the Midrash of Rabbi Eliezer"
Nachmanides meant. Its chapter 22 is titled *The Fall of the Angels*:

> Rabbi said: **The angels who fell from their holy place in heaven saw the
> daughters of the generations of Cain walking about naked, with their eyes
> painted like harlots, and they went astray after them**, and took wives from
> amongst them, as it is said, "And the sons of Elohim saw the daughters of men
> that they were fair; and they took them wives of all that they chose" (Gen.
> 6:2).
>
> Rabbi Joshua said: The angels are flaming fire, as it is said, "His servants
> are a flaming fire" (Ps. 104:4)… but **when they fell from heaven, from their
> holy place, their strength and stature (became) like that of the sons of
> men, and their frame was (made of) clods of dust**…
>
> Rabbi Zadok said: **From them were born the giants (Anakim)**, who walked with
> pride in their heart, and who stretched forth their hand to all (kinds of)
> robbery and violence, and shedding of blood, as it is said, "And there we saw
> the Nephilim, the sons of Anak" (Num. 13:33)…
>
> Rabbi Joshua said: The Israelites are called "Sons of God," as it is said,
> "Ye are the sons of the Lord your God" (Deut. 14:1). **The angels are called
> "Sons of God," as it is said, "When the morning stars sang together, and all
> the sons of God shouted for joy"** (Job 38:7)…

Two things. First, "Rabbi Joshua" answers the hardest question — *how could
spirits father children?* — with an answer: in falling, **they became flesh**.
Second, the women here are **Cain's** daughters. The midrash keeps the angels
*and* the Cainite women; the Christian Seth reading (see [the church
sheet](church-fathers.html)) keeps the Cainite women and drops the angels.

### The Midrash of Shemḥazai and Azael (medieval) → *toward the session's reading*

A medieval Hebrew story, preserved in *Yalkut Shimoni* (Bereshit §44) and in
*Bereshit Rabbati*, tells the tale at full length. It has no licensed English
translation, so here is its outline (our summary of the Hebrew). When the
generation of the flood turned to idols, two angels, **Shemḥazai and Azael**,
reminded God that they had opposed making man — *"What is man, that You are
mindful of him?"* (Psalm 8:4) — and asked to go down and show how to live.
God warned them that on earth **the evil inclination would rule them too**.
They went, and fell. Shemḥazai's sons ate a thousand camels, horses and oxen a
day, and dreamed of the coming flood. Shemḥazai **repented**, and hangs
between heaven and earth; Azael did not, and still deals in *"dyes and women's
ornaments"* — which is why the Yom Kippur goat goes to Azazel. *Bereshit
Rabbati* adds that Shemḥazai's sons **fathered Sihon and Og** — the same
family tree as *Niddah* 61a.

The point of the story is sharp: **put an angel in human flesh and it does no
better than we do.** The problem is not the *nature* of the creature. It is
the *yēṣer*.

## 3. The seven names of the Nephilim

### *Genesis Rabbah* 26:7 → *toward the session's reading*

> "The giants were on the earth in those days." **They were called by seven
> names: Eimim, Refa'im, Giborim, Zamzumim, Anakim, Avim, Nefilim.** Eimim –
> as anyone who saw them, fear of them [eimatan] would descend upon them.
> Refa'im – as anyone who saw them, his heart would become soft [rafeh] like
> wax. Giborim – Rabbi Abba bar Kahana said, in the name of Rabbi Yoḥanan: The
> marrow of the femur of one of them measured eighteen cubits. Zamzumim –
> Rabbi Yosei bar Ḥanina said: Intimidating military commanders. Anakim – …
> They would wear numerous necklaces upon necklaces [anakim]… Avim – as they
> brought annihilation upon the world and were themselves annihilated from the
> world… **Nefilim – as they toppled [hipilu] the world, fell from the world,
> and filled the world with stillborns [nefalim] with their immorality.**

The rabbis did exactly what the [Scripture sheet](elsewhere-in-scripture.html)
does: they joined the **Nephilim** of Genesis 6 to the **Emim, Rephaim,
Zamzummim and Anakim** of Numbers and Deuteronomy, as one people under seven
names. Every name is read as a pun on what they did.

And on *"men of the name"*:

> **Rabbi Aḥa said in the name of Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi: Contention is as
> troublesome as the generation of the Flood.** Here "men of renown" is
> stated, and elsewhere [concerning Koraḥ's rebellion]: "Distinguished of the
> convocation, men of renown" (Numbers 16:2) is stated. Just as "men of
> renown" stated there refers to contention, so, too, "men of renown" stated
> here refers to contention.

The rabbis caught the same link the passage sheet points out: the only other
*ʾanšê šēm* in the Bible are **Korah's rebels**.

## 4. Og, who rode out the flood

If the Nephilim died in the flood, how could the spies meet them afterward,
and Og be *"left of the remaining Rephaim"*? The rabbis' answer was Og
himself.

### Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Genesis 14:13 → *a story, and a warning*

Genesis 14:13 says *"one who had escaped"* came to tell Abram that Lot was
captured. Who was he?

> **And Og came, who had been spared from the giants that died in the deluge,
> and had ridden protected upon the top of the ark, and sustained with food
> by Noah; not being spared through high righteousness, but that the
> inhabitants of the world might see the power of the Lord, and say, Were there
> not giants who in the first times rebelled against the Lord of the world, and
> perished from the earth?**

The same Targum at Deuteronomy 3:11: *"only Og king of Mathnan remained of the
remnant of the giants who perished in the deluge."* The *Talmud* (*Niddah*
61a, Rabbi Yoḥanan, 3rd century) makes the same identification: the one who
escaped *"is referring to Og, who was saved from… the generation of the
flood."*

### *Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer* 23 → *the story told for children*

> …except Noah and those who were with him in the ark… **except Og, king of
> Bashan, who sat down on a piece of wood under the gutter of the ark. He
> swore to Noah and to his sons that he would be their servant for ever. What
> did Noah do? He bored an aperture in the ark, and he put (through it) his
> food daily for him**, and he also was left, as it is said, "For only Og,
> king of Bashan, remained of the remnant of the giants" (Deut. 3:11).

It is a folk tale, and the rabbis knew it; the Talmud (*Zevachim* 113b) needs
a miracle to keep Og from being boiled. But notice what it is for. It is an
answer to a real question in the text — how the Nephilim are *"also after
this"* — and it makes Og, the terror of Deuteronomy, a giant who survived by
**clinging to the ark and being fed by Noah**.

## 5. The 120 years (6:3)

Session 7 met the two readings: a limit on a lifetime, or time left before
the flood. The Targums take the second, and make it **a respite for
repentance**:

> Adonoy said, My spirit will not continue to judge man forever [this evil
> generation shall not exist before me forever], since he is nothing but
> flesh [since they are flesh and their deeds are evil]. His days shall be a
> hundred and twenty years **[I will give them a period of a hundred and
> twenty years for them to repent]**. — *Targum Onkelos* 6:3

> …Have I not imparted My Holy Spirit to them… that they may work good works?
> And, behold, their works are wicked. **Behold, I will give them a
> prolongment of a hundred and twenty years, that they may work repentance,
> and not perish.** — *Targum Pseudo-Jonathan* 6:3

Rashi and Ibn Ezra follow them. (*Genesis Rabbah* 26:6 goes its own way and
ties the 120 years to the lifespan of **Moses**, Deuteronomy 34:7.) First
Peter says something very like the Targums: God *"patiently waited in the
days of Noah as an ark was being constructed"* (3:20).

## 6. The evil inclination (6:5)

The rabbis' teaching on the *yēṣer hāraʿ*, the evil inclination, is built on
6:5 and 8:21. Two short texts:

> **The Sages taught: So difficult is the evil inclination that even its
> Creator calls it evil**, as it is stated: "For the inclination of a man's
> heart is evil from his youth" (Genesis 8:21). **Rav Yitzḥak says: A person's
> evil inclination renews itself to him every day**, as it is stated: "And that
> every inclination of the thoughts in his heart was only evil all day [kol
> hayyom]" (Genesis 6:5). "Kol hayyom" can also be understood as: Every day.
> — *Talmud Bavli, Kiddushin* 30b

The Hebrew of the first line has a pun the English cannot keep: *even its
**Maker*** is *yôṣərô* — the one who ***formed*** it, from the same root as
*yēṣer*. And the same page gives the remedy: *"My children, I created an evil
inclination, which is the wound, and I created Torah as its antidote."*

> Rabbi Ḥiyya the Great said: **Wretched is the dough whose baker attests in
> its regard that it is inferior** – "[and the Lord said to His heart…] as the
> inclination of man's heart is evil from his youth." — *Genesis Rabbah* 34:10
> (c. 200 CE)

## 7. "And it grieved him to his heart" (6:6)

### *Genesis Rabbah* 27:4 → *toward the session's reading*

The fullest early Jewish meditation on God's grief. It is worth reading
whole:

> "The Lord regretted [vayinaḥem] that He had made man on the earth" – Rabbi
> Yehuda and Rabbi Neḥemya, Rabbi Yehuda said: [God said:] 'There was regret
> before Me that I created him below ["on the earth"], for had I created him on
> High, he would never have rebelled against Me.' Rabbi Neḥemya said: [God
> said:] '**I take solace [mitnaḥem]** in the fact that I created him below
> ["on the earth"], for had I created him on High, just as he incited the lower
> beings [on earth] to rebel against Me, so he would have incited the supernal
> beings [the angels] to rebel against Me.'
>
> Rabbi Aivu said: [God said:] '**There was regret before Me that I created an
> evil inclination in him**, as had I not created an evil inclination in him,
> he would not have rebelled against Me.'…
>
> "And He was saddened in His heart" – Rabbi Berekhya said: This is analogous
> to a king who built a palace by means of an architect. He saw it and it was
> not to his liking. At whom should he be angry? Is it not the architect? So,
> "He was saddened in his heart."…
>
> **A certain heretic asked Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korḥa**, saying to him: 'Do you
> not say that the Holy One blessed be He foresees the future?' He said to
> him: 'Yes.' [The heretic continued:] 'But is it not written: "He was saddened
> in his heart"?' He said to him: 'Has a male offspring ever been born to you in
> your lifetime?' He said to him: 'Yes.' He said to him: 'What did you do?' He
> said to him: 'I rejoiced and encouraged everyone else to rejoice.' He said
> to him: '**But did you not know that he would ultimately die?**' He said to
> him: '**At a time of rejoicing there is rejoicing; at a time of mourning
> there is mourning.**' He said to him: 'So was the situation before the Holy
> One blessed be He, as Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi said: **Seven days the Holy
> One blessed be He mourned His world before the Flood came to the world.**
> What is the source? "He was saddened [vayitatzev] in his heart," and atziva
> is nothing other than mourning, just as it says: **"The king is grieving
> [ne'etzav] over his son"'** (II Samuel 19:3).

Four things are happening here.

- Rabbis Yehuda and Neḥemya take the two senses of *niḥam* — **regret** and
  **comfort** — and each builds a reading on one. (That is the same pair the
  passage sheet traces back to Lamech in 5:29.)
- Rabbi Aivu puts the regret on the **evil inclination** — the *yēṣer* of
  verse 5.
- Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korḥah (2nd century) answers a real objection — *how can
  a God who knows the future be grieved by it?* — with a father who rejoiced
  at a son's birth though he knew the son would one day die.
- And Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi (3rd century) says what the grief *was*:
  **mourning**, seven days of it, the length of a Jewish mourning period —
  and he proves it from **David weeping over Absalom** (*"the king is grieved
  over his son,"* 2 Samuel 19:2 in English Bibles). The verb of Genesis 6:6
  is the verb of a father over a lost son.

### Targum Onkelos 6:6 ⇢ *a different direction*

The official synagogue translation could not bring itself to say it:

> Adonoy was comforted [regretted in His Word] that He had made man on the
> earth, and He grieved in His heart **[and He said with His word to break
> their strength in accordance with His Will]**.

The Aramaic drops *"it grieved him to his heart"* altogether and puts a
decision in its place. The translators were protecting God from a feeling.
The Hebrew did not.

### Nachmanides (Ramban) on 6:6 → *toward the session's reading*

> AND THE ETERNAL REPENTED… AND IT GRIEVED HIM AT HIS HEART. The Torah speaks
> in the language of men. **The purport is that they rebelled, and grieved His
> holy spirit with their sins.**

Ramban reaches for Isaiah 63:10 — *"they rebelled and grieved his Holy
Spirit"* — the same verse the passage sheet does. (Ibn Ezra, more cautiously:
*"The Torah spoke in the language of men, for we know that God is not a man
that he should repent."*)

## 8. "But Noah found favor" (6:8)

### *Genesis Rabbah* 28:9 and 29:1 → *toward the session's reading*

> Rabbi Abba bar Kahana said: "As I regret that I made them, but Noah found
> favor [in God's eyes]" (Genesis 6:7–8) – this is bewildering. **However, even
> Noah, who [alone] remained from them, it is not that he was worthy, but
> rather that he found favor [in God's eyes].** — 28:9

> Rabbi Ḥanina… said: **Noah had [only] one ounce [onkia] of virtue to his
> credit.** If so, why was he spared? It was "by the pureness of Your hands."
> — 29:1

The *Talmud* says the same: *"The sentence of Noah was also decided; but he
was spared… due to the fact that he found favor in the eyes of God"*
(*Sanhedrin* 108a, the school of Rabbi Yishmael, 2nd century). And *Genesis
Rabbah* 29:3 draws the line finely: God **found** Abraham, David and Israel —
*"He [Noah] found [favor], the Holy One blessed be He did not find [him]."*

These rabbis read verse 8 the way the session does: **grace first.** Not
every Jewish reader agreed. Pseudo-Jonathan supplies the missing merit —
*"But Noah, **who was righteous**, found favour before the Lord"* — and
Nachmanides says Scripture *"afterwards… tells why he was pleasing before
G-d: because he was a perfectly righteous man."* *Genesis Rabbah* itself
holds three answers side by side (no merit; the merit of Moses who would
descend from him; the merit of his children). But the oldest line is the
starkest: *not that he was worthy.*

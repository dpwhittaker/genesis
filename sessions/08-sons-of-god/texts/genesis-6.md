# Genesis 6:1–8 (NET)

The passage for **Session 8**, printed clean so you can work in it. Eight
verses, in two paragraphs of four. The first is the strangest paragraph in
Genesis. The second is the saddest.

Two suggestions before you start.

**Count who "sees."** God *saw* that what he made was good, seven times over
in Genesis 1. Watch who does the seeing in these eight verses, and what they
see.

**Watch for words you have met before.** *Good*, *took*, *forever*, *name*,
*heart*, *comfort*, *pain*. Almost every important word in this passage has
been used once already in Genesis 1–5, and the notes below trace each one
back.

---

## Genesis 5:32 — where we left off

**32** After Noah was 500 years old, he became the father of Shem, Ham, and
Japheth.

## Genesis 6:1–4 — the sons of God and the daughters of humankind

**1** When humankind began to multiply on the face of the earth, and daughters
were born to them, **2** the sons of God saw that the daughters of humankind
were beautiful. Thus they took wives for themselves from any they chose.
**3** So the LORD said, "My Spirit will not remain in humankind indefinitely,
since they are mortal. They will remain for 120 more years." **4** The
Nephilim were on the earth in those days (and also after this) when the sons
of God would sleep with the daughters of humankind, who gave birth to their
children. They were the mighty heroes of old, the famous men.

## Genesis 6:5–8 — what the LORD saw

**5** But the LORD saw that the wickedness of humankind had become great on
the earth. Every inclination of the thoughts of their minds was only evil all
the time. **6** The LORD regretted that he had made humankind on the earth,
and he was highly offended. **7** So the LORD said, "I will wipe humankind,
whom I have created, from the face of the earth—everything from humankind to
animals, including creatures that move on the ground and birds of the air,
for I regret that I have made them." **8** But Noah found favor in the sight
of the LORD.

*The next verse opens a new heading — "This is the account of Noah" (6:9) —
and tells the reason for the flood a second time, in different words. That
is where Session 9 begins.*

---

## Ten notes on the Hebrew, for when you get there

The NET is a careful translation, and in this passage it smooths over
several of the words the session turns on. Where it does, the note gives the
Hebrew underneath.

### 1. "Began to multiply" (6:1, 6:5)

> *kî-**hēḥēl** hāʾādām **lārōb*** — "when humankind **began** to **grow
> many**"

Two small words set up the whole passage. *Lārōb*, "to become many," is the
blessing of Genesis 1:28 — *be fruitful and multiply* — coming true. Four
verses later the same root comes back: *kî **rabbâ** rāʿat hāʾādām*, "that
the evil of humankind **was many**" (6:5). Humanity multiplied, and so did
what was wrong with it.

*Hēḥēl*, "began," is the word *Genesis Rabbah* 23:7 hears as ominous. It
lists three places where Genesis says something "began" and reads each as the
start of a rebellion: Enosh's day (4:26), this verse, and Nimrod, who
***began* to be a mighty warrior** (*hēḥēl lihyôt **gibbōr***, 10:8). Hold
on to *gibbōr* — it is the word for the "mighty heroes" of 6:4.

### 2. "Saw that they were good… and took" (6:2)

> *wayyirʾû… kî **ṭōbōt** hēnnâ **wayyiqḥû*** — "they **saw**… that they
> were **good**, and they **took**"

The NET says *beautiful*; the Hebrew says ***good*** — *ṭôb*, the word God
used seven times over his own work in Genesis 1. And the three verbs *saw —
good — took* are a pattern Genesis has already used once, and the Bible will
use again every time someone helps himself to what is not his:

| | saw | good | took |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Eve** (3:6) | "the woman **saw**" | "that the tree was **good** for food" | "she **took**" |
| **The sons of God** (6:2) | "they **saw**" | "that they were **good**" | "they **took**" wives |
| **Pharaoh's court** (12:14–15) | "they **saw**" Sarai | "that she was very beautiful" | "she was **taken**" |
| **Shechem**, "prince of the land" (34:2) | "he **saw**" Dinah | — | "he **took** her" |
| **Achan** (Joshua 7:21) | "I **saw**" | "a **good** robe from Babylon" | "I wanted them, so I **took** them" |
| **King David** (2 Samuel 11:2–4) | "he **saw** a woman" | "very **good** in appearance" | "he **took** her" |
{: .ff-wrap}

*(The table gives the Hebrew words literally; the NET varies them — "a nice
robe," "very attractive.")*

Notice who is doing it. After the garden, the people in this table are almost
all **rulers** — a pharaoh's officials, a prince, a king — taking women
because they can. The last phrase of 6:2 says the same: *mikkōl ʾăšer
bāḥārû*, "**from all whom they chose**." Nobody is asked.

### 3. "The sons of God" (6:2, 6:4)

> *bənê **hāʾĕlōhîm*** — "the sons of **God**" (or "of **the gods**")

In Hebrew, "son of ___" usually means **"one who belongs to the class
___."** A "son of a year" is a year-old lamb; "the sons of the prophets" are
prophets (2 Kings 2:3); a "son of death" is a man who deserves to die (1
Samuel 20:31); a "son of man" is a human being. So *bənê hāʾĕlōhîm* most
naturally means **"members of the class *ʾĕlōhîm*"** — divine beings, the
way "sons of man" means human beings. The word *ʾĕlōhîm* can mean God or
"gods, heavenly beings"; the form is the same.

Here is every verse in the Hebrew Bible that uses this phrase or its close
cousins:

| Verse | Hebrew | NET |
|---|---|---|
| Genesis 6:2, 4 | *bənê hāʾĕlōhîm* | the sons of God |
| Job 1:6; 2:1 | *bənê hāʾĕlōhîm* | the sons of God (who come before the LORD, with Satan among them) |
| Job 38:7 | *bənê ʾĕlōhîm* | all the sons of God (in parallel with "the morning stars," at creation) |
| Psalm 29:1 | *bənê ʾēlîm* | you heavenly beings |
| Psalm 89:6 (Heb. 7) | *bənê ʾēlîm* | the heavenly beings |
| Psalm 82:6 | *bənê ʿelyôn* | sons of the Most High (to the "gods" of the divine assembly) |
| Deuteronomy 32:8 | *bənê ʾĕlōhîm* (Dead Sea Scroll 4QDeut<sup>j</sup>) | the heavenly assembly |
{: .ff-wrap}

Outside Genesis, **every one of these is in heaven.** (The last is a
manuscript reading: the traditional Hebrew text of Deuteronomy 32:8 says
"sons of *Israel*," the Greek Bible says "angels of God," and a Dead Sea
Scroll has "sons of God"; the NET follows the scroll.) The Bible does call
*people* God's children — Israel is "my son, my firstborn" (Exodus 4:22),
"children of the LORD your God" (Deuteronomy 14:1), "children of the living
God" (Hosea 1:10), and the king is told "You are my son" (Psalm 2:7) — and
the other two readings of Genesis 6 lean on those verses. But none of them
uses this phrase. The [Scripture sheet](elsewhere-in-scripture.html) prints
them all.

### 4. "My Spirit will not remain… forever, since they are flesh" (6:3)

> *lōʾ-**yādôn** rûḥî bāʾādām **ləʿōlām** bəšaggam hûʾ **bāśār***

Three things.

***Yādôn*** appears nowhere else in the Bible, and nobody is sure what it
means. The ancient translations guessed *remain* (Greek, Latin), *strive*
(KJV), or *judge*. The sense of the sentence survives the guess.

***Ləʿōlām***, "**forever**," is the word of Genesis 3:22: *"he must not be
allowed to… take also from the tree of life and eat, and **live forever**"*
(*wāḥay ləʿōlām*). Twice now God has drawn the same line — humans do not get
*ʿōlām* — once after humans reached upward for it, and now after heaven
reached down.

***Bāśār***, "**flesh**," is the NET's *mortal*. The breath in humankind is
God's (2:7); the body is dust. The verdict is that it will stay that way.

(For the 120 years — a new limit on a lifetime, or the time left before the
flood — see [Session 7, Part 4](../../07-generations-of-adam/). Both readings
are old; the session does not depend on either.)

### 5. "The Nephilim" (6:4)

> ***hannəpilîm*** *hāyû bāʾāreṣ bayyāmîm hāhēm* ***wəgam ʾaḥărê-kēn***

**The word.** *Nəpilîm* occurs in exactly two places in the Bible: here, and
twice in one verse of the spies' report (Numbers 13:33). It looks like it
comes from *nāpal*, "**to fall**," and the old guesses all start there:
*the fallen ones* (fallen from heaven, or fallen in battle — Ezekiel 32:27
calls ancient warriors *gibbōrîm nōpəlîm*, "fallen mighty men"), or *those
who fall upon others*. The Greek translators wrote ***gigantes***, and from
the Greek through the Latin it reached the King James as ***giants***.

**What the verse does not say.** Nothing in Genesis 6 says the Nephilim were
**tall**. Size enters from Numbers 13:33 — *"we seemed like grasshoppers"* —
and from the Greek word, and then from later retellings. Genesis says only
that they were on the earth, and when.

**"And also after this."** The parenthesis looks forward on purpose. The only
other time the Nephilim appear, after the flood, is in the mouths of the ten
spies — in a report the Bible calls *dibbâ*, a "bad" or "evil report"
(Numbers 13:32; 14:36–37). Part 3 of the session takes that up.

**Who are "they"?** The grammar is loose. The last line — *"they were the
mighty heroes of old"* — most naturally means the **children** born to the
daughters of humankind. Whether those children *are* the Nephilim, or the
Nephilim were already there when it happened, the verse does not quite say.
Most readers, ancient and modern, have taken them as the same.

### 6. "The mighty heroes of old, the men of the name" (6:4)

> *hēmmâ **haggibbōrîm** ʾăšer **mēʿôlām** ʾanšê **haššēm***

***Gibbōrîm*** are warrior-heroes: Nimrod is a *gibbōr* (10:8–9), and so are
David's "mighty men" (2 Samuel 23:8). ***Mēʿôlām***, "of old," is the same
word as *forever* in the verse before, used backward: these are the heroes
of *ʿōlām*, the legendary past.

***ʾAnšê haššēm*** is literally "**the men of the name**" — the NET's
*famous men*. The exact phrase occurs once more in the Bible: the 250
"famous men" who rebel with Korah against Moses (Numbers 16:2) — a link the
rabbis made too (*Genesis Rabbah* 26:7). And
**"name"** becomes a thread. At Babel the builders say *"let us **make a
name** for ourselves"* (11:4). One chapter later God says to Abram, *"**I**
will make your name great"* (12:2). And two verses after this one, Noah's
first son is introduced: ***Shem*** — which is simply the Hebrew word
*šēm*, "**Name**" (6:10). The line that carries the promise is called Name.

### 7. "Every inclination of the thoughts of their hearts" (6:5)

> *wəkol-**yēṣer** maḥšəbōt **libbô** raq raʿ kol-hayyôm*

The NET's *minds* is literally ***heart*** (*lēb*), which in Hebrew is where
you think and decide, not only where you feel. Keep the word; it comes back
in the next verse.

***Yēṣer***, "inclination," is a noun from ***yāṣar***, the potter's verb —
the verb of Genesis 2:7, *"the LORD God **formed** the man from the soil."*
A *yēṣer* is a *form*, a shape that is being made. The creature God formed is
now forming things of its own, and they are *only evil, all the day*. The
same diagnosis returns after the flood (8:21), and the rabbis built their
teaching of the *yēṣer hāraʿ*, the "evil inclination," on these two verses.

### 8. "The LORD regretted… and it pained him to his heart" (6:6)

> ***wayyinnāḥem*** *YHWH…* ***wayyitʿaṣṣēb*** *ʾel-*___libbô___

The NET's *he was highly offended* hides the most important word in the
passage. ***Wayyitʿaṣṣēb*** is from ***ʿ-ṣ-b***, **pain**: "it **pained**
him," "he was **grieved**" — to his ***heart***. Now look at where Genesis
has used those two roots before:

| | *n-ḥ-m* — comfort / regret | *ʿ-ṣ-b* — pain, toil |
|---|---|---|
| **3:16–17** | — | *"with **pain** you will give birth"*; *"in **painful toil** you will eat"* |
| **5:29** — Lamech names Noah | *"this one will **comfort** us"* | *"from the **painful toil** of our hands"* |
| **6:6** — the LORD | *"the LORD **regretted**"* | *"it **pained** him to his heart"* |
{: .ff-wrap}

Lamech hoped his son would bring ***comfort*** from the ***pain*** of the
cursed ground. The next time Genesis uses both words, the one feeling them is
**God**. The pain the curse laid on the woman and the man in 3:16–17, God now
takes into his own heart. Isaiah says the same of Israel in the wilderness:
*"they rebelled and **grieved** his Holy Spirit"* (63:10 — the same root;
the NET again says *offended*), and Paul echoes Isaiah: *"do not grieve the
Holy Spirit of God"* (Ephesians 4:30).

**Two hearts.** Verse 5 and verse 6 end on the same word, *libbô*, "his
heart" — first the human heart, whose every shape is evil, then God's heart,
which is in pain. And after the flood the LORD will speak *"to his heart"*
again (8:21, NET *to himself*) — and what he says there is mercy.

**"Regretted."** *Niḥam* can mean *be sorry*, *relent*, *change course*, or
*be comforted*. First Samuel 15 uses it three times in one chapter: *"I regret
that I have made Saul king"* (15:11), *"the Preeminent One of Israel does not…
change his mind, for he is not a human being"* (15:29), and *"the LORD
regretted that he had made Saul king"* (15:35). The same writer holds both,
on purpose: God does not waver like a man, and God is not unmoved. The
[rabbinic sheet](rabbinic-sources.html) has the second-century answer to a
man who asked how a God who knows the future could be grieved by it — and a
third-century rabbi's proof that the grief of 6:6 is **mourning**, from the
day David wept for Absalom: *"The king is **grieved** over his son"* (2 Samuel
19:2, the same root).

**One more echo.** This exact verb form — *wayyitʿaṣṣēb*, "was pained" —
appears once more in the whole Hebrew Bible: Genesis 34:7, when Jacob's sons
hear that Dinah has been taken by the prince of the land (see note 2). Both
times, the grief follows a daughter being taken.

### 9. "I will wipe out… from the face of the ground" (6:7)

> ***ʾemḥeh*** *ʾet-hāʾādām… mēʿal pənê* ***hāʾădāmâ***

*Māḥâ* is to **wipe** — what you do to a dish (2 Kings 21:13), or to a name
written in a book (Exodus 32:32). And the pun of 2:7 runs backward: the
*ʾādām* who was taken from the *ʾădāmâ* is to be wiped off the face of it.
The list that follows — *humankind, animals, creatures that move on the
ground, birds* — walks backward through the sixth and fifth days of Genesis
1, and *"whom I have created"* uses the verb of Genesis 1, *bārāʾ*. This is
creation being taken down. When the flood comes, 7:23 repeats the list word
for word.

### 10. "But Noah found favor" (6:8)

> ***wənōaḥ*** *māṣāʾ* ***ḥēn*** — "but **Noah** found **favor**"

In Hebrew, *Noah* is two letters, **נח** (*n-ḥ*). *Favor*, *ḥēn*, is the same
two letters reversed, **חן** (*ḥ-n*). The man is his own answer, spelled
backward.

And ***ḥēn*** — favor, grace — appears here **for the first time in the
Bible.** Notice the order. Verse 8 says Noah *found* grace. Only verse 9 says
he was *righteous*. The first time grace is named in Scripture, it arrives
before any description of the man who receives it.

# Babylon's tower

A source sheet for **[Session 11](../)**. Israel's neighbours have no story
of a tower to heaven that God stopped; no such tablet has ever been found.
What they have is the story Babylon told about itself — its city built by the
gods, its temple-tower raised by its kings *"to rival the heavens"* — and,
further afield, a few stories of giants climbing to heaven and of how
languages began. They are gathered here, with what the scholars who collected
them made of them.

---

## 1. Built by the gods: *Enuma Elish*

Session 1 read Babylon's creation epic, which begins *"When in the height
heaven was not named, / And the earth beneath did not yet bear a name"* and
ends with the gods proclaiming Marduk's fifty names (**[the full
text](../../01-the-neighbors-stories/texts/enuma-elish.html)**). The passage
in between, where the gods build Babylon, was not known when that translation
was made in 1902; it was recovered from tablets found at Assur and published in
1919–1923.

> "Build Babylon, whose construction you have desired;
> let a city be built, let there be fashioned a covered(?) shrine."
> The Anunnaki carried the basket, the first year [they made] its bricks.
> On the approach of the second year they raised aloft the head of Esagila, over against the Apsu,
> they built the *zigurrat* (temple-tower) of the upper Apsu,
> for Marduk, Enlil (and) Ea established his heart's desire therein.
> …
> he caused the gods, his fathers, to sit down at his board (with the words): "This Babylon, let it be the abode of your dwelling."
> — *Enuma Elish VI 57–72*, trans. D. D. Luckenbill (1921)

Stephen Langdon's translation of the same lines, two years later:

> The Anunnaki *seized the pickaxe*;
> For one year they were making its bricks.
> When the second year arrived
> they raised the top of Esagila the imitation of the nether sea.
> They built the lofty stage-tower on the nether-sea.
> For Marduk, who is Enlil and Ea, they established his temple as his abode.
> — *Enuma Elish VI 59–64*, trans. S. Langdon (1923)

*Esagila* ("House whose top is raised") was Marduk's temple in Babylon; the
*ziggurat*, or "stage-tower," stood beside it. The *Apsu* is the freshwater
deep beneath the earth: the temple's top was built to match the deep below.
(The line just before, in which Marduk names the city *Bābili*, *"homes of the
great gods,"* V 129, has no open English translation.)

## 2. Built by the kings: Etemenanki

The temple-tower of Babylon was called ***Etemenanki***, Sumerian for "House,
Foundation-Platform of Heaven and Earth" (or "…of Heaven and the Underworld").
It stood about 91 metres square at its base. No one knows when it was first
built; Andrew George argues it was standing in the second millennium BC, when
*Enuma Elish* (above) has the gods build Babylon's ziggurat. Sennacherib of
Assyria boasted of tearing it down when he sacked Babylon in 689 BC, and
Esarhaddon began the rebuilding. It was raised again by Nabopolassar (626–605 BC)
and finished by his son
Nebuchadnezzar II (605–562), the king who took Jerusalem. Alexander the Great
had what was left of it cleared for a rebuilding that never came.

### Nabopolassar

> At that time, (with regard to) Etemenanki — the ziggurat of Babylon, which
> before my time had become very weak (and) had been allowed to collapse —
> the god Marduk — the lord — commanded me to firmly secure its foundation on
> the surface (lit. "breast") of the netherworld (and) to have its summit
> rival the heavens.
>
> I fashioned hoes, spades, and brickmolds (made) of elephant ivory, ebony,
> and *musukkannu*-wood, and made the vast number of workmen levied in my land
> carry (them). I had (them) make mud bricks without number (and) mold baked
> bricks like countless drops of rain. I had the Araḫtu River carry refined
> (and) crude bitumen like a raging flood.
> — *Nabopolassar, Etemenanki cylinder* (RIBo Nabopolassar 6, col. i)

The Akkadian of *"to have its summit rival the heavens"* is *rēšīša šamāmī ana
šitnunim*. Genesis 11:4: *"a tower with its top in the heavens."* Later in the
same inscription the king says he *"carried mud bricks and mud on my head"*
himself, and prays: *"Like the bricks of Etemenanki, which are firmly in place
for eternity, firmly secure the foundation(s) of my throne."*

### Nebuchadnezzar

> (With regard to) Etemenanki, the ziggurat of Babylon, whose emplacement
> Nabopolassar, the king of Babylon, my father who engendered me, had had
> purified… and whose foundation(s) he had firmly placed on the surface (lit.
> "breast") of the netherworld, whose four walls he had raised thirty cubits on
> the outside using bitumen and baked brick, but whose superstructure he had
> not raised up (to its summit), I set to work raising the superstructure of
> Etemenanki to have (its summit) rival the heavens.
>
> I mustered the widespread people whom the god Marduk, my lord, had entrusted
> to me… all of the lands, everywhere, (and) every single inhabited settlement
> from the Upper Sea to the Lower Sea, far-off lands, the widespread people of
> the inhabited world, kings of remote mountains and distant islands that are
> in the Upper and Lower Seas… and I imposed (the carrying of) basket(s) on
> the workmen of the gods Šamaš and Marduk in order to build Etemenanki.
>
> For the god Marduk, my lord, I skillfully built a h[oly] shrine, a
> well-adorn[ed] bedroo[m], as (it was in) distant days, on its summit.
> — *Nebuchadnezzar II, Etemenanki cylinder* (RIBo Nebuchadnezzar II 27,
> ii 1–iv 4)

The "Upper Sea" is the Mediterranean and the "Lower Sea" the Persian Gulf: the
whole known world, from one edge of the map in Genesis 10 to the other,
carrying baskets of brick to one tower. Older translations, in the public
domain, say the same: *"Marduk the lord commanded me to lay its foundation in
the heart of the earth (and) to raise its turrets to heaven"* (Nabopolassar,
trans. Hilprecht, in R. F. Harper, 1901); *"All peoples of scattered
habitations whom Marduk bestowed upon me I compelled to do service for the
building of E-temin-anki"* (Nebuchadnezzar, trans. S. Langdon, 1905 —
"scattered" there renders *rapšātim*, "widespread"; it is not the Hebrew word
of Genesis 11).

### His name on the bricks

Most of the bricks Nebuchadnezzar made carry the same stamp:

> Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, patron of (the temple) E-sagil and (the
> temple) E-zida, eldest son of Nabopolassar, king of Babylon, am I.
> — *British Museum, Guide to the Babylonian and Assyrian Antiquities* (1908)

### The names

- ***Etemenanki***: "House, Foundation-Platform of Heaven and Earth" — *"house
  of the foundation of heaven and earth"* (Harper, 1901). The Sumerian *ki* can
  mean "earth" or "underworld."
- ***Bābili***, Babylon: written with signs meaning "gate of the god(s)." The
  excavator Robert Koldewey: *"the earliest settlement, which was named Babilu
  or Babilani, the gate of god or gate of the gods"* (1914). Most scholars now
  think "gate of the god" was itself the Babylonians' own explanation of an
  older name, *Babil(l)a*, of unknown meaning (I. J. Gelb, 1955). Genesis gives
  the name a third meaning, from Hebrew *bālal*, "confuse."

## 3. A visitor's description: Herodotus

The Greek historian Herodotus wrote in the 450s–430s BC. Whether he saw
Babylon himself is debated; his description agrees in many points with the
excavations.

> As they dug the fosse, they made bricks of the earth which was carried out of
> the place they dug, and when they had moulded bricks enough they baked them in
> ovens; then using hot bitumen for cement and interposing layers of wattled
> reeds at every thirtieth course of bricks, they built first the border of the
> fosse and then the wall itself in the same fashion.
> — *Herodotus 1.179*

> In the midmost of the other is still to this day the sacred enclosure of Zeus
> Belus, a square of two furlongs each way, with gates of bronze. In the centre
> of this enclosure a solid tower has been built, of one furlong's length and
> breadth; a second tower rises from this, and from it yet another, till at
> last there are eight. The way up to them mounts spirally outside all the
> towers; about halfway in the ascent is a halting place, with seats for
> repose, where those who ascend sit down and rest. In the last tower there is a
> great shrine; and in it a great and well-covered couch is laid, and a golden
> table set hard by. But no image has been set up in the shrine, nor does any
> human creature lie therein for the night, except one native woman, chosen
> from all women by the god, as say the Chaldaeans, who are priests of this
> god.
>
> These same Chaldaeans say (but I do not believe them) that the god himself is
> wont to visit the shrine and rest upon the couch…
> — *Herodotus 1.181–182*

*"Zeus Belus"* is Herodotus's name for Bel-Marduk. Compare the tower in
Genesis: *brick* for stone and *bitumen* for mortar (11:3) — and a God who
*"came down to see"* (11:5).

## 4. Gilgamesh: a name, and a wall of baked brick

### "I will establish my name"

In the Old Babylonian version, Gilgamesh answers his friend Enkidu's fear of
the monster Humbaba:

> If I fall, I will establish my name.
> …
> I am determined
> [to enter] the cedar forest.
> I will, indeed, establish my name.
> — *Gilgamesh, Yale tablet 148, 158–160*, trans. M. Jastrow and A. T. Clay (1920)

R. Campbell Thompson's verse rendering of the same speech (1928): *"(I,
forsooth), if I should fall, my name will have stablish'd (for ever)."* Modern
editors read the speech's opening line as a question about who can *go up to
heaven* — only the gods, Gilgamesh answers, live for ever; mortals' days are
numbered, so a man must make a name. (That reading is newer than any
public-domain translation.)

### The wall of Uruk

At the end of the epic Gilgamesh comes home from his search for eternal life
with nothing — and shows the boatman his city wall:

> Gilgamish spake to him, (yea), to the boatman Ur-Shanabi (this wise):
> "Do thou, Ur-Shanabi, go up and walk on the ramparts of Erech,
> Look on its base, and take heed of its bricks, if its bricks be not kiln-burnt,
> — *Gilgamesh XI 322–325*, trans. R. Campbell Thompson (1928)

The same lines open the epic (I 18–23). Thompson's next line, about a
foundation of *"bitumen,"* is an old misreading: the Akkadian asks whether the
Seven Sages did not lay its foundations. The brick is right — *agurru*,
kiln-fired brick. *Erech* is Uruk, one of the first cities of Nimrod's kingdom
(Genesis 10:10).

## 5. One language: *Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta*

A Sumerian epic about Enmerkar, a legendary king of Uruk, contains a spell,
"the incantation of Nudimmud" (Nudimmud is the god Enki):

> "On that day when there is no snake, when there is no scorpion, when there is
> no hyena, when there is no lion, when there is neither dog nor wolf, when
> there is thus neither fear nor trembling, man has no rival! At such a time,
> may the lands of Šubur and Ḫamazi, the many-tongued, and Sumer, the great
> mountain of the me of magnificence, and Akkad, the land possessing all that is
> befitting, and the Martu land, resting in security — the whole universe, the
> well-guarded people — may they all address Enlil together in a single
> language! For at that time, for the ambitious lords, for the ambitious
> princes, for the ambitious kings… Enki, the lord of abundance and of
> steadfast decisions, the wise and knowing lord of the Land, the expert of the
> gods, chosen for wisdom, the lord of Eridug, shall change the speech in their
> mouths, as many as he had placed there, and so the speech of mankind is truly
> one."
> — *Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta 136–155* (ETCSL)

When Samuel Noah Kramer published the passage in 1943 he read it as the past —
a golden age when all peoples spoke one language, ended when Enki changed
their speech: a Sumerian "Babel of tongues," and Thorkild Jacobsen kept that
reading. The grammar allows either, and since the 1970s many scholars (Bendt
Alster, Herman Vanstiphout and others) have read it as a spell about the
*future*, a day when Enki will bring the many-tongued lands to one speech; the
translation above reads it that way. Either way, it has no tower and no scattering. The same poem has the
king ask for a temple *"brought down from heaven"* and calls him a tree
*"uniting heaven and earth; its crown reaches heaven."*

## 6. Further afield

### Giants who piled mountains (Greece)

> They were fain to pile Ossa on Olympus, and Pelion, with its waving forests,
> on Ossa, that so heaven might be scaled. And this they would have
> accomplished, if they had reached the measure of manhood; but the son of
> Zeus, whom fair-haired Leto bore, slew them both before the down blossomed
> beneath their temples and covered their chins with a full growth of beard.
> — *Homer, Odyssey 11.315–320*, trans. A. T. Murray (1919)

Philo of Alexandria knew that critics compared Babel to this story, and
answered them (**[Babel among its readers](early-readers.html)**, §1).

### Hermes divides the languages (Rome)

A Latin handbook of myths by Hyginus has the only classical story of a
division of languages. Sir James Frazer's summary (1918):

> Thus the Greeks had a tradition that for many ages men lived at peace,
> without cities and without laws, speaking one language, and ruled by Zeus
> alone. At last Hermes introduced diversities of speech and divided mankind
> into separate nations. So discord first arose among mortals, and Zeus,
> offended at their quarrels, resigned the sovereignty and committed it to the
> hands of the Argive hero Phoroneus, the first king of men.
> — *Frazer, Folk-Lore in the Old Testament* (1918), i. 384, on Hyginus,
> *Fabulae* 143

(Frazer embroiders a little: Hyginus has no "peace" and no resigning. There is
no tower.)

### Around the world

Frazer collected tower and language stories from every continent (*Folk-Lore
in the Old Testament*, vol. I, ch. 5). He found stories of people trying to
climb to the sky — piling up poles or porridge pestles in Africa, a pole in
Assam — but none of them with a confusion of tongues; and stories of how
languages began, but none of them with a tower. Where a tower and a confusion
of tongues turned up together — in Mexico, among the Karen of Burma, in the
Admiralty Islands — he judged them borrowed from the Bible or from
missionaries: *"There can be little doubt that this story is merely an echo of
missionary teaching"* (of the Admiralty Islands). Of the Mexican version, set
at the great pyramid of Cholula, he noted the bitumen: *"bitumen seems never to
have been used by the Mexicans for such a purpose and is not found anywhere
near Cholula."* His conclusion about the stories that explain languages
without a tower: they *"may… be accepted as independent efforts of the human
mind to grapple with that difficult problem."*

## 7. The tower tablet that wasn't

In 1876 George Smith, who had found the Babylonian flood story four years
earlier (Session 10), announced a Babylonian account of the tower:

> . . . . Babylon brought to subjection,
> [small] and great he confounded their speech.
> . . . . Babylon brought to subjection,
> [small] and great he confounded their speech.
> their strong place (tower) all the day they founded;
> to their strong place in the night
> entirely he made an end.
> — *G. Smith, The Chaldean Account of Genesis* (1876), on tablet K 3657

L. W. King re-read it in 1902:

> There is, however, no mention of a tower or building of any sort, and Babylon
> is referred to as suffering through the evil designs of the god or king… the
> lines supposed to recount the destruction of the tower by night really
> describe how the complaints of the oppressed people prevented the king, or
> possibly an avenger of the people, from getting any sleep at night upon his
> couch.

And E. A. Wallis Budge of the British Museum, in 1921:

> In like manner the text on K. 3657, which Smith thought referred to the Tower
> of Babel, is now known to contain no mention of a tower or building of any
> sort. … Enquiries are from time to time made at the Museum for tablets which
> deal with the Temptation of Eve, and the destruction of the Tower of Babel,
> and the Divine commands to Adam and Eve; it is perhaps not superfluous to say
> that nothing of the kind exists.

Some popular websites still give Smith's reading.

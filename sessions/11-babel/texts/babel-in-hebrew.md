# Babel to Abram in Hebrew

A source sheet for **[Session 11](../)**. Genesis 10, the table of nations,
is on its own sheet, **[the table of nations](the-table-of-nations.html)**,
with the map. This sheet gives the rest of the passage, Genesis 11:1–12:3, and
fourteen notes on what the Hebrew does that an English translation cannot
easily show. All counts are from the Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible.

---

## Babel: Genesis 11:1–9

> **1** The whole earth had a common language and a common vocabulary. **2**
> When the people moved eastward, they found a plain in Shinar and settled
> there. **3** Then they said to one another, "Come, let's make bricks and bake
> them thoroughly." (They had brick instead of stone and tar instead of
> mortar.) **4** Then they said, "Come, let's build ourselves a city and a
> tower with its top in the heavens so that we may make a name for ourselves.
> Otherwise we will be scattered across the face of the entire earth."
>
> **5** But the LORD came down to see the city and the tower that the people
> had started building. **6** And the LORD said, "If as one people all sharing
> a common language they have begun to do this, then nothing they plan to do
> will be beyond them. **7** Come, let's go down and confuse their language so
> they won't be able to understand each other." **8** So the LORD scattered
> them from there across the face of the entire earth, and they stopped
> building the city. **9** That is why its name was called Babel—because there
> the LORD confused the language of the entire world, and from there the LORD
> scattered them across the face of the entire earth.

## From Shem to Abram: Genesis 11:10–32

> **10** This is the account of Shem. Shem was 100 years old when he became the
> father of Arphaxad, two years after the flood. **11** And after becoming the
> father of Arphaxad, Shem lived 500 years and had other sons and daughters.
>
> **12** When Arphaxad had lived 35 years, he became the father of Shelah.
> **13** And after he became the father of Shelah, Arphaxad lived 403 years and
> had other sons and daughters.
>
> **14** When Shelah had lived 30 years, he became the father of Eber. **15**
> And after he became the father of Eber, Shelah lived 403 years and had other
> sons and daughters.
>
> **16** When Eber had lived 34 years, he became the father of Peleg. **17**
> And after he became the father of Peleg, Eber lived 430 years and had other
> sons and daughters.
>
> **18** When Peleg had lived 30 years, he became the father of Reu. **19** And
> after he became the father of Reu, Peleg lived 209 years and had other sons
> and daughters.
>
> **20** When Reu had lived 32 years, he became the father of Serug. **21** And
> after he became the father of Serug, Reu lived 207 years and had other sons
> and daughters.
>
> **22** When Serug had lived 30 years, he became the father of Nahor. **23**
> And after he became the father of Nahor, Serug lived 200 years and had other
> sons and daughters.
>
> **24** When Nahor had lived 29 years, he became the father of Terah. **25**
> And after he became the father of Terah, Nahor lived 119 years and had other
> sons and daughters.
>
> **26** When Terah had lived 70 years, he became the father of Abram, Nahor,
> and Haran.
>
> **27** This is the account of Terah. Terah became the father of Abram, Nahor,
> and Haran. And Haran became the father of Lot. **28** Haran died in the land
> of his birth, in Ur of the Chaldeans, while his father Terah was still alive.
> **29** And Abram and Nahor took wives for themselves. The name of Abram's
> wife was Sarai. And the name of Nahor's wife was Milcah; she was the daughter
> of Haran, who was the father of both Milcah and Iscah. **30** But Sarai was
> barren; she had no children.
>
> **31** Terah took his son Abram, his grandson Lot (the son of Haran), and his
> daughter-in-law Sarai, his son Abram's wife, and with them he set out from Ur
> of the Chaldeans to go to Canaan. When they came to Haran, they settled
> there. **32** The lifetime of Terah was 205 years, and he died in Haran.

## The call: Genesis 12:1–3

> **1** Now the LORD said to Abram, "Go out from your country, your relatives,
> and your father's household to the land that I will show you. **2** Then I
> will make you into a great nation, and I will bless you, and I will make your
> name great, so that you will exemplify divine blessing. **3** I will bless
> those who bless you, but the one who treats you lightly I must curse, so that
> all the families of the earth may receive blessing through you."

## Fourteen notes

### 1. "One lip"

*"A common language and a common vocabulary"* (11:1) is, in Hebrew, ***śāpâ
ʾeḥāt ûḏəḇārîm ʾăḥāḏîm*** — "one lip, and one set of words." *Lip* is the
word for *language* all through the story: five times in nine verses (11:1, 6,
7 twice, 9). The prophet uses it when he looks ahead to the day the
scattering is undone. The NET has *"I will then enable the nations to give me
acceptable praise. All of them will invoke the LORD's name"* (Zephaniah 3:9);
the Hebrew is *"I will give the peoples a pure **lip**."* The nations, calling
on the *name*.

Chapter 10 has already said *"every one according to its language"* (10:5),
with *lāšôn*, "tongue," the ordinary word. So the two chapters do not run in
order: chapter 11 goes back to explain how chapter 10 came to be.

### 2. "From the east," or "eastward"?

*Miqqedem* (11:2) can mean either. The NET reads *eastward*, the Greek
translators and Rashi *from the east*. If *eastward*, the builders go the way
Genesis's exiles go: the man and woman are driven out *"on the eastern side of
the orchard in Eden"* (3:24) and Cain settles *"east of Eden"* (4:16).

### 3. "They settled there" — twice

***Wayyēšəḇû šām***, "and they settled there," closes 11:2 — and closes 11:31,
when Terah's family, bound for Canaan, gets as far as Haran. The same two
words, twenty-nine verses apart. The next thing God says is *"Go"* (12:1).

### 4. The sound of bricks

*"Come, let's make bricks and bake them thoroughly"* (11:3) is ***hāḇâ
nilbənâ ləḇēnîm wənišrəpâ liśrēpâ***, literally "let's brick bricks and burn
them to a burning." And the aside, *"They had brick instead of stone and tar
instead of mortar,"* is ***wattəhî lāhem halləḇēnâ ləʾāḇen wəhaḥēmār hāyâ
lāhem laḥōmer*** — *ləḇēnâ / ʾeḇen*, *ḥēmār / ḥōmer*, each pair a near-rhyme.
The aside explains bricks and bitumen as if to people who build with stone and
mortar. Mesopotamia had clay and bitumen and very little stone; Herodotus,
describing Babylon in the fifth century BCE, tells of walls of baked brick
laid in hot bitumen (on **[Babylon's tower](babylons-tower.html)**).

These exact words come back once more: Pharaoh makes the Israelites' lives
bitter *"by hard service with mortar and bricks"* — *bəḥōmer ûḇilḇēnîm*
(Exodus 1:14). The next empire's building project, in the same two words.

### 5. "Come" — three times

*Hāḇâ*, "come on," opens the builders' plans twice (11:3, 4) and God's once
(11:7). God answers them in their own words — *"they said, 'come, let's make
bricks… come, let's build,' and I say, 'come, let's go down'"* is how Umberto
Cassuto paraphrased it, following Rashi. Look at the letters, too: they said
***nilbənâ***, "let's make bricks" (n-l-b-n); God says ***nāḇəlâ***, "let's
confuse" (n-b-l) — nearly the same letters, turned around.

### 6. "For ourselves" — twice

*"Let's build ourselves a city… so that we may make a name for ourselves"*
(11:4): ***niḇneh-lānû… wənaʿăśeh-lānû šēm*** — "let's build *for us*… let's
make *for us* a name." Hold the verb *make* (*ʿāśâ*): God uses it in 12:2, *"I
will make you into a great nation,"* ***wəʾeʿeśḵā ləḡôy gāḏôl***. They make
themselves a name; God makes Abram a nation, and makes his name great.

### 7. The mirror

Read the story from both ends toward the middle and it folds. This layout
follows J. P. Fokkelman (*Narrative Art in Genesis*, 1975) and G. J. Wenham
(*Genesis 1–15*, 1987):

| | |
|---|---|
| **A** — the whole earth, one language (1) | **A′** — the language of the whole earth (9) |
| **B** — they settled *there* (2) | **B′** — the LORD scattered them from *there* (8) |
| **C** — *to one another* (3) | **C′** — *not understand each other* (7) |
| **D** — *"Come, let's make bricks"* (3) | **D′** — *"Come, let's go down and confuse"* (7) |
| **E** — *"let's build… a city and a tower"* (4) | **E′** — *"the city and the tower that the people had started building"* (5) |
| **X** — ***"But the LORD came down to see"*** (5) | |
{: .ff-wrap}

The turning point is God stooping to look. Everything before it is the
builders' plan; everything after it is God's answer.

### 8. *Šām, šēm, šāmayim*

***Šām***, "there," comes five times in nine verses (11:2, 7, 8, 9 twice).
The builders wanted a ***šēm***, "name" (11:4), with its top in the
***šāmayim***, "heavens" (11:4). The name they get is in verse 9: *"That is why
its name (**šəmāh**) was called Babel."* And the very next verse begins *"This
is the account of **Šēm**"* — Shem, "Name." (Cassuto drew attention to the
play between *šām* and *šēm* in this story.)

### 9. *Babel* and *bālal*

*"That is why its name was called Babel — because there the LORD confused
(***bālal***) the language"* (11:9). In Babylon's own language the name was
***Bāb-ili***, "gate of the god." Genesis gives it a Hebrew meaning instead,
as it does with many names (*Eve*, 3:20; *Noah*, 5:29; *Peleg*, 10:25). The
"why it is called" formula, *ʿal-kēn qārāʾ šəmāh*, is one of Genesis's
narrator's asides (Session 3).

### 10. "Scattered"

The verb ***pûṣ***, "scatter," appears in Genesis only five times: the
Canaanite families "were scattered" (10:18), with no hint of judgment; the
builders fear it (11:4); and the LORD does it, twice (11:8, 9). Its only other
use in Genesis is Jacob's last words over Simeon and Levi: *"I will scatter
them in Israel"* (49:7). The verb of 9:19, *"from them the whole earth was
populated,"* is *nāp̄ṣâ*, a close cousin of *pûṣ*; chapter 10's *"separated"*
and *"spread"* (10:5, 32) is *nip̄rəḏû*. What changes at Babel is not the word
but who does it: only there is the LORD the one who scatters.

### 11. "Began"

The verb *ḥālal*, "begin," appears five times in Genesis 1–11 (*hûḥal* at
4:26, *hēḥēl* at 6:1 and 10:8, *wayyāḥel* at 9:20, *haḥillām* at 11:6): people
*began* to call on the name of the LORD (4:26); humankind *began* to multiply
(6:1); Noah *began* to plant a vineyard (9:20); Nimrod *began* to be a mighty
warrior (10:8); *"this is what they have begun to do"* (11:6). The rabbis
and the Aramaic translators read even 4:26 darkly, as the beginning of
idolatry (Session 7). The NET keeps *began* in all five, though at 4:26 it
renders the rest of the phrase *"began to worship the LORD."*

### 12. Shem's ten generations

| | **Became a father at** | **Lived after** | **Whole life** |
|---|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| Shem | 100 | 500 | 600 |
| Arphaxad | 35 | 403 | 438 |
| Shelah | 30 | 403 | 433 |
| Eber | 34 | 430 | 464 |
| Peleg | 30 | 209 | 239 |
| Reu | 32 | 207 | 239 |
| Serug | 30 | 200 | 230 |
| Nahor | 29 | 119 | 148 |
| Terah | 70 | — | 205 (11:32) |
| Abram | — | — | 175 (25:7) |

The whole lives are the Hebrew text's own sums; Genesis 11 does not state them,
and, unlike Genesis 5, it never says *"and then he died."* The Greek
translation adds an eleventh name, Kainan, between Arphaxad and Shelah — and
Luke's genealogy of Jesus follows the Greek (Luke 3:36). *Pirkei Avot* 5:2
counts ten generations from Noah to Abraham, as from Adam to Noah (see
**[Babel among its readers](early-readers.html)**).

### 13. "Barren; she had no child"

*"But Sarai was barren; she had no children"* (11:30) says it twice: ***ʿăqārâ
ʾên lāh wālāḏ***, "barren — there was not to her a child." The section of Genesis that began with *"be fruitful and multiply"*
(1:28) ends on a woman who cannot.

### 14. Five blessings, five curses

***Lek-ləḵā***, *"Go, you"* (12:1) — the *ləḵā* can be heard as *"for
yourself,"* and Rashi reads it so: *"for your own benefit and for your own
good."* Then count *bless* in verses 2–3:

| | **Hebrew** | **NET** |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *wa-ʾăḇārekḵā* | "I will bless you" |
| 2 | *bərāḵâ* | "you will exemplify divine blessing" (literally, "be a blessing") |
| 3 | *wa-ʾăḇārăḵâ* | "I will bless" |
| 4 | *məḇārăḵeḵā* | "those who bless you" |
| 5 | *wə-niḇrəḵû* | "may receive blessing" |

And the word *cursed* (*ʾārar*) in Genesis 1–11: the serpent (3:14), the
ground (3:17), Cain (4:11), the ground again (5:29), Canaan (9:25). Five. (The
other "curse" word, *qillēl*, "treat lightly," is the one God promises never
to do to the ground again, 8:21, and the one used of Abram's despiser in 12:3.)

Two more things in verse 3. Those who bless Abram are plural, *"those who bless
you"*; the one who despises him is singular, *"the one who treats you
lightly"* — as if God expects many of the one and few of the other. And *"all
the families of the earth"* is ***kōl mišpəḥōt hāʾăḏāmâ*** — *families*, the
word of the refrain in chapter 10 (10:5, 18, 20, 31, 32), and *ʾăḏāmâ*, the
ground cursed in 3:17. The verb *niḇrəḵû* can be read *"will be blessed"* or
*"will bless themselves"* (that is, use Abram's name when they bless); the NET
splits the difference with *"may receive blessing."*

# The ground under Babel

A source sheet for **[Session 11](../)**. Genesis tells Babel as a story about
people — what they wanted, what they feared, what God did. It gives no date.
But it does give a setting: a plain in Shinar, brick and bitumen, a city and
a tower, with Erech (Uruk) among the first cities of the land (10:10). That
setting can be studied. This sheet gathers what climate scientists and
archaeologists say about it: how wet the lands around Shinar once were, how
they dried, how the first cities rose on the plain, which languages their
people spoke, and how much of all that scientists think the climate caused.

Some of it is settled and some is argued over, and the sheet tries to say
which is which. *Settled* means specialists broadly agree. *Debated* means
serious scholars disagree. Genesis tells none of this. The handout's view is
that science can describe what pressed people together on that plain, and
Genesis asks what they did with it.

## 1. The wet millennia

<figure class="map-figure">
<img src="../wet-millennia.svg" alt="Sketch map of north-east Africa, Arabia and Mesopotamia in the wet millennia before the first cities, with grassland then and desert now hatched in green, dated lakes and settlements marked, and sea and marsh at the head of the Persian Gulf about 4000 BC hatched in blue.">
<figcaption>Before the cities, about 8500–4000 BC. A sketch drawn from dated lakes and settlements; its edges are uncertain by hundreds of kilometres. How it was drawn is at the end of this section.</figcaption>
</figure>

When the last ice age ended, the summer monsoon of Africa and the Indian Ocean
grew stronger and reached farther north than it does now. Specialists call
this the **African Humid Period**, or the **"Green Sahara"**; in Arabia, the
**Holocene Humid Period**. (The more familiar phrase "Holocene Climatic
Optimum" usually means the warmest stretch of the period, which came at
different times in different places; for this region the rainfall terms are
the precise ones.) It came and went at different times in different places,
and the Levant, Anatolia and Iran each have their own story.

**Egypt and the eastern Sahara.** *Settled:* rain arrived around 8500 BC, and
people followed it into what is now one of the driest deserts on earth.
Rudolph Kuper and Stefan Kröpelin, drawing on about 150 excavations, divide
what followed into four phases (*Science*, 2006):

| | **Phase** | **What happened** |
|---|---|---|
| 8500–7000 BC | Reoccupation | first settlements in the northern regions |
| 7000–5300 BC | Formation | the main occupation; cattle herding begins |
| 5300–3500 BC | Regionalization | the Egyptian desert empties, except for refuges such as the Gilf Kebir; many move south into Sudan |
| 3500–1500 BC | Marginalization | Egypt all desert; herders hold on in northern Sudan (Wadi Howar) until about 1500 BC |
{: .ff-wrap}

"Green" here means dry grassland with seasonal lakes, not savanna: perhaps
50–100 mm of rain a year in Egypt's Western Desert (O. Bubenzer and H.
Riemer, 2007), against almost none today. The end came suddenly in some
places and gradually in others, and later the farther south one goes (T.
Shanahan and others, 2015). A count of more than 3,000 radiocarbon dates
shows the Sahara's people falling away between about 4300 and 3200 BC, while
the Nile valley stayed occupied (K. Manning and A. Timpson, 2014).

**Arabia.** *Settled:* Arabia was wetter from about 8000 to 4000 BC, with
lakes in what are now sand seas, and the wet phase ended earlier in the
north than in the south. The dated lakes on the map:

| **Lake** | **Wet** | **Source** |
|---|---|---|
| Tayma, north-west Arabia | about 6850–5950 BC | I. Neugebauer and others, 2022 |
| Jubbah, in the Nafud | from about 8000 BC; dated levels about 6950–5950 BC | Crassard and others, 2013 |
| Mundafan, edge of the Empty Quarter | about 7000–4000 BC | Crassard and others, 2013 |

*Debated:* how wet. Some argue that many of these "lakes" were marshes fed by
groundwater and that the extra rain was modest (Y. Enzel and others, 2015);
others answer that they were true lakes (M. Engel and others, 2017). In
south-east Arabia the interior was abandoned around 3950–3350 BC, and people
moved to the coasts (M. Petraglia and others, 2020).

**The Levant.** Not a simple "wet, then dry." The Soreq cave near Jerusalem
records the wettest conditions around 8000–5000 BC, but the Dead Sea stood
high again from about 3650 to 1550 BC (M. Bar-Matthews and others, 1997; C.
Migowski and others, 2006). How to reconcile the two is *debated.*

**Anatolia and Iran** went the other way: the mid-Holocene there was wetter
and more wooded than the early Holocene, with oak spreading in the Zagros
around 4300 BC and Lake Van's best conditions about 4200–2000 BC (L. Wick and
others, 2003; M. Djamali and others, 2010).

**The later droughts.** A short dry spell around 3300–3100 BC shows clearly
in the Levant. A bigger one, around 2200 BC (the "4.2 ka event"), shows in
dust from caves and sea cores across the region, and geologists now use it to
mark the start of the latest stage of the Holocene. *Debated:* how strong and
how widespread it was (a 2024 review of records around the world found it
was not unusual globally), and whether it brought down the empire of Akkad,
as Harvey Weiss argued in 1993.

**How the map was drawn.** No openly licensed reconstruction of this region's
land around 4000 BC exists. Computer climate models were tried and set
aside: run for 6,000 years ago, they leave Egypt's Western Desert almost as
dry as today, and the modellers themselves note that their monsoon falls
short of what the lake and pollen records show (C. Brierley and others,
2020). So the map is a sketch:

- Plain green is today's fertile land, as on the Part 1 map: everything that
  is not desert on the Köppen–Geiger climate map for 1991–2020 (Beck and
  others, 2023), plus the watered river valleys.
- The hatched green band in Africa reaches about 24°N, the northern edge of
  grassland at the peak (Kuper and Kröpelin). The pollen sites of the *BIOME
  6000* database agree: 6,000 years ago they show steppe as far north as the
  Gilf Kebir, about 23°N (where one site was already desert), and desert
  farther north.
- The hatched green over Arabia is a wash, not a boundary; how wet the
  interior was is the open question above.
- The dates beside the dots are the ones in the tables above.

## 2. Shinar: sea, marsh and the first cities

**The Gulf came in.** *Settled:* at the height of the ice age the Persian Gulf
was dry land, with the Tigris and Euphrates running through it to the sea.
As the ice melted, the sea filled it, reaching about today's level by around
5100 BC and standing a metre or two higher for a time after (B. Lokier and
others, 2015; B. Mauz and others, 2022). Around 4000 BC the Gulf's shoreline
ran roughly along a line from Nasiriyah to Amarah — about 250 kilometres
inland from today's coast, close to Ur and Eridu (L. Forti and others, 2022).
Eridu stood beside freshwater marsh from at least 5800 BC (C. Hritz and
others, 2012).

**Then the land came out.** As the rivers carried silt into the marshes, the
deltas pushed the coast back south. The tides that once reached far up the
rivers had faded by about 3500 BC and were gone before 3000 BC (L. Giosan and
R. Goodman, 2025). So in the south the land did not shrink as the region
dried: it grew. The marshes and the river branches shrank instead.

**The Sumerians remembered the marsh.** Uruk's founder, in a Sumerian poem:

> Where there was a marsh then in Unug, it was full of water. Where there was
> any dry land, Euphrates poplars grew there. Where there were reed-thickets,
> old reeds and young reeds grew there. Divine Enki who is king in Eridu tore
> up for me the old reeds, drained off the water completely. For fifty years
> I built, for fifty years I gave judgments. Then the Martu peoples, who know
> no agriculture, arose in all Sumer and Akkad. But the wall of Unug extended
> out across the desert like a bird net. *(Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird,
> from lines 290–321; Unug is Uruk)*

**The first cities.** *Settled:*

- **Eridu**, which Sumerian tradition counted the first city, has a run of
  some eighteen temples built one on top of another from about 5400 BC.
- **Uruk** grew through the fourth millennium. By about 3100 BC it covered
  some 250 hectares (about 600 acres); estimates of its people run from
  10,000 to 50,000.
- **Cities in the north came first, in some ways.** Tell Brak in north-east
  Syria reached about 130 hectares between 3900 and 3400 BC, on rain-fed farms
  and in a relatively wet phase.
- **The plain became a land of cities.** In the stretch of the southern plain
  surveyed by Robert McC. Adams (*Heartland of Cities*, 1981), villagers by
  the tens of thousands moved toward Uruk in the late fourth millennium. By
  the Early Dynastic II–III period (about 2700–2350 BC), 78 per cent of the
  settled area lay in sites of more than 40 hectares (about 100 acres), and
  only 10 per cent in villages. He called it the first society on earth where
  most people lived in cities.
- **Babylon** came late. It is first mentioned around 2200 BC, as a small
  town; it became a great city under Hammurabi (1792–1750 BC). The first true
  ziggurat is Ur-Nammu's at Ur, about 2100 BC. Etemenanki, the tower of
  Babylon on **[Babylon's tower](babylons-tower.html)**, was finished in the
  form Herodotus describes around 600 BC.

## 3. Did the drying make the cities?

The idea that a drying climate drove people into river valleys and cities
is a real one, with a literature behind it. It is also *debated.*

**For.** Hans Nissen argued that between about 3500 and 2500 BC the sea and
the Euphrates fell, the river shrank to a few channels, and people crowded
around the water that was left. Nick Brooks (2006) compared Egypt,
Mesopotamia and other early states and proposed that people gathered in
"refuges" with surface water as the land dried around them. Douglas and James
Kennett (2006) added the sea: a rising Gulf made rich marshes, and then drying
squeezed people into towns by the rivers, and the towns into rivalry. Even
Adams, after setting out his numbers, wrote that *if* the climate had grown
drier then, the crowding into cities and the competition for water could
hardly be a coincidence, and added at once that this was a matter for future
research.

The fullest recent comparison, by eighteen archaeologists and climate
scientists, puts it carefully:

> Thus, the evidence suggests that Mesopotamia's cultural development
> benefitted from a reliable climate regime in the Neolithic and Chalcolithic
> periods and that the 'urban revolution' coincided with a period
> characterised by regional climatic deterioration in the form of increased
> aridity punctuated by RCC [rapid climate change] in the 4th millennium BC.

They also write: *"In the south, marine regression is likely to have played a
role in the growth of settlement in the alluvium."* And they warn:

> However, linking social changes to RCC is extremely problematic.
> *(J. Clarke, N. Brooks and others, Quaternary Science Reviews, 2016)*

**Against, or something else.**

- **The first big towns of the north grew in a wet phase,** not a dry one.
  One study of the whole Fertile Crescent:

  > We cannot be certain that the climate was a causal factor in this
  > development, or that similar sites would not have arisen in drier
  > conditions… *(D. Lawrence and others, PLOS ONE, 2016)*

- **In the south, the land grew.** The newest work puts the weight on the
  sea and the delta rather than on rain:

  > Rather than invoking a hypothetical aridity affecting fluvial discharge,
  > we link this fundamental transition to environmental constraints imposed
  > by large scale coastal morphodynamics. *(L. Giosan and R. Goodman, PLOS
  > ONE, 2025)*

  Their picture: early Sumer farmed with the help of the tides, and when the
  growing delta took the tides away, people had to dig canals together — and
  the work of canals made the state.
- **Trade and rivers.** Guillermo Algaze (2008) stresses the advantages of
  the southern plain: high yields, many kinds of land close together, and
  cheap transport by boat.
- **Marshes.** Jennifer Pournelle argues that the first towns were islands
  in a marsh, and that canals came after the cities, not before.
- **Politics and war.** Adams himself thought the strongest reasons were
  rivalry between towns and the power of their rulers.
- **Egypt.** That the Sahara's people left as it dried is *settled.* That
  this "helped trigger" Egypt's kingdom (Kuper and Kröpelin's phrase) is
  *debated*: some 2,000 years separate the two, and the main recent review of
  Egypt's beginnings finds population pressure an unlikely driver (A.
  Stevenson, 2016).

**Where that leaves it.** *Settled:* rivers, marshes and the moving coast
shaped where and how the first cities formed. *Settled:* the climate around
Shinar was drying in the right broad centuries. *Debated:* whether the drying
was a cause. Many scholars count it one push among several. No one serious
makes it the only one.

## 4. Languages in the first cities

Genesis ends Babel with a confusion of speech. Is there any evidence that the
move into cities brought strife between peoples whose languages had drifted
apart? **No.** Here is what there is.

**There is nothing to test it with before about 3300 BC.** Writing begins at
Uruk around then, and the earliest tablets are accounts, written in a
language we cannot securely identify. Most scholars assume Sumerian; the
evidence for it is thin, and one leading specialist doubted it (R. Englund,
2004). Languages leave no trace in pottery or walls.

**Linguistics cannot reach back that far.** The comparative method can trace
a language family back perhaps six to eight thousand years; beyond that,
likenesses can't be told from chance. Sumerian has no known relatives at all.
The Semitic languages (Akkadian, later Hebrew and Arabic) were one language
perhaps around 3750 BC by one estimate (A. Kitchen and others, 2009;
*debated*), but there is no sign that Sumerian and Semitic share an ancestor.

**The first cities we can read mixed their languages, apparently in peace.**
Around 2600–2500 BC, about half the scribes named at Abu Salabikh, a Sumerian
town, have Semitic names (R. D. Biggs, 1974). By then Sumerian and Akkadian
had lived side by side long enough to change each other: Akkadian took
Sumerian's verb-last word order and some of its words (C. Woods, 2006).
Sumerian had a word for interpreter, *eme-bal*, "tongue-turner," already in a
word list of about 2500 BC. A cylinder seal of about 2200 BC belonged to
Shu-ilishu, "interpreter of Meluhha" (the Indus valley; the seal is in the
Louvre).

**A king who boasted of languages.** Shulgi of Ur (reigned about 2094–2047
BC):

> When I ...... like a torrent with the roar of a great storm, in the capture
> of a citadel in Elam ......, I can understand what their spokesman answers.
> By origin I am a son of Sumer; I am a warrior, a warrior of Sumer. Thirdly,
> I can conduct a conversation with a man from the black mountains. Fourthly,
> I can do service as a translator with an Amorite, a man of the mountains
> ....... I myself can correct his confused words in his own language.
> Fifthly, when a man of Subir yells ......, I can even distinguish the words
> in his language, although I am not a fellow-citizen of his. When I provide
> justice in the legal cases of Sumer, I give answers in all five languages.
> *(Shulgi B, from lines 206–220)*

**The friction that is recorded** comes about a thousand years after the
first cities, and it is about a way of life, not about speech. The Amorites
(*Martu*) were herders from the steppe who spoke a Semitic language of their
own. The city-dwellers' poems sneer at them:

> Now listen, their hands are destructive and their features are those of
> monkeys… He is clothed in sack-leather ......, lives in a tent, exposed to
> wind and rain, and cannot properly recite prayers. He lives in the
> mountains and ignores the places of gods, digs up truffles in the
> foothills, does not know how to bend the knee, and eats raw flesh. He has
> no house during his life, and when he dies he will not be carried to a
> burial-place. My girlfriend, why would you marry Martu? *(The Marriage of
> Martu, lines 127–141)*

The bride's answer, in the next line: *"I will marry Martu!"* Around 2034 BC
King Shu-Suen of Ur named a year for the wall he built against them, the
*Muriq-Tidnim*, "the one that keeps the Tidnum (an Amorite people) away."
Harvey Weiss links the Amorites' movement to the drought of about 2200 BC;
that link is *debated.* In the Sumerian literature that survives, the
complaints are about how the Amorites live, not how they talk; Shulgi, above,
made their language a royal accomplishment.

**The only Sumerian story about one language** is the "spell of Nudimmud" in
*Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta*, on **[Babylon's
tower](babylons-tower.html)**; scholars still argue whether it looks back to
a lost unity of speech or forward to a hoped-for one.

## 5. "One mouth": the closest parallel

The nearest thing in the ancient Near East to Babel's *"one lip"* comes from
the first millennium BC, from the empires Israel knew. Sargon II of Assyria
(722–705 BC) built a new capital, Dur-Sharrukin ("Fort Sargon," at modern
Khorsabad), and settled it with deportees. His cylinder inscription for the
city:

> Peoples of the four regions of the world, of foreign tongue and divergent
> speech, dwellers of mountain and lowland, all that were ruled by the light
> of the gods, the lord of all, I carried off at Assur, my lord's, command, by
> the might of my scepter. I unified them (made them of one mouth) and
> settled them therein. Assyrians, fully competent to teach them how to fear
> god and the king, I dispatched to them as scribes and sheriffs
> (superintendents). The gods who dwell in heaven and earth, and in that
> city, listened with favor to my word, and granted me the eternal boon of
> building that city and growing old in its midst. *(trans. D. D.
> Luckenbill, 1927, §122)*

A modern edition renders the key phrase by its sense:

> (72) People from the four (quarters of the world), (speaking) foreign
> language(s) (and of) diverse speech, those who had dwelt in (both)
> mountain(s) and (low)land(s), as many as the "Light of the Gods" (Šamaš),
> the lord of all, shepherded, whom I had carried off as booty by the power
> of my staff at the command of the god Aššur, my lord, I made act in concert
> and (re)settled (them) inside it (Dūr-Šarrukīn). *(G. Frame, RINAP 2, Sargon
> II 43, line 72)*

The Akkadian is *pâ ištēn šuškunu*, literally "to make (them) have one
mouth." Assyrian kings used it for making people act as one; Sargon uses the
same phrase of the rebel king of Hamath, who *"had caused the cities of
Arpadda, Simirra, Damascus and Samaria to revolt against me, had unified them
(lit., made them of one mouth) and prepared for battle"* (Luckenbill §55).
Whether Sargon also meant teaching the settlers a common language is
*debated.*

Sargon did not grow old in his city. He was killed in battle in 705 BC, about
a year after its dedication, and his body was never recovered. His son
Sennacherib moved the capital to Nineveh.

**How scholars use it.** Christoph Uehlinger (*Weltreich und "eine Rede"*,
1990) read Genesis 11:1–9 against this Assyrian language of empire: *one
speech* as the forced unanimity of a world empire, and the unfinished city as
an echo of Dur-Sharrukin abandoned. Arie van der Kooij (2006) called it the
most important study of the story in the light of Mesopotamian sources; he
accepted the link to Sargon's city, but held that *one speech* in Genesis
means one language as well as one will. Others read the story mainly as an
account of how the world's many cultures began — not a punishment but, in
Theodore Hiebert's words, *"God's design for the world"* (*Journal of
Biblical Literature*, 2007).

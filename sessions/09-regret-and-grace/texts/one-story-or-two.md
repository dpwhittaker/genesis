# One story or two? Reading the Flood's doublets

A source sheet for **Session 9**, Part 4. The Flood narrative (Genesis
6:5–9:17) tells many things twice — two reasons, two counts of animals, two
entries into the ark, two calendars, two promises, two names for God. The
handout lays out three ways of reading the doublets. This sheet gives the
sources for each, in their own words where we may quote them:

- **Part A — Two sources woven together** (the Documentary Hypothesis: "J"
  and "P").
- **Part B — One story built as a mirror** (the chiasm, and other arguments for
  unity).
- **Part C — The oldest answer: one Author** (the rabbis and Rashi on why each
  thing is said twice, Rabbi Mordechai Breuer's "aspects", and the evangelical
  case for Moses).

The whole passage, coloured by strand, is on [the Flood, told
twice](the-flood-told-twice.html). The handout's rule holds here too: each
view is given at its strongest, with the question it has to answer. You may
hold any of them.

## Part A — Two sources woven together

### Where the field stands now

Driver and Skinner, quoted below, set out the case a century ago, and their
verse-by-verse division is still the starting point. The field has moved since,
but not away from this. Nearly every critical scholar still divides the Flood
into a **Priestly** strand and a **non-Priestly** one. What is debated is
whether the non-Priestly strand was ever a separate, continuous source — the
"J" of the classic Documentary Hypothesis, defended today by neo-documentarians
such as Joel Baden and Baruch Schwartz — or a set of additions and revisions,
as many European scholars (Erhard Blum, Reinhard Kratz, Konrad Schmid) hold;
and how each is dated. Most date P to the exile or after; Jewish scholars such
as Yehezkel Kaufmann and Jacob Milgrom argued for an earlier P, partly on the
evidence of its Hebrew. (David Carr's *Reading the Fractures of Genesis*, 1996,
is a readable account of the debate.)

What the two strands let you hear, on this reading: a God who grieves *to his
heart* before the flood and speaks *to his heart* after it (non-P, 6:6 and
8:21); and a God who makes a covenant with *"every living creature"* and *"the
earth,"* grounds a law on his own image (9:6), and sets a sign in the clouds
that he himself will watch *"and remember"* (P, 9:12–16) — a promise that needed
no temple, written, on the usual dating, for people who had lost theirs.

**Its test, and its weakness.** The two-source reading makes a claim that could
have failed: that each strand, read alone, is a story. The Priestly strand
passes; the non-Priestly one nearly does (it lacks the building of the ark and
the exit). Its weakness is the other side of its flexibility: verses that do
not sort (7:3 and 7:9 use the "wrong" words) are credited to the editor, and a
theory that can absorb every exception is harder to test.

### S. R. Driver, *Introduction to the Literature of the Old Testament* (1913)

Driver (Oxford) wrote the standard English statement of the theory.

> …phenomena disclose themselves which show incontrovertibly that it is
> composed of distinct documents or sources, which have been welded together
> by a later compiler or redactor into a continuous whole. — p. 8

One of the two chief signs, he says, is that *"the same event is doubly
recorded."* His Flood examples: 6:9–13 repeats 6:5–8; 7:1–5 repeats 6:18–22,
*"with the difference that of every clean beast seven are to be taken into the
ark,"* while in 6:19 *"two of every sort, without distinction, are
prescribed."*

On the divine names — which many people think are the whole case — Driver was
emphatic that they are not:

> …it is an entire mistake to suppose that it is the sole or fundamental
> criterion, or even that it is the principal criterion, of diversity of
> authorship… **Elohim is but one out of more than fifty phraseological
> criteria alone.** — 1913 addenda

And he answered in advance the reading that would later be Cassuto's:

> It is true that Elohim and Jahweh represent the Divine Nature under different
> aspects, viz. as the God of nature and the God of revelation respectively;
> but it is only in a comparatively small number of instances that this
> distinction can be applied without great artificiality… — p. 13 n.

On the Flood in particular: *"the main narrative is that of P, which has been
enlarged by the addition of elements derived from J: here, however, these
elements form a tolerably complete narrative"* (p. 14 n.).

### John Skinner, *Genesis* (International Critical Commentary, 1910)

Skinner's commentary is the fullest English case for two sources in the Flood.

> [The Flood] is, as has often been observed, the first example in Gen. of a
> truly composite narrative… The resolution of the compound narrative into its
> constituent elements in this case is justly reckoned amongst the most
> brilliant achievements of purely literary criticism.

He rests it on **four lines of evidence**:

**1. Language.** *"The key to the whole process is, of course, the distinction
between the divine names"* — and then pairs of words that sort the same way:

| The LORD strand (J) | The God strand (P) |
|---|---|
| *a man and his mate* (7:2) | *male and female* (6:19; 7:9, 16) |
| *wipe out*, *māḥâ* (6:7; 7:4, 23) | *destroy*, *šiḥēt* (6:13, 17; 9:11, 15) |
| *die*, *mût* (7:22) | *expire*, *gāwaʿ* (6:17; 7:21) |
| *every living thing*, *kol-hayəqûm* (7:4, 23) | *all flesh*, *kol-bāśār* |
| *dry*, *ḥārēb* (8:13b) | *dry*, *yābēš* (8:14) |
| *all your household* (7:1) | Noah's family named in full |
{: .ff-wrap}

**2. Representation.** *"In J clean and unclean animals are distinguished, the
former entering the ark by sevens and the latter in pairs… According to J, the
cause of the Flood is a forty-days' rain"*; P has a *"chronological scheme
according to which the waters increase for 150 days, and the entire duration
of the Flood is one year."*

**3. Duplicates.** The occasion (6:5–8 ∥ 6:11–13); the command to enter (7:1–5
∥ 6:17–22); the entry (7:7 ∥ 7:13); the coming of the waters (7:10 ∥ 7:11);
the rising (7:17b ∥ 7:18); the destruction (7:22–23 ∥ 7:21); the abating (8:2b–3a
∥ 8:1–2a); the drying (8:13b ∥ 8:13a, 14); the promise (8:20–22 ∥ 9:8–17).

**4. Continuity.** *"The final confirmation of the theory is that the two series
of passages form two all but continuous narratives."*

Why would an editor keep both? *"His object evidently was to produce a
synthetic history of the Flood without sacrificing a scrap of information that
could with any plausibility be utilised for his narrative."*

And why does P have no clean animals and no sacrifice? Because P is the source
*"who traces the distinction to the positive enactments of the Sinaitic
legislation (Lv. 11, Dt. 14), and consequently ignores it here."* — *"Very
significant are the omission of Noah's sacrifice, and the ignoring of the laws
of cleanness and uncleanness amongst animals."* (Skinner was also capable of
condescension: he called P's covenant *"a striking instance of the development
of religious ideas in the direction of legalism."* Not every critic shared the
attitude, and the theory does not depend on it.)

### Julius Wellhausen, *Prolegomena to the History of Israel* (1878; Eng. 1885)

Wellhausen, whose book made the theory the scholarly consensus, conceded the
point that its critics still press:

> But we have to remember that in Genesis vi.-ix. the Jehovist account is
> mutilated, but the priestly one preserved entire.

He also thought the LORD strand the closer of the two to the Babylonian story
(*"the Jehovist has a much greater affinity with the Babylonian story than the
Priestly Code"*).

### Richard Elliott Friedman (1987, 2003) — described

Friedman's *Who Wrote the Bible?* (1987) made the Flood the showcase for a
popular audience: he prints the two strands in different type and argues they
are *"separable and complete,"* each with its own vocabulary, details and
picture of God. His *The Bible with Sources Revealed* (2003), pp. 42–47, draws
some lines differently from Driver and Skinner — he gives the raven (8:7) to P
and the dove to J (see [the passage sheet](the-flood-told-twice.html)).

### Ancient editors who did exactly this

**Gilgamesh and Atrahasis.** Jeffrey Tigay (*The Evolution of the Gilgamesh
Epic*, 1982; *Empirical Models for Biblical Criticism*, 1985) showed from the
tablets themselves that the later Gilgamesh epic absorbed older works — Tablet
XI's flood story is taken over from *Atrahasis*. One seam is still visible:
the survivor, elsewhere called Utnapishtim, is called by the older hero's name.

> I caused Atra-ḫasis to see a dream, and he heard the decision of the gods. —
> *Gilgamesh* XI 196 (Clay, 1922; XI 197 in modern editions)

R. Campbell Thompson (1928) translated the name as a title — *"to th' Abounding
in Wisdom"* — and added in a footnote: *"Atra-hasis, another name for
Uta-Napishtim."* (The current critical text has the name once more, at XI 49,
in one manuscript.)

**The Diatessaron.** Around 170, Tatian wove the four Gospels into one.
Eusebius (c. 325): *"Tatian, formed a certain combination and collection of the
Gospels, I know not how, to which he gave the title Diatessaron, and which is
still in the hands of some."* It was read in the Syriac churches until
*"Rabbula, Bishop of Edessa (411-435), ordered the priests and deacons to see
that every church should have a copy of the separate Gospels,"* and
*"Theodoret, Bishop of Cyrus (423-457), removed more than two hundred copies"*
from the churches of his diocese (*Catholic Encyclopedia*, 1913).

**The question this view has to answer.** No manuscript of J or P alone has
ever been found; J is incomplete (no building of the ark, no exit); and the
argument from vocabulary must explain the exceptions (7:3 uses *male and
female* in a J verse; 7:9 says *God* in a J passage).

## Part B — One story built as a mirror

### Gordon J. Wenham, "The Coherence of the Flood Narrative" (1978)

Wenham's article (*Vetus Testamentum* 28: 336–348) argued that *"Gen. vi 10 to
ix 19 appears to be a palistrophe"* — a mirror-shaped story of 31 items:

| | Going down | | Coming up |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Noah (6:10a) | A′ | Noah (9:19) |
| B | Shem, Ham and Japheth (6:10b) | B′ | Shem, Ham and Japheth (9:18b) |
| C | Ark to be built (6:14–16) | C′ | Ark (9:18a) |
| D | Flood announced (6:17) | D′ | No flood in future (9:11–17) |
| E | Covenant with Noah (6:18–20) | E′ | Covenant with all flesh (9:8–10) |
| F | Food in the ark (6:21) | F′ | Food outside the ark (9:1–4) |
| G | Command to enter the ark (7:1–3) | G′ | Command to leave the ark (8:15–17) |
| H | 7 days waiting for the flood (7:4–5) | H′ | 7 days waiting for the waters to go (8:12–13) |
| I | 7 days waiting for the flood (7:7–10) | I′ | 7 days waiting for the waters to go (8:10–11) |
| J | Entry to the ark (7:11–15) | J′ | Raven and dove leave the ark (8:7–9) |
| K | The LORD shuts Noah in (7:16) | K′ | Noah opens the window (8:6b) |
| L | 40 days of flood (7:17a) | L′ | 40 days — the end (8:6a) |
| M | Waters increase (7:17b–18) | M′ | Waters go down (8:5) |
| N | Mountains covered (7:19–20) | N′ | Mountain tops seen (8:4–5) |
| O | 150 days the waters prevail (7:21–24) | O′ | 150 days the waters go down (8:3) |
| | | **P** | **GOD REMEMBERS NOAH (8:1)** |
{: .ff-wrap}

The time periods, he wrote, form *"a symmetrical pattern, 7, 7, 40, 150, 150,
40, 7, 7. The turning point of the narrative is found in viii: 1 'God
remembered Noah'."* (Only periods counted in *days* belong to the pattern; the calendar
dates do not.) He was candid about the strain: one week of waiting is
mentioned twice (7:4, 10), and Noah's sacrifice (8:20–22) stands outside the
mirror. Two more strains he does not list: the two 150s are one span too (the
waters *"prevailed… 150 days,"* 7:24, and *"had gone down by the end of the
150 days,"* 8:3 — both count from 7:11 to the landing in 8:4); and the exit
from the ark (8:18–19) has no partner. (A "Wenham chiasm" circulating online, with items such as *"Curse on
earth (6:13b)"*, is not his; the table above is from the article.)

**The Mesopotamian order.** Wenham listed seventeen features that Genesis shares
with the Mesopotamian flood stories — *"At least seventeen features appear in
both stories, usually in the same order"*: the decision to destroy; the warning;
the command to build; obedience; the command to enter; the entry; the closing
of the door; the flood; the destruction; the end of the rain; the grounding on
a mountain; the window; the birds; the exit; the sacrifice; the god smelling
the sacrifice; the blessing. **J alone has twelve of them; P alone has ten.**
Only the combined story has the whole ancient shape. (The source critics'
reply: put two partial retellings of one tradition together and you will
always get more of it than either has alone — so the count fits both views.) Gary Rendsburg made the
same argument in more detail (in *Gilgameš and the World of Assyria*, 2007,
pp. 115–127): Genesis 6–8 parallels Gilgamesh XI *"point by point, and in the
same order."*

**What Wenham did *not* claim.** He did not argue for Moses. He granted that his
evidence was not *"absolutely"* incompatible with two sources and *"a most
ingenious and thorough redactor,"* and himself preferred *"one epic source"*
later reworked by a priestly editor.

### Bernhard W. Anderson (1978) — the final form as a chiasm

Anderson, who accepted J and P, read the *finished* text as a designed whole
(*Journal of Biblical Literature* 97: 23–39, outline on p. 38):

- Transition (6:9–10)
  - A. Violence in creation (6:11–12)
    - B. First divine speech: resolve to destroy (6:13–22)
      - C. Second divine speech: "enter the ark" (7:1–10)
        - D. Beginning of the flood (7:11–16)
          - E. The rising flood (7:17–24)
            - **God remembers Noah (8:1)**
          - E′. The receding flood (8:1–5)
        - D′. Drying of the earth (8:6–14)
      - C′. Third divine speech: "leave the ark" (8:15–19)
    - B′. God's resolve to preserve order (8:20–22)
  - A′. Fourth divine speech: covenant (9:1–17)
- Transition (9:18–19)

His units are larger, built on God's speeches; unlike Wenham, he includes the
sacrifice. And he noted the difficulty of finding a chiastic pattern in either
source on its own.

### Umberto Cassuto (1941) — the names follow the meaning

Cassuto (1883–1951), an Italian rabbi who became professor of Bible at the
Hebrew University, answered the Documentary Hypothesis in a set of lectures
(*Torath HaTeudoth*, 1941; English, *The Documentary Hypothesis*, 1961). His
rule for the names: *YHWH* *"when the text reflects the Israelite conception of
God"* — personal, ethical — and *Elohim* for *"God conceived as the Creator of
the physical universe, as the Ruler of nature, as the Source of life."* In the
Flood, the LORD appears where the moral motive is stressed (6:5–8; 7:1), *"when
reference is made to the sacrifices, or to the clean animals"* (7:5; 8:20–21),
and in *"the relationship of a father full of compassion towards his son, who
is dear to his heart (vii 16: and YHWH shut him in)."*

### The responses

**J. A. Emerton** (Cambridge), "An Examination of Some Attempts to Defend the
Unity of the Flood Narrative in Genesis," *VT* 37 (1987) and 38 (1988),
examined Cassuto, E. Nielsen, F. I. Andersen, Wenham and Y. T. Radday in turn.
He held that *"the two most important of the alleged discrepancies within the
narrative are the differences in chronology and in the number of creatures
taken into the ark,"* and that *"the hypothesis of a redactor is reasonable"*;
of the first two defences (Cassuto's and Nielsen's) he wrote that he did *"not
find either of the defences to be convincing."* Wenham
replied in "Method in Pentateuchal Source Criticism," *VT* 41 (1991).

**Joshua Berman**, *Inconsistency in the Torah* (Oxford, 2017), ch. 13, argues
that ancient Near Eastern writers repeated, set rival accounts side by side and
revised freely, so that the critics' tests are anachronistic; he offers a
revised chiasm answering Emerton. A reviewer summarises one of his points:
*"when Genesis 6-9 is segregated into P and non-P, the compelling comparisons
with Near Eastern cognate antecedents disappear."*

**And from the other side:** S. R. Scott (*Chiasmus: The State of the Art*,
2020) argues that J and P were each built chiastically and interleaved by an
editor: *"The two-source theory remains the best explanation for the doubling."*

### Kenneth Kitchen (1966) — ancient style

Kitchen, an Egyptologist, argued (*Ancient Orient and Old Testament*, pp.
116–121) that many "doublets" are a summary followed by the details, as in
Egyptian and Urartian royal inscriptions; that *"two"* in 6:19–20 is a general
statement and *"seven"* in 7:2–3 a specific one; and that the dates give one
consistent total of *"a year and ten days."* His motto: *"Exegesis, not
surgery, is the answer."*

**The question this view has to answer.** A mirror can be found wherever one
looks for it, and an editor could have built it out of two sources; the
divine-name rule does not fit every verse (Driver's objection above); and two
pairs or seven, forty days or a year, still have to be explained.

## Part C — The oldest answer: one Author, nothing said twice for nothing

### *Genesis Rabbah* 33:3 — the two names as two attributes

The midrash comments on 8:1, *"God remembered Noah."*

> Woe unto the wicked, as they transform [God's] attribute of mercy into the
> attribute of strict justice. Everywhere that "the Lord" is stated, it refers
> to the attribute of mercy… Fortunate are the righteous, as they transform
> the attribute of strict justice into the attribute of mercy. Everywhere that
> God [Elohim] is stated, it refers to the attribute of justice.

So in 6:5–7 it is the LORD — Mercy — who decides on the flood (the wicked had
turned mercy into judgment); in 8:1 it is God — Justice — who remembers Noah
(the righteous had turned judgment into mercy). The same paragraph adds that
Noah was remembered also *"due to the merit of the kosher animals that he took
with him."*

### More from *Genesis Rabbah* on the doublets

> It means, rather, seven males and seven females. — 32:4, on 7:3 (and it
> notices that 7:2 says not *"unclean"* but *"the animals that are not pure"*:
> the rabbis' lesson is that Scripture spends extra words to avoid an
ugly one)

> They violated the Torah that was given in forty days; therefore, [the Flood
> lasted] "forty days and forty nights." — 32:5 (Rabbi Shimon bar Yoḥai)

> Noah was lacking in faith; if the water had not reached up to his ankles, he
> would not have entered the ark. — 32:6

> …it was water at first, but after it continued to fall, it became a flood. —
> 31:12

### The tradition's answer in brief

For Jewish tradition the Torah has one Author, and a thing said twice is said
for a reason. *Two of every kind* is the minimum; the seven pairs of clean
animals are for Noah's offering (Rashi on 6:19 and 7:2, below). The seven days
before the rain were days of mourning: *"Rav says: These were the days of
mourning for the death of Methuselah"* (*Sanhedrin* 108b) — or, in *Genesis
Rabbah* 27:4, God's own seven days of mourning for his world. The rain fell
gently at first, so that it might still become a rain of blessing (Rashi on
7:12). The decree was sealed for one sin in particular — *"Their fate was sealed
only on account of their sin of robbery"* (Rashi on 6:13, the *ḥāmās* of the
second telling). Noah built for a long time so that people would ask why:
*"so that the men of the generation of the Flood might see him employed on it
for 120 years and might ask him, 'What do you need this for'?… perhaps they
might repent"* (Rashi on 6:14). And *never again* is said twice, in 9:11 and
9:15, because a word said twice is an oath: *"a negative expression is an oath
only in a case where one said no, no, stating the term two times… as it is
written: 'All flesh shall not be excised any more by floodwaters' (Genesis
9:11), and it is again written: 'And the waters shall no more become a flood'
(Genesis 9:15)"* (*Shevuot* 36a, from Rava). Isaiah 54:9 calls it a vow.

The rabbis also argued whether *"righteous in his generations"* (6:9) is
praise or blame — Rashi gives both: *"he was righteous even in his
generation… Others, however, explain it to his discredit: in comparison with
his own generation he was accounted righteous, but had he lived in the
generation of Abraham he would have been accounted as of no importance."* And
the Zohar (on the portion *Noaḥ*) faults Noah for not praying for his world — the reason, it says,
Isaiah calls the flood *"the waters of Noah"* — where Rashi on 6:14, above,
has him warning them for a hundred and twenty years. *These and these are the
words of the living God.*

### Rashi (1040–1105) on each doublet

> **6:19, "two of every kind":** Even of the least numerous amongst them there
> were not less than two—one male, the other female.

> **7:2, "the clean animals":** It means those cattle which will in future be
> permitted to Israel as clean; **we thus learn that Noah studied the Torah.**
> — and seven pairs, *"so many, in order that he might offer some of them as a
> sacrifice when leaving the Ark."*

> **7:7, "because of the floodwaters":** Noah, also, was of those people who are
> wanting in faith: he believed and he did not believe that the Flood would
> come, and he would not enter the Ark until the waters forced him to do so.

> **7:9, "two by two":** In this number they were all equal — the least number
> was two.

> **7:12, "the rain":** when He poured down the water at first He made it fall
> in mercy (gently), in order that if the people would repent, it might prove a
> rain of blessing; but when they did not repent it became a destructive
> flood.

> **7:16, "the LORD shut him in":** He protected him so that they could not
> smash up the Ark… But the literal meaning of the text is: He shut the door in
> front of him against the waters.

> **8:1, "God remembered":** This Divine Name really signifies the God of Strict
> Justice but it is transformed into Divine Mercy through the prayers of the
> righteous, whilst the evil practised by wicked people transforms Divine Mercy
> into Strict Justice.

> **8:21, "never again… never again":** He repeated the expression that it might
> serve as a solemn oath.

(Rashi does not comment on the two names in 7:16; that reading is Cassuto's.)

### The Talmud: clean animals before Sinai?

The rabbis felt the problem the critics would later press:

> The Gemara asks: And were there pure and impure species at that time, during
> the period of Noah? — *Zevaḥim* 116a

One answer: *"Noah caused all of the animals to pass before the ark. All
animals that the ark accepted, i.e., drew in, were known to be pure."* And a
folk-tale in *Sanhedrin* 108b gives the raven a complaint about the arithmetic:
*"Your Master hates me, as He commanded to take from the kosher species seven
and from the non-kosher species two."*

### Rabbi Mordechai Breuer (1921–2007) — the "theory of aspects"

Breuer, an Orthodox Bible teacher in Jerusalem, made the most daring move in
the traditional camp — and a contested one; many in Orthodoxy rejected it. He **accepted the critics' division of the text** — the
strands are real, and the man of faith, as Rabbi Amnon Bazak summarises him,
*"believes that God Himself wrote J, E, D, and P"* and did the weaving too. In
his own words, God *"redacted one document characterized by justice and one
characterized by mercy"* ("The Study of Bible and the Primacy of the Fear of
Heaven," in S. Carmy, ed., *Modern Scholarship in the Study of Torah*, 1996,
pp. 170–171). He called it *shitat ha-beḥinot*, the theory of **aspects**: God, he wrote,
*"who is beyond the limitations of time and space, prepared the Torah, declaring
in one utterance what man can comprehend only as a combination of differing
sources."*

On the Flood (*Pirkei Bereshit*, 1998/99, chapter 8), Breuer printed each
aspect separately, explained what each says, and then read the combination.
The two Flood stories continue the two creation stories: the *God* aspect
(justice) continues Genesis 1, the *LORD* aspect (mercy) Genesis 2–3. Both
begin *"he saw"* (6:5, 6:12) — and, as one summary puts it, *"in this case
neither aspect could prevent the flood."* The question between them is
whether humanity stands apart from the animals (6:5, the human heart) or shares
the fate of *"all flesh"* with them (6:12).

### Moses and older records — the evangelical traditional view

Evangelical defenders of Mosaic authorship mostly take one of two routes. Some
argue, with **Kitchen** (Part B), that the doublets are ancient narrative
style. Others hold that Moses **used older written records**: P. J. Wiseman's
"tablet theory" (1936) read each *"This is the account of…"* as the closing
label of an older tablet — which would put 6:5–8 and 6:11–13 on two different
tablets — and Duane Garrett (*Rethinking Genesis*, 1991) describes sources
passing through *"the Mosaic redaction; and the post-Mosaic redaction(s)."*

**The question these readings have to answer.** The names do not always fit the
attributes (it is Mercy who decides on the flood — which the midrash itself
must explain); Noah keeps distinctions the Torah says were given later (the
Talmud asks it); and Breuer's own answer grants the strands that the
documentary critics found.

---

## What they share

- **The doublets are real.** No one — rabbi, critic or evangelical — thinks
  the repetitions are imaginary. They disagree about what they are.
- **The final story is meant to be read whole.** The critics' editor worked
  *"without sacrificing a scrap"*; Wenham and Anderson find a design; Breuer's
  Author braided the aspects on purpose.

# Can God regret? Readers of Genesis 6:6, from Philo to now

A source sheet for **Session 9**. *"The LORD regretted that he had made
humankind on the earth, and it pained him to his heart"* (Genesis 6:6,
literally) is one of the most argued-over sentences in the Bible. This sheet
gathers what the main readers actually said, in their own words wherever we
can quote them, grouped by the five readings on the [handout](../) (Part 2).

The groups are not teams. Several of these writers hold two readings at once —
Calvin is the clearest case — and the readings overlap at the edges. What
every one of them shares is worth saying first: **no one here thinks God is
fickle, and no one thinks human sin leaves him indifferent.** They disagree
about how to say both.

## 1. Translate it gently

### The Greek Bible (the Septuagint), c. 250 BCE

> …then God laid it to heart that he had made man upon the earth, and he
> pondered it deeply. — *Genesis 6:6*, Greek, in Brenton's translation (1851)

The Greek is *enethymēthē ho theos… kai dienoēthē* — "God took it to heart…
and thought it through." There is no *regret* and no *pain*. In verse 7, the
standard editions of the Greek read *hoti ethymōthēn hoti epoiēsa autous*,
**"for I was angry that I made them"** — and that is the Bible Philo, the
apostles and the early church fathers read. (Brenton's own Greek text has a
variant, "I considered," and his English says "I am grieved"; his footnote
records the other reading: *"Alex. … I became angry."*)

The Greek translators of the Torah never use the ordinary word for *repent*
(*metanoeō*) at all — of God or anyone. The translators of the prophets, a
little later, had no such scruple: in Jeremiah 18, Joel, Amos and Jonah, God
*metanoei*. (Details on the [Scripture sheet](elsewhere-in-scripture.html),
§10.)

### Targum Onkelos and Targum Pseudo-Jonathan

The Targums are Aramaic translations read aloud in the synagogue, line by
line after the Hebrew.

> And it repented the Lord in His Word that He had made men upon the earth.
> **And He said, (in His Word,) that He would break their strength according to
> His pleasure.** — *Targum Onkelos*, Genesis 6:6

> And it repented the Lord in His Word that He had made man upon the earth;
> **and He passed judgment upon them by His Word.** — *Targum
> Pseudo-Jonathan*, Genesis 6:6

Both keep *repented* but move it into God's *Word* (*Memra*) — a reverent
buffer the Targums put between God and anything too human. And both replace
*it pained him to his heart* with a **decision**.

## 2. God speaks our language

### Philo of Alexandria, *That God Is Unchangeable* (*Quod Deus sit immutabilis*)

Philo (c. 20 BCE – c. 50 CE), a Jewish philosopher in Alexandria and an older
contemporary of Jesus and Paul, wrote this whole treatise on Genesis 6:4–12. He
read the Greek Bible.

> Perhaps some very wicked persons will suspect that the lawgiver is here
> speaking enigmatically, when he says that the Creator repented of having
> created man, when he beheld their wickedness; on which account he determined
> to destroy the whole race. But let those who adopt such opinions as these
> know, that they are making light of and extenuating the offences of these
> men of old time, by reason of their own excessive impiety; **for what can be
> a greater act of wickedness than to think that the unchangeable God can be
> changed?** — §§21–22

> for it is impossible for us, who are but men, to foresee all the
> contingencies of future events, or to anticipate the opinions of others;
> but to God, as dwelling in pure light, all things are visible — §29

> Now, some persons, when they hear the expressions which I have just cited,
> imagine that the living God is here giving away to anger and passion; **but
> God is utterly inaccessible to any passion whatever.** But, nevertheless,
> such things are spoken of with reference to God by the great lawgiver in an
> introductory sort of way, for the sake of admonishing those persons who
> could not be corrected otherwise. — §52 (*giving away* is Yonge's, for
> *giving way*)

Then his key: Scripture speaks of God in two registers.

> …there are two principal positions laid down with respect to the great
> cause of all things: **one, that God is not as a man; the other, that God is
> as a man.** But the first of these assertions is confirmed by the most
> certain truth, while the latter is introduced for the instruction of the
> many… And this is said for the sake of instruction and admonition, and not
> because he is really such by nature. — §§53–54

*Not as a man* is Numbers 23:19. *As a man* is the Greek of Deuteronomy 8:5,
*"as a man would instruct his son"* (Yonge's footnote says 1:31, but Philo's
Greek is 8:5). Two registers, Philo says, for two kinds of reader: those who
can love God as he is, and those who must first learn to fear him (§69). He
goes further than most later readers would follow — he compares Moses to a
physician who tells frightened patients comforting untruths (§§65–67).

On verse 7, Philo reads the Greek *"I was angry"*:

> Perhaps Moses here means to show, that bad men are made so by the anger of
> God, but good men by his grace; for immediately afterwards he proceeds to
> add, but "**Noah found grace in the eyes of the Lord.**" — §70

> …when he says, "I was indignant that I had made them," not reversing the
> order of the words so as to say, "Because I had made them I was indignant;"
> for the latter expression would have become a person who repented of what he
> had done, an idea which is inconsistent with the nature of God, which
> foresees everything. — §72

(Yonge's English at §51, quoting the verse, says *"because I have considered
and repent"*; Philo's Greek there is *ethymōthēn*, "I was angry." At §§70–72
Yonge translates it correctly.)

### Origen, *Against Celsus* (c. 248)

> We speak, indeed, of the wrath of God. We do not, however, assert that it
> indicates any passion on His part, but that it is something which is assumed
> in order to discipline by stern means those sinners who have committed many
> and grievous sins. — *Contra Celsum* 4.72

And elsewhere, of *"those expressions which occur in the Old Testament, as when
God is said to be angry or to repent,"* he speaks for *"us, who maintain that
God is altogether impassible"* (*De Principiis* 2.4.4).

### Augustine, *City of God* 15.25 (c. 418–420)

Augustine's Latin Bible, like the Greek, read *"the Lord thought and
reconsidered"* in 6:6 and *"I am angry that I have made them"* in 6:7.

> **The anger of God is not a disturbing emotion of His mind, but a judgment by
> which punishment is inflicted upon sin.** His thought and reconsideration
> also are the unchangeable reason which changes things; for He does not, like
> man, repent of anything He has done, because in all matters His decision is
> as inflexible as His prescience is certain. But if Scripture were not to use
> such expressions as the above, it would not familiarly insinuate itself into
> the minds of all classes of men, whom it seeks access to for their good,
> that it may alarm the proud, arouse the careless, exercise the inquisitive,
> and satisfy the intelligent; and this it could not do, **did it not first
> stoop, and in a manner descend, to them where they lie.**

### Maimonides, *Guide for the Perplexed* (c. 1190)

On the Hebrew of *it pained him to his heart*, Maimonides offers two meanings:

> In Genesis vi. 6 the word has either the second or the third signification.
> In the first case, the sense of the Hebrew va-yit'aẓẓeb el libbo is "God was
> angry with them on account of the wickedness of their deeds"… Taking the
> verb in the third signification, we explain the passage thus: "**And man
> rebelled against God's will concerning him**"; for leb (heart) also signifies
> "will," — I.29

> And a figure of this kind is admissible, since "the Torah speaketh in
> accordance with the language of man" — I.29

> Whenever any one of His actions is perceived by us, we ascribe to God that
> emotion which is the source of the act when performed by ourselves, and call
> Him by an epithet which is formed from the verb expressing that emotion. —
> I.54

### Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I, q. 19, a. 7 (c. 1266–68)

Aquinas states the objection from Genesis himself:

> It seems that the Will of God is changeable. For the Lord says (Genesis
> 6:7): "It repenteth Me that I have made man." But whoever repents of what he
> has done, has a changeable will. — *Objection 1*

> I answer that, The will of God is entirely unchangeable. On this point we
> must consider that **to change the will is one thing; to will that certain
> things should be changed is another.** — *Answer*

> These words of the Lord are to be understood metaphorically, and according
> to the likeness of our nature. For when we repent, we destroy what we have
> made; although we may even do so without change of will; as, when a man
> wills to make a thing, at the same time intending to destroy it later.
> Therefore God is said to have repented, by way of comparison with our mode
> of acting, in so far as by the deluge He destroyed from the face of the
> earth man whom He had made. — *Reply 1*

And quoting Gregory the Great (*Moralia*, c. 590): *"The sentence of God
changes, but not His counsel"* (*Reply 2*).

### Martin Luther, *Lectures on Genesis* (c. 1536)

Luther asks the question bluntly and gives the standard answer its due — then
takes his own road.

> If God foresees everything, why does the text say that he now first sees? If
> God is wise, how can regret for having created anything befall him? — §154

> While I do not condemn this interpretation, a simpler meaning of the
> Scripture seems to be that the Holy Scriptures express the thought of men in
> the ministry. For when Moses says that God sees and regrets, **this is really
> done in the hearts of those who have the ministry of the Word.** — §155

> Noah, who had the Holy Spirit and was a minister of the Word, saw the
> wickedness of men and, seeing such things, he was moved by the Holy Spirit
> to grief. — §156

> We must not think that God has a heart or that he can suffer pain, but when
> the spirit of Noah, Lamech or Methuselah is grieved, God himself is said to
> be grieved. — §178

For Luther the grief is real — it is the grief of the Holy Spirit in the
hearts of God's people (compare Ephesians 4:30). And: *"It is better and safer
to stand at the manger of Christ, the man"* (§158).

### John Calvin, *Commentary on Genesis* (1554)

> The repentance which is here ascribed to God does not properly belong to
> him, but has reference to our understanding of him. For since we cannot
> comprehend him as he is, it is necessary that, for our sake, he should, in a
> certain sense, transform himself. That repentance cannot take place in God,
> easily appears from this single consideration, that nothing happens which is
> by him unexpected or unforeseen.

> **Certainly God is not sorrowful or sad; but remains for ever like himself
> in his celestial and happy repose: yet, because it could not otherwise be
> known how great is God's hatred and detestation of sin, therefore the Spirit
> accommodates himself to our capacity.**

What the figure is meant to teach, he says, is *"that God was so offended by
the atrocious wickedness of men, as if they had wounded his heart with mortal
grief"* — and he draws the pastoral conclusion:

> Moreover, this paternal goodness and tenderness ought, in no slight degree,
> to subdue in us the love of sin; since God, in order more effectually to
> pierce our hearts, clothes himself with our affections.

### Calvin, *Institutes* 1.17.12–13 (1559)

> As to repentance, we must hold that it can no more exist in God than
> ignorance, or error, or impotence. — 1.17.12

> Because our weakness cannot reach his height, any description which we
> receive of him must be lowered to our capacity in order to be intelligible.
> And the mode of lowering is to represent him not as he really is, but as we
> conceive of him… so we ought not to imagine any thing more under the term
> repentance than a change of action, men being wont to testify their
> dissatisfaction by such a change. — 1.17.13

### The confessions

> There is but one living and true God, everlasting, **without body, parts, or
> passions**; of infinite power, wisdom, and goodness; the Maker, and
> Preserver of all things both visible and invisible. — *Thirty-Nine Articles*,
> Article 1 (1563/1571; spelling modernised; the same words are in the Irish
> Articles of 1615)

> There is but one only living and true God, who is infinite in being and
> perfection, a most pure spirit, invisible, **without body, parts, or
> passions, immutable**, immense, eternal, incomprehensible, almighty, most
> wise, most holy, most free, most absolute, working all things according to
> the counsel of his own immutable and most righteous will, for his own glory;
> **most loving, gracious, merciful, longsuffering, abundant in goodness and
> truth, forgiving iniquity, transgression, and sin**… — *Westminster
> Confession of Faith* 2.1 (1646)

Notice that the Westminster divines put *without passions* and *most loving,
gracious, merciful* in the same sentence, and the second half is Exodus 34:6–7
almost word for word.

### Keil and Delitzsch (1861)

The great nineteenth-century conservative commentary stands between readings 2
and 3:

> This shows that the repentance of God does not presuppose any variableness
> in His nature or His purposes… **The repentance of God is an anthropomorphic
> expression for the pain of the divine love at the sin of man**… In these
> words mercy is seen in the midst of wrath, pledging the preservation and
> restoration of humanity.

## 3. A real grief

### The rabbis: *Genesis Rabbah* 27:4, as Rashi tells it

Session 8's [rabbinic sheet](../../08-sons-of-god/texts/rabbinic-sources.html)
(§7) prints the midrash in full. Rashi (1040–1105), whose commentary most Jews
still read beside the text, retells it on 6:6. A non-Jew asks Rabbi Joshua ben
Korḥa how God could grieve over what he foresaw; the rabbi asks whether the
man rejoiced when his son was born, though he knew the son would die. The man
answers:

> "At the time of joy, let there be joy, at the time of mourning let there be
> mourning". — and the rabbi: "**Such, too, is the way of the Holy One, blessed
> be He**: although it was clear to Him that in the end men would sin and would
> be destroyed, He did not refrain from creating them for the sake of the
> righteous men who were to issue from them" — *Rashi on Genesis 6:6*, citing
> *Genesis Rabbah* 27:4

Rashi gathers the other readings too, side by side, in the same comment:

> It was a consolation to Him that He had created man on earth, for had he been
> one of the heavenly beings he would have incited them also to rebel against
> God… The thoughts of God turned from Divine mercy to Divine justice: He
> considered what to do with man whom He had made on the earth… **He mourned at
> the failure of His handiwork.**

(The first reads *niḥam* as *be comforted*; the second as *change one's mind*
— literally, *"the thought of the Place [God] turned from the attribute of mercy
to the attribute of justice"*; the third keeps the grief.)

### Tertullian (c. 207) and Lactantius (c. 314)

Not every church father treated God's emotions as figures. Tertullian, against
those who argued *"If God is angry, and jealous, and roused, and grieved, He
must therefore be corrupted, and must therefore die,"* answered that *"the human
soul have the same emotions and sensations as God, although they are not of the
same kind"* (*Against Marcion* 2.16). And Lactantius wrote a whole book, *On
the Anger of God*, to argue *"that it follows that God is angry, since He is
moved by kindness"* (ch. 6): a God who could not be angry at evil could not
love the good.

### Abraham Joshua Heschel, *The Prophets* (1962) — described

Heschel (1907–1972), a Polish-born Jewish theologian who taught in New York and
marched with Martin Luther King Jr., argued that the prophets' God is
**moved**. Where Greek philosophy prized *apatheia* — freedom from feeling — as
the mark of the divine, the prophets knew a God of *pathos*: concern,
involvement, a God affected by what his people do. Heschel distinguished pathos
from *passion* (an irrational emotion that overpowers): divine pathos is free,
ethical and chosen — *"not an attribute but a situation,"* God's relation to a
people. He observed that both Jewish and Christian theologians had
traditionally treated impassibility as *"the fundamental principle"* of their
doctrine of God (p. 254), and he meant to overturn it. (The chapters are "The
Theology of Pathos," "The Philosophy of Pathos," "Anthropopathy" and "The
Meaning and Mystery of Wrath," among others.)

A Jewish critic: the Orthodox theologian **Eliezer Berkovits** replied that
Heschel's pathos fit Christian premises better than Jewish ones ("Dr. A. J.
Heschel's Theology of Pathos," *Tradition* 6:2, 1964).

### Terence Fretheim — described

Fretheim, a Lutheran Old Testament scholar, argued in *The Suffering of God*
(1984) and "The Repentance of God" (*Horizons in Biblical Theology* 10, 1988)
that the Old Testament speaks of God suffering *because of* his people, *with*
them and *for* them, and that it is *"not at all embarrassed about saying that
God is open to change."* He held that both changelessness and changeability
belong to God, *"each in its own sphere"* — God's faithfulness never changes;
his ways of acting toward people do. (That puts him close to reading 5.)

## 4. An open future

### Open theism (1994–2000) — described

In *The Openness of God* (1994), five evangelical scholars — Clark Pinnock,
Richard Rice, John Sanders, William Hasker and David Basinger — argued that God
has chosen to create a world whose future is partly open, settled only as free
creatures decide. Gregory Boyd, *God of the Possible* (2000), pp. 55–57, makes
Genesis 6:6 a lead text: the regret was genuine, he argues, and its genuineness
is shown by what God did next — starting over. If the outcome had been certain
when God made humankind, he asks, how could he truly regret it? John Sanders,
*The God Who Risks* (1998), pp. 49–50, reads the Flood the same way and the
promise of 8:21 as God deciding *"to try different courses of action in the
future."*

### The response

Most evangelical theologians rejected the view. Bruce Ware's *God's Lesser
Glory* (2000) argued that a God who regrets decisions that turned out badly is
a God whose wisdom can fail. On 16 November 2001 the Evangelical Theological
Society resolved:

> We believe the Bible clearly teaches that God has complete, accurate and
> infallible knowledge of all events past, present and future including all
> future decisions and actions of free moral agents.

— by 253 votes to 66, with 41 abstaining (minutes in *Journal of the
Evangelical Theological Society* 45, 2002). In 2003 the Society voted on
whether Pinnock and Sanders could remain members; neither expulsion reached the
two-thirds required. The Society's president said the vote had turned on
inerrancy, and that open theism itself *"has not been cleared."*

The texts most often answered to the open view are Isaiah 46:10 (*"who
announces the end from the beginning"*), 1 Samuel 15:29, and the New
Testament's *"foreknown before the foundation of the world"* (1 Peter 1:20).

## 5. Relenting is part of who he is

### Tertullian, *Against Marcion* 2.23–24 (c. 207)

Marcion, a second-century teacher in Rome, taught that the God of the Old
Testament — a judge who grew angry and changed his mind — could not be the
Father of Jesus. He used God's repenting over Saul and over Nineveh as evidence.
Tertullian's reply is the earliest Christian defence of God's relenting, and it
does not call it a figure of speech:

> [you] interpret it with similar perverseness just as if it were with
> fickleness and improvidence that He repented, or on the recollection of some
> wrong-doing — 2.24

> He does not change His judgments through inconstancy or want of foresight,
> but dispenses reward according to the deserts of each case with a most
> unwavering and provident decision. — 2.23

> For it will have no other meaning than **a simple change of a prior
> purpose**; and this is admissible without any blame even in a man, much more
> in God, whose every purpose is faultless. Now in Greek the word for
> repentance (μετάνοια) is formed, not from the confession of a sin, but from
> a change of mind, which in God we have shown to be **regulated by the
> occurrence of varying circumstances.** — 2.24

> Certainly not, my reply is; **God will never repent of an act of justice.**
> — 2.24

### Calvin's "tacit condition" (*Institutes* 1.17.14)

Calvin, who held reading 2, also supplied the key to reading 5:

> Those who think so labour under delusion as to the meaning of threatenings,
> which, though they affirm simply, nevertheless contain in them **a tacit
> condition dependent on the result.**… When Jonah prophesies that in forty
> days Nineveh will be overthrown, **he does it in order to prevent the
> overthrow.**

### Robert B. Chisholm Jr. (1995) — described

Chisholm, Old Testament professor at Dallas Theological Seminary and senior
Old Testament editor of the NET Bible, sets out this reading in "Does God
'Change His Mind'?", *Bibliotheca Sacra* 152 (1995), from p. 387. He argues
that treating every *niḥam* as a figure of speech fails to take seriously Joel
2:13 and Jonah 4:2, which name God's willingness to relent among his
**fundamental attributes**, next to grace, compassion, patience and love. His
resolution: when God issues a **decree** or swears an **oath** (Numbers 23:19;
1 Samuel 15:29; Psalm 110:4), he will not change his mind; when he
**announces** an intention — explicitly or implicitly conditional, as Jeremiah
18 says — the response he meets may move him to change course.

Other evangelicals put it in covenant terms: when God relents he has not
taken on a different character; he is *"continuing to be what He promised to
be"* (Sinclair Ferguson). Robert Picirilli (*JETS* 44, 2001): in his eternal
mind God does not repent; in his actions toward us in time, he does.

**The hard case for this reading** is Genesis 6 itself: God relents in
Jeremiah, Joel and Jonah from what he has *threatened*, but in 6:6 he regrets
what he has *done* — as in 1 Samuel 15 and Jeremiah 42:10.

## What they share

Read side by side, the five readings agree on more than they first seem to:

- **God is not fickle.** Not one of these writers thinks God was caught out,
  changed his mind on a whim, or regretted the way we regret a bad purchase.
  Even the open theists insist God is perfectly wise.
- **Sin matters to God.** Philo's *anger*, Augustine's *judgment*, Calvin's
  *mortal grief*, the rabbis' *mourning*, Heschel's *pathos* — every reading
  takes 6:6 to mean that what humans did was not indifferent to God.
- **Grace follows.** Philo, reading the very next line, said it first: *bad
  men are made so by the anger of God, but good men by his grace — "Noah found
  grace."*

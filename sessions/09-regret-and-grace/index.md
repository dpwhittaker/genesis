# 9. Regret, Grace, and a Story Told Twice

**Passage:** Genesis 6:5–8, and a first look at the Flood, 6:9–9:17 ([the whole Flood narrative, with its doublets marked](texts/the-flood-told-twice.html))

**Source sheets:** **[regret and favor across the Bible](texts/elsewhere-in-scripture.html)** · **[can God regret?](texts/god-who-regrets.html)** (Philo to the present) · **[one story or two?](texts/one-story-or-two.html)** (the sources for J and P, the chiasm, and the rabbis' reading)
{: .no-print}

## A note before we begin

We ran out of time last week four verses short of the ark. Those four verses
(Genesis 6:5–8) say three of the weightiest things in Genesis: that the
world's evil is *ours*; that God *regretted* making us; and — for the first
time in the Bible — that someone found *grace*. Then the story of the Flood
begins, and if you read it slowly you notice something strange: much of it is
told twice.

Two of today's questions have divided faithful readers for a very long time.
Can God change his mind? And was Genesis written by one hand or woven from
several? Neither is a test of faith, and Christians and Jews who love this book
answer both in more than one way. This session lays the answers out as fairly
as we can, says where the New Testament speaks, and leaves you room. If some of
it unsettles you, that is an honest response to an old and serious question —
and you are in very good company.

## Aim of this session

Four movements: **whose fault** the world's evil is (6:5); what it means that
**God regretted** (6:6–7); **grace** named for the first time (6:8); and the
Flood as **a story told twice** — three ways of reading its doublets. We end
with a look ahead to next week, and the two other flood stories the class has
already met.

## Part 1 — Whose fault is it? (6:5)

> But the LORD saw that the wickedness of **humankind** had become great on
> the earth. **Every** inclination of the thoughts of their minds was **only**
> evil **all the time**. *(Genesis 6:5)*

**Where we stopped.** Four verses about heavenly beings who crossed a line,
and the "heroes of old" born of it. Then verse 5 changes the subject. Not *the
sons of God*; not *the Nephilim*. ***Humankind*** — *hāʾādām*, the word of
Genesis 1:27 and 2:7. When the LORD looks at the world before the flood, the
wickedness he sees is ours.

**We have heard this before.** In the garden, both humans were asked what they
had done, and both pointed away: *"The woman whom you gave me…"*; *"The serpent
tricked me, and I ate"* (3:12–13). God did not dispute the facts — the serpent
*had* tricked her. He still questioned each of them and sentenced each of them,
the serpent included. Being deceived was real; it was not an excuse. Genesis 6
tells the same story at the scale of the world. The sons of God did cross the
line, and Jude and 2 Peter say they answer for it. But the verdict on the earth
falls on *humankind*.

**The words leave no room.** *Every* inclination, *only* evil, *all the day* —
the Hebrew stacks its totals: *kol… raq… kol-hayyôm*. *Inclination* is
***yēṣer***, from the potter's verb of 2:7: the creature God *formed* is now
forming things of its own. The noun appears in Genesis exactly twice. This is
the first. Hold on to it.

### Two answers in Jesus' day

By the first century, Jews had two ways of explaining where evil comes from,
and both began with Genesis. One was ***1 Enoch***'s (Session 8): angels came
down, seduced and taught, and their spirits became the demons — evil is done
*to* us, from above. The other came from Jesus ben Sira, a Jerusalem scribe,
around 180 BCE:

> Say not: 'From God is my transgression,' / For that which He hateth made He
> not. / Say not: '(It is) He that made me to stumble,' / For there is no need
> of evil men. *(Ben Sira 15:11–12)*

God, he goes on, *"gave him into the hand of his inclination"* — the *yēṣer*
of 6:5 (Session 5.2). Around 100 CE, after Rome had burned the Temple, the
author of ***4 Ezra*** traced the catastrophe to the same root: *"the first
Adam, clothing himself with the evil heart, transgressed and was overcome; and
likewise also all who were born of him"* (3:21–22).

**The New Testament keeps both and lets neither become an excuse.** It knows
the powers from above — *"our struggle is not against flesh and blood, but
against… the spiritual forces of evil in the heavens"* (Ephesians 6:12). But
when it asks why *I* sinned, it answers like Ben Sira, almost in his words:

> Let no one say when he is tempted, "I am tempted by God," for God cannot be
> tempted by evil, and he himself tempts no one. But each one is tempted when
> he is lured and enticed by his own desires. *(James 1:13–14)*

And Jesus: *"from within, out of the human heart, come evil ideas… All these
evils come from within and defile a person"* (Mark 7:21, 23).

### What the flood could not wash

Now the second *yēṣer*. When the waters are gone and Noah has offered his
sacrifice, the LORD says *to his heart*:

> "I will never again curse the ground because of humankind, **even though the
> inclination of their minds is evil from childhood on.**" *(8:21)*

It is the diagnosis of 6:5 in nearly the same words — and this time it is the
reason for **mercy**. The flood will wash the earth. It will not wash the human
heart; that walks out of the ark with Noah. Genesis is telling us early that
God's answer to 6:5 will not, in the end, be water. The prophets name the
answer: *"I will give you a new heart, and I will put a new spirit within
you"* (Ezekiel 36:26).

true. Why does Scripture still refuse to let them be the last word?

## Part 2 — "The LORD regretted" (6:6–7)

> The LORD **regretted** that he had made humankind on the earth, and he was
> highly offended. So the LORD said, "I will wipe humankind, whom I have
> created, from the face of the earth… for I **regret** that I have made
> them." *(Genesis 6:6–7)*

Last week we heard the *pain* in this verse — *it pained him to his heart*,
which the NET smooths to *highly offended*. Today, the other verb: *regretted*,
Hebrew ***niḥam*** — to be sorry, to relent, to change course. And here the
Bible seems to argue with itself.

**Scripture says both.** God *niḥam*s in more than thirty verses. He regrets
making humankind (Genesis 6) and making Saul king (1 Samuel 15:11, 35). He
*relents* over the golden calf (Exodus 32:14), over Nineveh (Jonah 3:10), and
twice at Amos's prayer (Amos 7:3, 6). And two verses say flatly that he does
not:

> God is not a man, that he should lie, / nor a human being, that he should
> change his mind. *(Numbers 23:19)*

The other is 1 Samuel 15:29 — in the very chapter that twice says he
regretted. (And Psalm 110:4: what the LORD has sworn, he *"will not revoke it."*)
The writer did not miss the tension; he put it there. Readers have held the two together in roughly five
ways. Each is held by serious believers, and each has a question it has to
answer.

**1. Translate it gently.** The Jews who put Genesis into Greek, around 250
BCE, wrote *"God laid it to heart that he had made man upon the earth, and he
pondered it deeply"* — and in verse 7, not *I regret* but *ethymōthēn*, **I was
angry**, that I made them.
Regret became thought; grief became anger. (The Greek Torah never once uses
the ordinary word for *repent*, of God or anyone.) The Aramaic Targum read in
the synagogues kept the regret but traded the grief for a decision: *"He said,
(in His Word,) that He would break their strength according to His pleasure."*
*The question it has to answer:* the Hebrew does say *regret* and *pain*.

**2. God speaks our language.** Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish philosopher
and an older contemporary of Jesus, wrote a whole book on these verses, *That God Is
Unchangeable*. To think *"that the unchangeable God can be changed,"* he says,
is the height of impiety; Scripture speaks in two registers — *"one, that God
is not as a man; the other, that God is as a man"* — the first *"confirmed by
the most certain truth,"* the second *"introduced for the instruction of the
many."* Maimonides and Nachmanides reached for an old rabbinic proverb: *the
Torah speaks in human language*. The church took the same road. Augustine: *"The anger of God is not a
disturbing emotion of His mind, but a judgment by which punishment is
inflicted upon sin"* (*City of God* 15.25). Aquinas: God does not change his
will; he *"wills that things should change."* The Reformation confessions
wrote it into the creed: God is *"without body, parts, or passions,
immutable"* (Westminster Confession 2.1). And Calvin, who held this view, said
what the figure is *for*:

> Certainly God is not sorrowful or sad; but remains for ever like himself in
> his celestial and happy repose: yet, because it could not otherwise be known
> how great is God's hatred and detestation of sin, therefore the Spirit
> accommodates himself to our capacity. *(Commentary on Genesis 6:6)*

*Its strength:* it guards God from being like us — fickle, surprised,
outmanoeuvred (*"with whom there is no variation,"* James 1:17). *The question
it has to answer:* if *pain to his heart* is a figure, what is it a figure
*of*? Pressed too hard, the verse goes cold.

**3. A real grief.** So others let the grief stand. The rabbis set it beside
God's foreknowledge without apology: a father rejoices when his son is born,
though he knows the son will one day die (Session 8, *Genesis Rabbah* 27:4).
In the twentieth century the Jewish theologian **Abraham Joshua Heschel**
(*The Prophets*, 1962) made it a principle: the God of the prophets is not the
unmoved god of Greek philosophy; he has *pathos* — he is moved by what people
do, and that is not weakness but care. Paul writes as though he agrees: *"do
not grieve the Holy Spirit of God"* (Ephesians 4:30). *The question it has to
answer:* how does a God who knows the end grieve the way we do?

**4. An open future.** In the 1990s some evangelical theologians — Clark
Pinnock, John Sanders, Gregory Boyd — read 6:6 at its most literal: God
regretted because how humans would turn out had not been settled in advance.
*Its strength:* it takes the verse at face value. *The question it has to
answer:* the God who *"announces the end from the beginning"* (Isaiah 46:10).
Most evangelicals have rejected it; in 2001 the Evangelical Theological
Society affirmed, 253 to 66, that God knows *"all events past, present and
future."*

**5. Relenting is part of who he is.** The last reading may be the most
biblical, and it is old. Jeremiah 18 states it as God's own rule: if a nation
I threatened *"stops doing wrong, I will cancel the destruction I intended"*;
if a nation I promised to bless turns to evil, *"I will cancel the good"*
(18:8, 10). Joel and Jonah go further. They quote the great description of God
from Exodus 34 — *gracious, compassionate, slow to anger* — and add one line:

> …you are a gracious and compassionate God, slow to anger and abounding in
> mercy, **and one who relents concerning threatened judgment.** *(Jonah 4:2;
> so also Joel 2:13)*

On this reading God's relenting is not a crack in his constancy; it *is* his
constancy. He is always the God who responds, and every threat carries what
Calvin called *"a tacit condition"* — Jonah preaches Nineveh's overthrow *"in
order to prevent the overthrow."* What God has *sworn* (Numbers 23:19; Psalm
110:4) never changes; what he *warns* is an invitation. (Robert Chisholm, the
NET Bible's senior Old Testament editor, argues this view; so, in the second century,
did Tertullian, against a teacher named Marcion who said a God who regrets
must be fickle — and so could not be the Father of Jesus.) *The question it has
to answer:* Jeremiah and Jonah describe God relenting from what he has
*threatened*. Genesis 6:6 regrets something he has already *done*.

**Where the New Testament stands.** Hebrews quotes Psalm 110:4 — *"The Lord
has sworn and will not change his mind"* (7:21) — and speaks of God's
*"unchangeable"* purpose, sealed with an oath (6:17–18). Paul says the Spirit
can be grieved. The New Testament keeps both edges, as 1 Samuel 15 does:
**what God has purposed and sworn does not change, and God is not unmoved by
what we do.** Whichever reading you hold has to keep both. Genesis 6 keeps
them in its own way: in the same breath as the regret, it says *but Noah*.

</div>

## Part 3 — Grace, for the first time (6:8)

> But Noah found **favor** in the sight of the LORD. *(Genesis 6:8)*

Last week: *Noah*, נח, is *favor*, חן, spelled backward, and this is the first
time the word ***ḥēn*** — grace — appears in the Bible. Three more things.

**Grace lives in the eyes of the giver.** *To find favor in someone's eyes* is
a Hebrew idiom used thirty-nine times, usually by someone lower before someone
higher. Jacob sends herds ahead to Esau *"to find favor in your sight,
my lord"* (33:8). Joseph finds favor with Potiphar (39:4). Ruth kneels before
Boaz — the NET says *"Why are you so kind…?"*, but the Hebrew asks, *"**Why
have I found favor in your eyes**, that you should notice me, a foreigner?"*
(Ruth 2:10). You can send gifts, work hard, kneel. You cannot make favor
happen. It lives in the other person's eyes, and Ruth's *why?* is the right
question.

**Only once does the narrator say it of God.** Moses, Gideon and David *ask*
for favor in the LORD's eyes, and God tells Moses he has it. But Genesis 6:8 is
the only verse in the Hebrew Bible where the storyteller simply reports it:
*Noah found favor in the eyes of the LORD* — and reports it before anything
else is said about Noah. Grace (6:8); then *righteous* (6:9); then *obedient*
(6:22). Hebrews keeps the order: Noah *"became an heir of the righteousness
that comes by faith"* (11:7).

**Gabriel says it again.** The Greek Bible put *ḥēn* into the word that
became the New Testament's word for grace, ***charis***: *"Noe found grace
before the Lord God."* When the angel comes to Mary he uses the same verb and
the same noun: *"Do not be afraid, Mary, for **you have found favor** with
God!"* (Luke 1:30).

### And then at Sinai

In the rest of the Torah, the vocabulary of these four verses comes together in
one place only — at the foot of Mount Sinai, after the golden calf.

- God threatens to destroy the whole people (Exodus 32:10). Moses begs him to
  ***relent*** — *niḥam* — *"Then the LORD relented"* (32:12, 14).
- Moses asks God to ***wipe*** him out of his book instead (32:32) — *māḥâ*,
  the verb of Genesis 6:7, *I will wipe humankind*.
- Five times in six verses the conversation turns on finding ***favor*** in
  God's sight: *"you have found favor in my sight"* (33:12–17).
- And then the LORD proclaims his name: *"The LORD, the LORD, the compassionate
  and **gracious** God…"* (34:6) — *ḥannûn*, from *ḥēn*.

*Wipe out, relent, favor.* For the people camped at Sinai, who had just
deserved their own flood, Genesis 6:5–8 was not only about Noah's world. It
was their story told in advance — and it ended in grace.

<div class="discuss" markdown="1">
**Talk about it.** Ruth asks Boaz *why* she has found favor. If someone asked
you why God has shown you favor, what would you say — and what does it do to
an answer that grace is named before righteousness?
</div>

## Part 4 — A story told twice (6:9–9:17)

Read on past verse 8 and the story starts again. A new heading — *"This is the
account of Noah"* (6:9) — and then the reason for the flood, a second time:

> The earth was ruined in the sight of God; the earth was filled with violence.
> God saw the earth, and indeed it was ruined, for all living creatures on the
> earth were sinful. So God said to Noah, "I have decided that all living
> creatures must die, for the earth is filled with violence because of them.
> Now I am about to destroy them and the earth." *(Genesis 6:11–13)*

| | **6:5–7** | **6:11–13** |
|---|---|---|
| God is called | *the LORD* | *God* |
| What he sees | *the wickedness of humankind* | *the earth… ruined* |
| What is wrong | the **heart** — every inclination | **violence**; *all flesh had ruined its way* (lit.) |
| What he does | regrets; *it pained him to his heart* | *the end of all flesh has come before me* (lit.) |
| The verdict | *I will **wipe** humankind* | *I am about to **destroy** them* |
{: .ff-wrap}

Two things the NET cannot show. *Ruined, ruined, ruined its way, destroy*
are one Hebrew verb, ***šāḥat***, four times over: they ruined the earth, so
God will ruin them — the sentence is the crime come home. And *"God saw the
earth, and indeed it was ruined"* is built on the frame of 1:31, *"God saw
all that he had made—and it was very good!"* The same look, the opposite
verdict.

Once you have noticed it, it keeps happening.

| | **One telling** | **The other** |
|---|---|---|
| **Why** | the heart (6:5–7) | violence (6:11–13) |
| **What to take** | *seven pairs of every kind of clean animal… two of every kind of unclean* (7:2) | *two of every kind… male and female* (6:19–20) |
| **The water** | rain, *40 days and 40 nights* (7:4, 12) | *all the fountains of the great deep burst open and the floodgates of the heavens were opened* (7:11) — Genesis 1 in reverse |
| **The clock** | 7 days; 40 days; 7 days; 7 days (7:4, 10; 8:6–12) | dated by Noah's age: 600th year, 2nd month, 17th day… 150 days… dry on the 27th day of the 2nd month, a year and ten days later (7:11, 24; 8:3–5, 14) |
| **The end** | *the LORD smelled the soothing aroma and said to himself, "I will never again…"* (8:20–22) | a covenant, with a sign in the clouds (9:8–17) |
| **God's name** | *the LORD* | *God* — and both in one verse: *"just as God commanded him. Then the LORD shut him in"* (7:16) |
{: .ff-wrap}

None of this is a modern discovery. The rabbis were arguing about two pairs
and seven pairs a millennium and a half ago. What do you do with a story told
twice? Three answers.

### 1. Two stories, woven together ("J and P")

Since the eighteenth century, and for most critical scholars today, the Flood
is the classic case for what is called the **Documentary Hypothesis**. Take the
verses that call God *the LORD* (Hebrew *YHWH* — German *Jahwe*, hence "J"),
with the words and ideas that travel with them, and you have one story. Take
the rest, and you have another, by the writer scholars call the **Priestly**
source ("P"). The two strands do not only use different names; they use
different *words* — *a man and his mate* (7:2) against *male and female*, the
phrase of Genesis 1:27 (6:19; 7:9, 16); *wipe out* against *destroy*. They have
different numbers. And they have different ideas of God. J's God is the God of
the garden — he forms, breathes, shuts the door, smells the sacrifice, speaks
to his heart — and J's Noah tells clean animals from unclean and builds an
altar. P has no clean animals and no sacrifice at all, because, the critics
say, for the priestly writer such things begin at Sinai; instead P dates
everything, opens the cosmic deep of Genesis 1, and ends with a covenant and a
sign. Pulled apart, said John Skinner in 1910, the passages *"form two all but
continuous narratives."*

Why would an editor keep both, contradictions and all? *"Without sacrificing a
scrap of information"* (Skinner): he revered both and would not lose either.
There are ancient examples of exactly this. ***Gilgamesh*** Tablet XI took its
flood over from ***Atrahasis*** — and near the end still calls the hero by
Atrahasis's name. And for more than two centuries the Syriac-speaking churches read a
single Gospel woven from four, Tatian's *Diatessaron* (c. 170), until
fifth-century bishops replaced it with the four separate Gospels. On this view the doublets are **seams**, and many who hold it date
the Priestly writer to the exile or after.

*The question it has to answer:* no copy of J or P alone has ever been found,
and J is missing pieces — no building of the ark, no exit. (The critics' reply:
the editor kept P whole and used J to fill it out.)

### 2. One story, built as a mirror (chiasm)

In 1978 Gordon Wenham, an evangelical Old Testament scholar, argued that the
whole Flood is a **palistrophe** — a mirror-shaped story, like the one we found
in Genesis 1 in Session 2. The time periods run **7, 7, 40, 150 — 150, 40, 7,
7**, and at the exact centre stands one sentence: ***"But God remembered
Noah"*** (8:1).

| | Going down | | Coming up |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Noah (6:10a) | A′ | Noah (9:19) |
| B | Shem, Ham and Japheth (6:10b) | B′ | Shem, Ham and Japheth (9:18b) |
| C | Ark to be built (6:14–16) | C′ | Ark (9:18a) |
| D | Flood announced (6:17) | D′ | No flood in future (9:11–17) |
| E | Covenant with Noah (6:18–20) | E′ | Covenant with all flesh (9:8–10) |
| F | Food in the ark (6:21) | F′ | Food outside the ark (9:1–4) |
| G | Command to enter the ark (7:1–3) | G′ | Command to leave the ark (8:15–17) |
| H | **7 days** waiting for the flood (7:4–5) | H′ | **7 days** waiting for the waters to go (8:12–13) |
| I | **7 days** waiting for the flood (7:7–10) | I′ | **7 days** waiting for the waters to go (8:10–11) |
| J | Entry to the ark (7:11–15) | J′ | Raven and dove leave the ark (8:7–9) |
| K | The LORD shuts Noah in (7:16) | K′ | Noah opens the window (8:6b) |
| L | **40 days** of flood (7:17a) | L′ | **40 days** — the end (8:6a) |
| M | Waters increase (7:17b–18) | M′ | Waters go down (8:5) |
| N | Mountains covered (7:19–20) | N′ | Mountain tops seen (8:4–5) |
| O | **150 days** the waters prevail (7:21–24) | O′ | **150 days** the waters go down (8:3) |
| | | **P** | **GOD REMEMBERS NOAH (8:1)** |
{: .ff-wrap}

On this reading the doublets are not seams; they are **the two halves of the
mirror**. The story rises like the water and falls like it. Two more arguments
go with it. Wenham counted seventeen features that Genesis shares with the
Mesopotamian floods, *"usually in the same order"* — but J alone has only
twelve of them and P alone ten, so the full ancient shape exists only in the
combined story (Gary Rendsburg made the same case point by point in 2007). And
Umberto Cassuto of the Hebrew University (1941) argued that the names follow
the *meaning*, not a source: *God* (*Elohim*) for the Creator who rules nature,
*the LORD* for God in personal, moral relation. So the animals go in *"just as
God commanded him"* — and then *"the LORD shut him in,"* which Cassuto heard as
*"a father full of compassion towards his son."*

*The question it has to answer:* a mirror is easy
to find when you choose which details count — Wenham himself grants that his
pattern counts one week twice and leaves Noah's sacrifice outside it — and
critics such as J. A. Emerton (1987–88) replied that a skilled editor could
build such patterns out of two sources.
(Bernhard Anderson, who accepted J and P, read the finished text as a chiasm
too. And Wenham himself did not argue for Moses; he thought one older epic had
been reworked by a priestly editor.)

### 3. Sinai: one Author, two aspects

The oldest answer is the tradition's. The Torah came from God through Moses —
it is what Jesus calls *"the law of Moses"* (Luke 24:44) — and in a book from
God nothing is said twice for nothing. The rabbis
noticed the two names long before Europe did, and read them as two sides of
God:

> Everywhere that "the Lord" is stated, it refers to the attribute of mercy…
> Everywhere that God [Elohim] is stated, it refers to the attribute of
> justice. *(Genesis Rabbah 33:3, on "God remembered Noah")*

So at the very centre of the Flood it is *Justice* that remembers Noah —
and, as Rashi puts it, *"is transformed into Divine Mercy through the prayers
of the righteous."* Rashi (11th century) reads each doublet as a lesson. Seven
pairs of the clean animals: *"those cattle which will in future be permitted to
Israel as clean; we thus learn that Noah studied the Torah"* — and kept the
extra *"in order that he might offer some of them as a sacrifice."* The rain
of 7:12 before the flood of 7:17: *"at first He made it fall in mercy… in order
that if the people would repent, it might prove a rain of blessing; but when
they did not repent it became a destructive flood."* In the twentieth century
**Rabbi Mordechai Breuer** of Jerusalem went further. He granted the critics
their two strands — the text does divide — and held that **God wrote both**:
one telling under the aspect of justice, one under the aspect of mercy,
braided so that you must hold them together, as Genesis has already given us
two accounts of creation. And evangelical defenders of Moses' authorship, such
as Kenneth Kitchen (1966), argue that "doublets" are how ancient writers told
things — a summary, then the details, as in Egyptian royal inscriptions — and
that the dates make one consistent calendar of *"a year and ten days."*

This reading also hears the Flood the way the people at Sinai would have:

- Noah builds to God's plan and *"did all that God commanded him"* (6:22) — in
  Hebrew, the sentence Exodus uses when Moses finishes the tabernacle (40:16).
- The ark's *covering* comes off on *the first day of the first month* (8:13)
  — the day the tabernacle was raised (Exodus 40:17). Everywhere else in the
  Bible, that word *covering* means the covering of the tabernacle.
- Clean animals, an altar, a law about blood (9:4; Leviticus 17), and *"the
  perpetual covenant"* with a *sign* (9:12–16) — the words Exodus uses for the
  Sabbath (31:16–17).
- The rabbis heard the forty days of rain as the forty days in which the
  Torah was given (*Genesis Rabbah* 32:5).

*The question it has to answer:* the names do not always fit the attributes —
it is *the LORD*, Mercy, who decides on destruction in 6:5–7 (the midrash
admits it: *"Woe unto the wicked, as they transform [God's] attribute of mercy
into the attribute of strict justice"*); and how did Noah keep food laws not
yet given? (The Talmud asks that too.)

| **The doublets are…** | seams | the two halves of a mirror | two aspects — justice and mercy |
| **Who** | Astruc (1753), Wellhausen (1878), Driver, Skinner; most critical scholars; Friedman | Cassuto (1941), Wenham (1978), Rendsburg, Berman (2017) | the rabbis (*Genesis Rabbah*, Rashi), Breuer; evangelical scholars of Mosaic authorship |
| **Strongest point** | names, words, numbers and theology all sort the same way | the whole has a shape, and an ancient pattern, that neither part has | every word is meant — and the Flood points to Sinai |
| **Hardest question** | no J or P has ever been found on its own | a mirror can be found wherever you look for one | Mercy decides on the flood; Noah keeps laws not yet given |
{: .ff-wrap}

Notice what all three share. The Sinai echoes were first catalogued by the
*source critics* — John Skinner noted in 1910 that the ark's *covering* is
*"elsewhere only of the covering of the Tabernacle"* — and a 2026 study finds
echoes of the golden calf in Genesis 6 on both sides of the J/P line. Every
reading agrees that the Flood is told with Sinai in view. They disagree about
**who** did it: a priestly writer, a single author, or the Author. You may
hold any of the three. Whichever you hold, the story in our Bibles is the
doubled one — and it is meant to be heard that way.

<div class="discuss" markdown="1">
**Talk about it.** Read 7:16 aloud: *"Those that entered were male and female,
just as God commanded him. Then the LORD shut him in."* Does it sound to you
like a seam, a design, or two sides of one God? Does anything about how you
read the Flood change, depending on which?
</div>

## Next week — the neighbours' floods

You have met the two other great flood stories already.

In **Session 1** we read ***Atrahasis***, the Babylonian epic copied out around
1635 BCE: the gods made humankind to carry their labour, humankind multiplied,
and the land grew loud. Its later version gives the reason in the god Enlil's
own words: *"[Concerning] their clamor I am troubled."* Plague, then drought
and famine — and when none of them worked, the flood.

In **Session 8** we met **Gilgamesh**, the hero who wanted a name. At the end
of his epic, afraid of dying, he goes looking for the one man who survived the
flood — and the survivor tells him the story. (This is the Tablet XI that, as
Part 4 said, took its flood over from *Atrahasis* and still calls its hero
*"Atra-ḫasis"*.) It has a goddess who weeps for what she did:

> The lady of the gods wails with her beautiful voice. / The former day is
> verily turned to clay. / When I spoke evil in the assembly of the gods— / O,
> that I spoke evil in the assembly of the gods,— / For the destruction of my
> people, I ordered the cataclysm. *(Gilgamesh XI 118–122)*

Modern editions make her the mother goddess of Session 1, who shaped humankind
from clay. After the survivor's sacrifice — *"The gods like flies gathered
about the sacrificer"* — she lifts a necklace of lapis flies: *"Ye gods here, I
shall not forget my necklace. Upon these days I shall think, so that forever I
will not forget."*

A boat, a mountain, the birds, a sacrifice, a token to remember by, and a
deity who regrets. Bring three questions next week. **Why does each
flood come? Who regrets it, and when — before or after? And what does each
god promise to remember?**

## So what?

**1. The flood's verdict falls on us — and the heart survives the flood.**
Genesis takes away the excuse (*"the serpent tricked me"*; *the angels did
it*), and then shows that water cannot fix what is wrong. Ezekiel's *new
heart* is the answer it is waiting for.

**2. God's purposes do not change; God's heart is not stone.** However you
read *regretted*, the Bible will not let you keep only one of those.

**3. Grace is in the eyes of the giver.** The first time Scripture names it,
the man who finds it has not yet been described — and the next people to
need it are standing at Sinai.

**4. A story told twice asks to be read twice.** Two reasons, two counts, two
calendars, two promises, two names for God. Whatever you conclude about how it
was made, the doubling is there to be heard — and next week we read it
against the neighbours' floods.

<div class="discuss" markdown="1">
**Talk about it — to close.** *First:* which do you find harder to believe —
that God regrets, or that God has never felt regret? *Second, this week:* catch
yourself once saying a version of *"the serpent tricked me."* Then say what
Genesis 6:5 says instead.
</div>

# 3. Clues in Plain Sight

**Passage:** Genesis 2:4–3:24 ([full text for the hunt](texts/genesis-2-3.md)) — with several look-backs at Genesis 1.

## A note before we begin

Today we are going to make a long list of *problems* in the Bible — and then we are not going to solve a single one of them.

For some of us that will feel wrong, maybe even dangerous. Many of us were taught, sometimes without anyone saying it out loud, that noticing a difficulty in Scripture is the first step toward losing it — so the faithful move is to explain the difficulty away as fast as possible, or better, not to see it at all. If that's the training you carry, this session may pinch a little. That's okay. You are not being asked to believe the Bible less today. You are being asked to *read it more closely than you ever have* — and to discover that the oldest, most devoted readers this text has ever had did exactly what we are about to do. They found the bumps. They circled them. They handed them down to their children as treasures.

Here is the claim we will test today: **the problems are not flaws in the text. They are features of it** — placed deliberately, by a real author with real skill (and, the church has always confessed, by the Spirit who carried him along), to catch the attentive reader's sleeve and say: *dig here.*

## Aim of this session

In Session 1 we learned what a creation story *was* in the ancient world by listening to the neighbors. In Session 2 we looked at the *shape* of Genesis 1 and found it measured like a cathedral. Both of those arguments came from outside the story's plot — from context and from architecture.

Today's argument comes from inside. When you read Genesis 2–3 slowly — at the pace of a hearer, not a skimmer — the story keeps doing strange things. It repeats what doesn't need repeating. It omits what any chronicle would include. It stops the action for a paragraph of river geography. It lets a snake talk and doesn't blink. Each of these is what we'll call a **problem**: not an error, but a bump — something that snags on the mind of anyone paying attention.

Our plan has two movements:

1. **Notice the problems** — all of them we can find, sorted by *what kind of reader catches each one*, from a listening child to a philosopher.
2. **Sort them again by what each problem *does*** — because that second sort is the argument. Random damage scatters. These problems *organize*. Every one of them turns out to have a job.

What we will *not* do today is tell you what the problems mean. That is Sessions 4 and 5. Today is only about learning to see them — and about becoming the kind of reader who can hold an open question without flinching.

## Part 1 — Two ways to meet a problem

When a Western reader trained on textbooks meets a contradiction, the reflexes are fight or flight: either *harmonize it* (produce an explanation that makes the bump disappear) or *dismiss the text* (the bump proves the book is broken). Both reflexes share one assumption — that a bump is a defect.

The ancient readers of this text had a third reflex, and it is all over the rabbinic literature: **delight**. A bump in the text is where the author buried something. The rabbis' word for their close-reading commentary, *midrash*, comes from a root meaning *to search, to dig*. You do not dig where the ground is smooth. You dig where something disturbed the soil.

Rabbi David Fohrman, a contemporary teacher in that tradition, names the thing that keeps us from seeing the bumps at all: the **lullaby effect**. A story we have heard from childhood stops being *heard*. Its strangest moves — a talking snake, God asking where people are hiding — slide past us like the verses of a lullaby, soothing precisely because we no longer listen to the words. The first discipline of this session is simply to wake up.

And here is a test we will use all day, so keep it in your pocket:

> **The dissolve-or-sharpen test.** Take any oddity and add context — the ancient world, the Hebrew, the customs of the first hearers. If the oddity **dissolves** (it was only strange because *we* are far from the text), it was never a problem; it was a *cultural artifact*. But if the oddity **sharpens** — if the more background you learn, the *stranger* it gets — you are holding a designed problem. Dig there.

One example of each, before the full hunt. The "waters above the firmament" in Genesis 1:7 startle a modern reader; then you learn (Session 1) that every ancient Near Eastern cosmology pictured a solid sky holding back an ocean overhead, and the strangeness *dissolves* — the text is simply speaking its hearers' language. But the talking serpent moves the other way. Learn the ancient context and you find that in the entire Hebrew Bible exactly one other animal speaks — Balaam's donkey (Numbers 22) — and that story treats a talking animal as a jaw-dropping miracle requiring the LORD to personally open "the mouth of the donkey." Yet in Eden, a snake strikes up a theological conversation and *no one reacts at all*. Not Eve, not the narrator, not God. The more you know, the *weirder* it gets. That is a sharpener. That is deliberate.

## Part 2 — Clearing the ground: the non-problems

Before the hunt, let's take some famous "problems" off the table. Each of these dissolves the moment you stand where the first hearers stood. They teach us plenty about the distance between our world and theirs — but the author did not plant them, so they are not clues.

| Looks like a problem… | …but dissolves, because |
|---|---|
| **Waters above the sky; a solid "firmament"** (1:6–8) | This is the standard furniture of *every* ancient cosmology (Session 1). The text speaks the cosmological language of its hearers, as naturally as we say "sunrise." |
| **Chapter and verse numbers; section headings** (e.g., the chapter break at 2:1) | Added in the Middle Ages and later — not inspired, and sometimes badly placed. Genesis 2:1–3 (the seventh day) plainly belongs to the *first* account; the "chapter 2" label cuts the Sabbath off from its own week. The second account begins at 2:4. |
| **"Evening and morning" — the day starts at night?** | Ordinary Israelite reckoning: the day runs sunset to sunset (see Leviticus 23:32). Backwards to us; unremarkable to them. |
| **God takes Adam's "rib"** (2:21–22) | The Hebrew *tsela* everywhere else means **side** — the side of the ark, of the altar, of the tabernacle. The woman is built from the man's *side*, not extracted like a molar (the NET renders it "part of the man's side"). A translation artifact. |
| **God walks "in the cool of the day"** (3:8) | Hebrew *ruach ha-yom* — the NET gets it right: "the breezy time of the day," the evening wind everyone in that climate knows. The phrase is only mysterious in older English. |
| **The grinding repetition** ("after its kind… after its kind…") | Oral-literature convention. Texts built to be *heard and memorized* repeat their formulas; every ancient audience expected it. |
| **The narrator's asides** ("That is why a man leaves his father and mother…", 2:24) | A standard biblical convention (Hebrew *ʿal-ken*, "therefore/this is why…") that pauses a story to connect it to the hearers' present. Genesis does it constantly (11:9; 16:14; 26:33). |

Notice what just happened: the list of *real* problems got shorter — and everything left on it got *heavier*. What survives the dissolve test is exactly what cannot be blamed on our distance from the text.

## Part 3 — The hunt: five kinds of readers

Now the passage itself. What follows are **twenty-eight problems** — yes, twenty-eight, seven times four; we checked, and that one *is* a coincidence — sorted by the kind of reader who tends to catch each one. The sorting is the point: these bumps are layered at every depth of the text, from its surface story down to its individual letters. A vandal scratches one surface. Only a builder leaves marks at every level.

Read them with the [passage](texts/genesis-2-3.md) open. Resist the urge to solve. If an explanation leaps to mind, notice how fast it came — that speed is usually the lullaby singing.

### Problems a child notices

A child hearing this story for the first time — not yet trained on what to ignore — catches these immediately.

1. **The snake talks, and nobody is surprised.** No one in the story reacts to a conversing reptile — not the woman, not the narrator, and in the whole rest of the Hebrew Bible the only talking animal (Balaam's donkey, Numbers 22:28) is presented as a stunning one-off miracle. Here it is Tuesday.
2. **"You will surely die" — and they don't.** God says "when you eat from it you will surely die" (2:17) — and the Hebrew is stronger still: *in the day* you eat from it. They eat. They do not die that day; Adam lives to 930 (5:5). A child will ask you about this on the car ride home.
3. **The snake is sentenced to crawl** — "on your belly you will crawl" (3:14). So… how was it getting around before? The story quietly implies a walking snake and never explains.

### Problems a careful reader notices

Slow down to reading speed, track the sequence and the details, and a second layer appears.

{: start="4"}
4. **Two creation accounts that don't line up.** Genesis 1: plants (day 3), then animals, then man and woman *together* (day 6), and the watchword is effortless cosmic order. Genesis 2: "no shrub of the field had yet grown on the earth" — then the man is formed *first*, then the garden grows, then the animals are formed, then the woman *last*. Different order, different names for God (*Elohim* alone in ch. 1; *YHWH Elohim* in chs. 2–3), different feel. And whoever set these side by side read them more closely than we ever will — and left both, seam showing, as if the seam didn't embarrass him at all.
5. **"It is not good for the man to be alone" — cue the animal parade.** The stated problem is loneliness (2:18); the very next verse is God forming beasts and birds and marching them past Adam for names. The solution the story flags is a full chapter of narrative away. Why does the search for a companion *route through the animals*?
6. **The command was given before the woman existed.** God issues the food rule to the man in 2:16–17; the woman is built in 2:22. Yet in 3:2–3 she knows the rule. Who told her — and did they tell it to her straight?

<div class="page-break"></div>

{: start="7"}
7. **She quotes a rule God never gave: "you must not touch it" (3:3).** The original command says nothing about touching. Somebody — Adam? the couple together? — has added a fence around the rule. The earliest rabbinic commentators pounced on this addition (*Avot de-Rabbi Natan* 1) — noticing, as we are, that it is *in* the text on purpose.
8. **The tree grows a third attraction.** The narrator gave the garden's trees two qualities: "pleasing to look at and good for food" (2:9). When the woman looks at *this* tree, she sees three: good for food, attractive to the eye — "and… *desirable for making one wise*" (3:6). Where did the third one come from?
9. **The river paragraph (2:10–14).** The story halts — mid-Eden, tension building — for four verses of river names, gold quality reports, and regional geography ("the gold of that land is pure; pearls and lapis lazuli are also there"). None of it is ever used in the story. Why is it *here*?
10. **Of all the things to tell us: "the man and his wife were both naked, but they were not ashamed" (2:25).** Humanity is fresh from the hand of God — and the one detail the narrator selects to close the scene is their nudity, phrased with a shame-word the story hasn't earned yet. Why would shame even come up?
11. **The eyes open — and the great revelation is… nakedness.** The serpent promised opened eyes and godlike knowledge (3:5). They eat; "the eyes of both of them opened" — and what they come to know is *that they are naked* (3:7). The story builds to a cosmic climax and lands on a wardrobe problem.
12. **Fig leaves, then skins.** They sew coverings for themselves (3:7); at the end, God makes them "garments from skin" and clothes them (3:21). The story tells us about clothing *twice* — and the second time, the couple's own solution has apparently failed and been replaced.
13. **He names her twice.** "This one will be called 'woman'" (2:23) — then, after everything falls apart, "the man named his wife Eve" (3:20). A whole second naming scene, as if the first didn't take.

<div class="page-break"></div>

### Problems a pattern-spotter notices

This is the Session 2 skill — reading for refrains, structure, and count — turned loose on both accounts.

{: start="14"}
14. **Day 2 never gets its "good."** "God saw that it was good" falls on every day of Genesis 1 — except the second, and then day 3 collects it *twice*. The refrain is too regular for the gap to be sloppiness, and the rabbis noticed it nearly two thousand years ago (*Genesis Rabbah* 4:6).
15. **Day 7 never ends.** Six times: "there was evening, and there was morning, the *n*th day." The seventh day — the day the whole poem was built to reach — has **no evening and no morning**. Every other day is closed out. This one is left open.
16. **Days before the sun.** Light on day 1; the sun not until day 4 — so the first three "evenings and mornings" tick by with no sun to measure them, and the plants of day 3 grow before there is a sun to grow them. Read as a lab report, the story breaks in its first paragraphs; whatever kind of text this is, it is *loudly* not that kind.
17. **She "saw that the tree… was good… she took."** Genesis 1's refrain is *God saw that it was good*. In 3:6 "the woman *saw* that the tree produced fruit that was *good* for food… she *took*." The narrator hands God's own signature verbs to the woman at the story's hinge — the same formula, bent. (File that; it will return in Genesis 6:2 — the sons of God *saw*… that they were *beautiful*… and *took*.)
18. **The garden story has bookends.** It opens with a human alone, a naming ("this one will be called 'woman'"), and a serpent introduced as *shrewd*; it closes with a naming ("Eve"), a serpent *cursed*, and humans put out alone into the world. Front and back mirror — and dead center between them sits verse 3:7: *"the eyes of both of them opened, and they knew they were naked."* We saw in Session 2 what Genesis 1 puts at its center on purpose. This story has a center too.

### Problems a Hebrew reader notices

Some bumps are invisible in translation. The first hearers tripped over these; we need footnotes.

{: start="19"}
19. **Naked meets shrewd.** The man and woman are *ʿărummim* — naked (2:25). The very next word-breath, the serpent is *ʿarum* — shrewd, crafty (3:1). In Hebrew the two words sit back to back and sound nearly identical: the storyteller has rhymed the humans' condition with the serpent's character, deliberately blurring the line between them. English cannot show you this at all.
20. **The earthling from the earth.** The man is *ʾadam*, taken from the *ʾadamah*, the ground (2:7) — roughly, "the human from the humus." The pun is the story's frame: it returns with full weight at 3:19, "for you are dust, and to dust you will return."
21. **ʾish and ʾishah.** "This one will be called 'woman' (*ʾishah*), for she was taken out of man (*ʾish*)" (2:23). The wordplay makes the two names two halves of one sound — one creature, divided into a matched pair — which is precisely what the surrounding verses are saying.

<div class="page-break"></div>

{: start="22"}
22. **Which tree is "in the middle"?** Our translations smooth this one flat — the NET's parenthesis at 2:9 calmly sets *both* trees "in the middle of the orchard." But the Hebrew word order says: "the tree of life, *in the midst of the garden*, and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil" — the tree of *life* holds the center, and the forbidden tree dangles after it, location unspecified. Yet when the woman describes her garden, the tree *she* puts "in the middle" is the forbidden one (3:3). Either the geography is fuzzy — or the *center has moved*, depending on who is describing the garden. The ambiguity has kept rabbinic readers busy for millennia — and notice what just happened: even a rigorous modern translation ironed the bump out. The lullaby has help.

### Problems a philosopher notices

The deepest layer: bumps not in the wording but in the *logic* — the ones people feel but are often afraid to say in church.

{: start="23"}
23. **The knowledge paradox.** The tree supposedly *grants* the knowledge of good and evil. But the woman already reasons morally before eating — she can recite what is permitted and forbidden (3:2–3). And if the couple truly lacked moral knowledge until they ate, how is the first bite a culpable act? You can only justly punish someone who already knows better — which they can't, until *after*. The name of the tree and the logic of the judgment sit in open tension.
24. **"You will be like God" — they already are.** The serpent's bait (3:5) offers what Genesis 1:26–27 says humanity already possesses: the image and likeness of God. The temptation is to grasp for a thing already given.
25. **Score the predictions.** God said: "when you eat from it you will surely die" (2:17). The serpent said: "Surely you will not die… your eyes will open and you will be like God, knowing good and evil" (3:4–5). Now check the story's own scoreboard: they do not die that day; their eyes *do* open (3:7); and God himself confirms "the man has become like one of us, *knowing good and evil*" (3:22). Read flatly, every one of the serpent's claims verifies, and God's sounds like the overstatement. Be careful here — the text is *not* calling God a liar; it is deliberately constructing a surface reading that cannot be right, precisely to force you below the surface. What does "die" mean, such that God told the truth? That question is the trailhead — and today we only mark it.
26. **Why plant the tree at all?** A garden of delight, two innocents, and — placed by God himself, before any fall, with no fence but a word — a beautiful, fragrant, lethal tree. What kind of parent leaves the one deadly thing in the middle of the nursery? The story hands you this question with open palms.
27. **God asks questions He knows the answers to.** "Where are you?" (3:9). "Who told you that you were naked?" (3:11). "What is this you have done?" (3:13). The all-knowing God of Genesis 1 does not need information. So these questions must be doing something *else*.
28. **Why does God rest?** (2:2–3, from last session's passage.) Omnipotence does not tire. Yet the account's entire destination is God ceasing. A poem built with that much care does not end on a nap; "rest" must mean something we haven't said yet.

## Part 4 — The second sort: what each problem is *doing*

Here is where the argument lands. Suppose the skeptic is right and these twenty-eight bumps are just damage — errors of a naive author, scars of clumsy editing. Then they should be *random*: scattered anywhere, doing nothing, pointing nowhere. Real damage has no job description.

So let's give every problem a job interview. Sort the same twenty-eight not by who notices them, but by **what each one accomplishes in the reader** — and watch what happens.

| The job | How it works | Problems that do it |
|---|---|---|
| **⏰ Alarm bells** | A jolt of absurdity or misdirection that breaks the lullaby — the story does something so odd you *must* wake up and pay attention. | The unremarked talking snake (1) · the animal parade answering loneliness (5) · the river-geography freeze (9) · "naked… not ashamed" (10) · the eyes-opened anticlimax (11) |
| **🏷 Genre signals** | Features that make a *flat-chronicle* reading impossible on purpose, forcing the question "then what kind of text is this?" | Two unharmonized accounts, seam showing (4) · days and plants before the sun (16) |
| **📐 Structural markers** | Breaks in an otherwise perfect pattern — and a break in a pattern is an *arrow*. They point at centers, climaxes, and open ends. | Day 2's missing "good" (14) · Day 7 left unclosed (15) · the double naming as bookends (13) · the garden story's mirrored frame with nakedness at dead center (18) |
| **🌱 Planted hooks** | Words and images set here so they can detonate *later* — in this story's own ending, or chapters and books away. | Naked/shrewd pun (19) · dust-to-dust frame (20) · ʾish/ʾishah (21) · "saw… good… took" (17) · the added touch-rule (7) · the tree's third attraction, *desire* (8) · fig leaves replaced by God-made garments (12) · the once-walking snake (3) · which-tree-is-central ambiguity (22) |
| **❓ Open provocations** | Questions the text *builds into itself* and refuses to answer on the surface, so the reader has to carry them — sometimes for the rest of the Bible. | "Surely die"? (2, 25) · how did she learn the command? (6) · the knowledge paradox (23) · already like God (24) · why plant the tree? (26) · God's unneeded questions (27) · why does God rest? (28) |

Three observations, and then the conclusion draws itself.

**First: everything found work.** Twenty-eight alleged defects; twenty-eight functioning devices. Not one bump is inert — no stray typo, no pointless glitch. Damage doesn't behave this way. Craft does.

**Second: several problems hold two jobs at once.** The river paragraph is an alarm bell *and* a planted hook (Eden's river will flow again in Ezekiel 47 and Revelation 22). The nakedness verse is an alarm bell *and* the keystone of the structure (18) *and* one half of a Hebrew pun (19). When a single oddity serves the surface story, the architecture, and the sound of the language simultaneously, "accident" has stopped being an explanation. Over-determination is the signature of design.

**Third: none of this is our discovery.** The touch-rule addition is discussed in *Avot de-Rabbi Natan*; Day 2's missing "good" in *Genesis Rabbah*; the ambiguous central tree, the walking serpent, God's strange questions — all ancient conversation. These problems were *found* by the text's most reverent readers, generation after generation, because the problems were put there to be found. The lullaby is the modern development, not the noticing.

And a pun deserves one more beat, because it may be the single cleanest proof in the list. A contradiction could — conceivably — be an accident of editing. A pun cannot. *ʿărummim* / *ʿarum*, naked-meets-shrewd, placed in consecutive breaths at the exact pivot of the story, is a *decision*, the way a rhyme is a decision. Nobody rhymes by accident.

So the claim from the top of this handout stands the test: these are not flaws in the text; they are the text's own signposts. A human author of enormous skill — and, we confess, the Spirit who inspired him — salted this story with bumps at every depth: some a child can feel, some only a Hebrew ear can hear, some only a philosopher will admit to. Each one says the same thing: *slow down. You are not reading the kind of book you think you are reading. Dig here.*

## Part 5 — What we are deliberately not doing

Every trail we flagged today, we left unwalked. What *does* the center of the garden story mean? What *is* the death God spoke truly about? Why *does* the search for a companion route through the animals — what is the story working out about humans and beasts? What is desire doing in verse 3:6, and what would it mean to be creatures who, unlike the beasts, can look at a desirable thing and *not take it*? Why rest — what is God's ceasing *for*?

Hold them. That is the assignment. Session 4 will return to Genesis 1 with the genre question answered and read it as its first hearers did — as the founding of a cosmic temple. Session 5 will walk back into the garden with every one of today's problems in hand and watch them pay off.

Leaving questions open for two weeks is itself part of the training. The Western reflex wants the answer *now*, because an open question feels like a threat. The text is teaching the older habit: an open question, held with trust, is a place where God has promised to meet the digger.

## Try it together

A hunt for your group, with the [clean text](texts/genesis-2-3.md) in hand — it works with two people or ten:

1. **Hunt (10–15 min).** In pairs, read Genesis 2:4–3:24 aloud, slowly. Mark every bump — anything unnecessary, out of order, repeated, missing, or illogical. No solving allowed; solving is a foul.
2. **Pool.** Combine the group's finds into one list. (You will almost certainly find bumps that are not among today's twenty-eight. They are there. The rabbis' lists are longer than ours.)
3. **First sort.** For each find: *who* would catch it — a child, a careful reader, a pattern-spotter, a Hebrew reader, a philosopher?
4. **Second sort.** Then the real question: what is it *doing*? Alarm bell, genre signal, structural marker, planted hook, open provocation — or something else?
5. **Step back.** Look at the sorted board and ask the only question of the day: *does this look like damage, or does this look like design?*

## Discussion questions

1. Which of the twenty-eight problems had you truly never noticed before today — and which one, honestly, bothers you most? What does the *lullaby effect* suggest about how familiarity has shaped your reading of texts you love?
2. Run the **dissolve-or-sharpen test** out loud on two items: the "waters above" (1:7) and the talking serpent (3:1). Practice articulating *why* context dissolves one and sharpens the other. Where else in Scripture have you dismissed a sharpener as if it were an artifact?
3. Read Numbers 22:21–33 (Balaam's donkey) next to Genesis 3:1. In one story a talking animal astonishes everyone; in the other it astonishes no one. If both texts had the same author-level intent, what is the Eden story *signaling* by refusing to be astonished?
4. Be honest about your training: when you hit a contradiction in the Bible, is your reflex to harmonize it quickly, to quietly shelve it, or to dig? What would change — in your reading, and in your group — if you treated every bump as an invitation rather than a threat?
5. Today ends with twenty-eight open questions and zero answers. Sit with that: does it make you anxious or curious — and why? What would it look like, practically, to "trust the story" for two weeks: to believe that the Author left these problems for you on purpose, and that they lead somewhere good?

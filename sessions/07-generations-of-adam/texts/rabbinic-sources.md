# The rabbis on Genesis 4:17–5:32

A source sheet for **Session 7**. Everything here comes from the Jewish
interpretive tradition, from the synagogue translations of the second century
to Maimonides in the twelfth. For the readings that are older than this
literature — Ben Sira, *Jubilees*, *1 Enoch*, Josephus — see the companion
sheet, **[the earliest readings](second-temple-sources.html)**.

**On dates.** These documents were compiled centuries after the sages they
quote, and they quote them by name. Where a passage is attributed to a named
rabbi we give that rabbi's own century as well as the document's — so you can
see how far the tradition reaches back, and hold the attribution for what it
is: **tradition, not transcript.**

| Document | Compiled | Attributed voices reach back to |
|---|---|---|
| **Targum Onkelos** | c. 2nd century CE, later fixed in Babylonia | — |
| **Genesis Rabbah** | c. 400–450 CE, Roman Palestine | 2nd–4th century sages |
| **Talmud Bavli** | c. 500–600 CE, Babylonia | 1st–5th century sages |

| **Rashi** | 1040–1105, northern France | quotes Genesis Rabbah throughout |

| **Maimonides** (Rambam) | c. 1180–1190, Egypt | — |

Each entry is marked **→ toward the session's reading** or **⇢ a different
direction**. Both are useful. Where the tradition agrees with us it is an old
witness that we did not invent this; where it diverges it is a reminder that a
good text carries more than one true reading.

## 1. The two lines are married — Naamah

### *Genesis Rabbah* 23:3 → *toward the session's reading*

Genesis 4:22 names Tubal-Cain's sister and says nothing about her. The
midrash has a use for her:

> "And the sister of Tuval Cain was Naama." Rabbi Abba bar Kahana said:
> **Naama was Noah's wife.** Why did they call her Naama? It is because her
> actions were pleasing [*ne'imim*]. The Rabbis say: Naama was someone else,
> [not Noah's wife]. And why did they call her Naama? It is because she
> played the tambourine pleasantly [*man'emet*] for idol worship.

If Rabbi Abba is right, the last woman named in Cain's line marries the last
man named in Seth's, and **everyone after the flood descends from both.**
The Rabbis' dissent — she was a temple musician for idols, and Noah married
someone else — keeps the lines apart. Both readings are on the page, which is
how *Genesis Rabbah* usually leaves things. (The same section has the
comment on Tubal-Cain that Session 6 used: *"Cain killed, but he did not
have an instrument with which to kill."*)

## 2. "Then it was begun" — Enosh (4:26)

### Targum Onkelos 4:26 ⇢ *a different direction*

> To Sheis also was born a son, and he named him Enosh. Then men began to
> call [idols] [men stopped praying] by the name Adonoy.

*(The Metsudah edition's two bracketed glosses show both ways the Aramaic
has been taken; the Aramaic itself says the sons of men **ceased** from
praying in the name of the LORD.)*

### Targum Pseudo-Jonathan 4:26 ⇢ *a different direction*

> And to Sheth also was born a son, and he called his name Enosh. That was
> the generation in whose days **they began to err, and to make themselves
> idols, and surnamed their idols by the name of the Word of the Lord.**

### *Genesis Rabbah* 23:7 ⇢ *a different direction*

> "Then commenced [*huḥal*]" – Rabbi Simon said: In three places this term
> is stated as an expression of **rebellion**: "Then commenced the
> proclaiming of the name of the Lord"; "it was [*heḥel*] when man began"
> (Genesis 6:1); "he began [*heḥel*] to be a mighty one in the earth"
> (Genesis 10:8).

The Hebrew *hûḥal* is "it was begun"; the same letters spell "it was
profaned" (see [note 3 on the passage sheet](genesis-5.html)). The whole
Aramaic tradition took the second sense, and made Enosh's generation the
beginning of **idolatry** — calling created things by God's name. It is a
strong reading, and it explains something the plain reading does not: why
the next thing the story needs is a flood. The session keeps the plain sense
— worship of the LORD by name began — because *Jubilees* 4:14, two centuries
older than any Targum, read it that way too. But hear the Targum: in a
synagogue, this verse was the start of the trouble.

The medievals split along the same line. **Rashi** on 4:26: the word *"must be
connected in meaning with ḥullin, 'profane matters' — viz, calling the names
of men and the names of idols after the name of the Holy One, blessed be He —
making them the objects of idolatrous worship."* **Maimonides** opens his
*Laws of Idolatry* (1:1) here: *"During the times of Enosh, mankind made a
great mistake, and the wise men of that generation gave thoughtless counsel.
Enosh himself was one of those who erred"* — honouring the stars as God's
servants until the honour became worship. **Ibn Ezra** takes the plain sense:
*huḥal "comes from the same root as teḥillah (first)… then men first started
praying,"* adding that if the word meant *profane*, the verse would say the
name was profaned, not called upon.

## 3. "This is the book" (5:1)

### *Genesis Rabbah* 24:2 → *toward the session's reading*

> Another matter, "this is the book of the descendants of Adam," it is
> written: "Your eyes saw my unformed parts, and in Your book they were all
> written" (Psalms 139:16). … Rabbi Yehuda bar Simon said: While Adam the
> first man was placed unformed before Him who spoke and the world came into
> being, **He showed him each and every generation and its scholars, each and
> every generation and its wise men, each and every generation and its
> interpreters of the Torah, each and every generation and its leaders**…
> the unformed ones whom Your eyes saw, they were already written in the
> book of Adam the first man. That is, "this is the book of the descendants
> of Adam."

The midrash has noticed that this is the only *tôlədōt* heading in Genesis
that calls itself a **book** — and reads the book as God's, shown to Adam
before he was finished: every generation to come, with its teachers. A
genealogy, on this reading, is not a record of the past. It is a promise
about the future, written first.

### *Genesis Rabbah* 24:7 → *toward the session's reading*

> Ben Azzai says: "This is the book of the descendants of Adam" – **this
> verse represents the central tenet of the Torah.** Rabbi Akiva says: "You
> shall love your neighbor as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18) – that verse
> represents the central tenet of the Torah, [as it teaches] that you should
> not say: Since I have been disparaged, let someone else be disparaged along
> with me; since I was cursed, let someone else be cursed along with me.
> Rabbi Tanḥuma said: If you do act like that, know who it is that you are
> disgracing: "**in the likeness of God He made him**" (Genesis 5:1).

Ben Azzai (early second century) is arguing that a *genealogy* — this one —
is the great principle of the Torah, set beside "love your neighbour." (Here
the two stand unranked; in the parallel in the *Sifra* on Leviticus 19:18 he
says outright that his is the *greater*.) His reason is the half-verse Rabbi
Tanḥuma supplies: the heading
says every person on the list, and every person descended from the list, is
in the likeness of God. That is the session's reading of 5:1–3 in one
sentence: the image was not lost in the garden. It is inherited, and so is
its claim on how you treat the next person on the list.

### Targum Pseudo-Jonathan 5:3 and *Genesis Rabbah* 24:6 ⇢ *a different direction — "in his likeness" excludes Cain*

> And Adam lived a hundred and thirty years, and begat Sheth, who had the
> likeness of his image and of his similitude: **for before had Hava born
> Kain, who was not like to him**; and Habel was killed by his hand. And Kain
> was cast out; neither is his seed genealogized in the book of the genealogy
> of Adam. But afterwards there was born one like him, and he called his name
> Sheth. — *Targum Pseudo-Jonathan* 5:3
>
> Another matter, "this is the book of the descendants of Adam," the following
> are his descendants, but the previous ones were not [ordinary] descendants.
> … Rabbi Simon said: All one hundred and thirty years that Eve was estranged
> from Adam, the male spirits would be aroused by her and she would bear
> offspring from them and the female spirits would be aroused by Adam and
> would bear offspring from him. — *Genesis Rabbah* 24:6

The session reads *in his likeness, according to his image* as the image of
God handed on. The tradition reads it as **exclusion**: Seth is the son in
Adam's likeness *as opposed to* what came before — Cain, whom this Targum
makes the son of an angel and not of Adam at all (its 4:1 has Eve "desire the
Angel"), and the demons Adam fathered in his 130 years apart from Eve (so too
*Eruvin* 18b, in the name of Rabbi Yirmeya ben Elazar). For the Targum, then,
Cain's line and Seth's were never one family. Hold that against the handout's
"one family, going two ways": the parallel names say one thing, this
tradition says the other, and the Targum's Cain is a very old reading.

## 4. Enoch — how the rabbis read him

### Targum Onkelos 5:24 ⇢ *a different direction*

> Chanoch walked with [the fear of] Elohim, and he was no longer [there] for
> Elohim **took him [put him to death]**.

The printed Onkelos reads "God took him" as **"God caused him to die"** — no
ascension, no exemption — though a manuscript variant negates the verb, and
the Metsudah gloss follows the printed text. That is the reading a Babylonian
synagogue most likely heard.

### *Genesis Rabbah* 25:1 ⇢ *a different direction*

> "Ḥanokh walked with God, and he was no longer, as God took him," Rabbi
> Ḥama bar Hoshaya said: **He is not written in the ledger of the righteous,
> but rather, in the ledger of the wicked.** Rabbi Aivu said: Ḥanokh was
> capricious; sometimes righteous, sometimes wicked. The Holy One blessed be
> He said: '**While he is still in his righteousness, I will take him
> away.**' Rabbi Aivu said: On Rosh Hashanah he judged him, when he judges
> all mankind.
>
> **Heretics** once asked Rabbi Abahu, saying to him: 'We do not find death
> mentioned in regard to Ḥanokh.' He said to them: 'Why?' They said: 'Taking
> is stated here, and taking is stated elsewhere: "That today the Lord is
> taking your master from upon your head"' (II Kings 2:5). He said to them:
> 'If it is [the word] taking that you are expounding, taking is stated
> here, and taking is stated elsewhere: "**Behold, I am taking from you the
> delight of your eyes**"' (Ezekiel 24:16). Rabbi Tanḥuma said: Rabbi Abahu
> answered them well.
>
> A noblewoman once asked Rabbi Yosei, saying to him: 'We do not find death
> mentioned in regard to Ḥanokh.' He said to her: 'Had it said: "Ḥanokh
> walked with God" and then remained silent, I would have said as you said.
> When it says: "And he was no longer, as God took him," **he was no longer
> in this world, as God took him.**'

This is the most instructive passage on the sheet, and it is an argument
about method. The "heretics" — in fourth-century Caesarea, often taken to be
Christians — make the case Hebrews 11 makes: *lāqaḥ* is the Elijah verb, so
Enoch did not die. Rabbi Abahu answers with a concordance: *lāqaḥ* is also
what God says to Ezekiel about the death of his wife. **The word proves
nothing by itself.** For the church, Hebrews 11:5 settles what the verb meant
here; Rabbi Abahu's point is narrower, and it stands: the verb alone does
not. The noblewoman gets the plainer version — "he was not" means he was not
in this world. And behind both is a suspicion about Enoch's character: the
man Genesis says walked with God, these sages say was *capricious*, and God
took him early *before he could fall*. That is a reading of "he was not" as
**he did not finish his years** — Enoch's 365 against everyone else's 900.

Why the suspicion? Because by the fourth century Enoch had become the hero
of an enormous apocalyptic literature — the *1 Enoch* material on the
[earliest readings sheet](second-temple-sources.html) — and these sages
wanted no part of a man who toured heaven and came back with secrets. Their
Enoch is a warning, not a scribe.

But *Genesis Rabbah* is not the whole tradition, and "the rabbis were unsure"
would misdescribe it. Rashi, below, opens *"he was righteous."* Later
teaching holds both senses in **levels** rather than in doubt — the plain
sense that he died, the deeper sense that he ascended: Nachmanides counts
Enoch with Elijah among those who entered the next life in the body, and the
Enoch a Jewish child is likeliest to meet is Chanoch the cobbler of the later
midrash, who joined the upper and lower worlds with every stitch — which the
Mussar teachers turned into: he made honest shoes.

### Rashi on 5:24 ⇢ *a different direction*

Rashi, a thousand years later, hands the *Genesis Rabbah* reading to every
Jewish schoolchild since:

> AND ENOCH WALKED [WITH GOD] — He was a righteous man, but his mind was
> easily induced to turn from his righteous ways and to become wicked. The
> Holy One, blessed be He, therefore took him away quickly and made him die
> before his full time. This is why Scripture uses a different expression
> when referring to his death by writing *ואיננו* "and he was not", meaning,
> he was not in the world to complete the number of his years.
>
> FOR GOD TOOK HIM — before his time; a similar meaning of *לקח* "to take" we
> find in (Ezekiel 24:16), "I take away from thee the desire of thine eyes
> [by a plague]".

### Targum Pseudo-Jonathan 5:24 → *toward Hebrews — and past it*

And yet the tradition kept the other reading too, in the very translation
read aloud in Palestinian synagogues:

> And Hanok served in the truth before the Lord; and, behold, he was not with
> the sojourners of the earth; for he was withdrawn, and **he ascended to the
> firmament by the Word before the Lord, and his name was called Metatron the
> Great Saphra.**

*Saphra* is Aramaic for **scribe**. Here Enoch is not only taken up alive but
turned into **Metatron**, the highest angel of later Jewish mysticism, the
heavenly scribe. This is the later Enoch-Metatron tradition — the one written
up in *3 Enoch* — inside the synagogue's own translation, a Targum whose final
form is late but which carries older Palestinian material. **Both readings
are Jewish, both are old, and the tradition kept both** — which is why the
handout, reading with Hebrews, can still give Rabbi Abahu his due.

## 5. Noah's name (5:29)

### *Genesis Rabbah* 25:2 → *toward the session's reading*

> "He called his name Noah, saying," Rabbi Yoḥanan and Rabbi Shimon ben
> Lakish, Rabbi Yoḥanan said: **The interpretation does not correspond to
> the name and the name does not correspond to the interpretation.** It
> should have said instead: 'Noah, this one will give us rest [*yaniḥenu*],'
> or 'Naḥman, this one will comfort us [*yenaḥamenu*].' However, when the
> Holy One blessed be He created Adam the first man, He gave him control
> over everything; the cow would heed the plowman and [even] the furrow
> would heed the plowman. **When Adam sinned, they rebelled against him; the
> cow did not heed the plowman and the furrow did not heed the plowman. When
> Noah appeared, they abstained** [*naḥu*]. …
>
> Rabbi Eliezer said: He was called after the name of his offering, as it is
> stated: "The Lord smelled the pleasing [*niḥoaḥ*] aroma" (Genesis 8:21).
> Rabbi Yosei bar Rabbi Ḥanina said: He was called for the name of the
> resting of the ark, as it is written: "The ark rested [*vatanaḥ*]" (Genesis
> 8:4).

Rabbi Yoḥanan (third century) says out loud what the passage sheet's note 6
says: the pun does not work. Then he makes it work — not as etymology but as
**story**: Noah is the man in whose days the ground, in rebellion since Adam,
*rested* and obeyed the plough again. That is exactly the thread the session
follows from 3:17 through 4:11–12 to 5:29. Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yosei add
the two other places the root *n-w-ḥ* lands in the flood story — the ark
rests, and God smells a *restful* aroma — so that Noah's name is fulfilled
three times over in the chapters that follow.

### Rashi on 5:29 → *toward the session's reading — the comfort was a plough*

> THIS WILL COMFORT US — He will ease from off us the toil of our hands. **For
> until Noah came people had no agricultural instruments and he prepared such
> for them.** The earth had brought forth thorns and thistles when they sowed
> wheat in consequence of the curse imposed upon Adam Harishon: in the days of
> Noah, however, this ceased (Tanchuma).

Cain's line, on the handout's reading, invented everything except farming.
Rashi's Noah — following the *Tanḥuma* — invents the tool that makes farming
possible again. The curse of 3:17 is answered by a plough.

### *Sanhedrin* 108b → *toward the session's reading — Methuselah and the flood year*

> With regard to the verse: "And it came to pass that after seven days the
> waters of the flood were upon the earth" (Genesis 7:10), the Gemara asks:
> What is the nature of these seven additional days? Rav says: **These were
> the days of mourning for the death of Methuselah**; and this is to teach you
> that eulogies for the righteous prevent calamities from ensuing.

The Talmud has done the arithmetic the handout does — 187 + 182 + 600 = 969,
so Methuselah's life ends in the flood year — and turned the seven days of
Genesis 7:4 into his *shivah*. The flood waits for the mourning to finish.

### Targum Pseudo-Jonathan 5:29 → *toward the session's reading*

> and he called his name Noah, (Consolation,) saying, This shall console us
> for our works that are not prosperous, and for the labour of our hands
> with the earth which the Lord hath cursed **on account of the guilt of the
> sons of men.**

The Targum adds the last clause. Lamech does not just remember the curse; he
remembers *why*. The genealogy is nine generations of people living under a
sentence they know the reason for.

## 6. Working with the numbers

### *Genesis Rabbah* 26:2 ⇢ *a different direction — the numbers as data*

> Rabbi Yudan said: What is the reason that all the generations begot
> children at one hundred years or at two hundred years, but this one [Noah]
> begot at five hundred years? However, the Holy One blessed be He said: 'If
> they will be wicked, I do not want them to be eradicated by the water; if
> they will be righteous, I would be imposing upon him [Noah] to make several
> arks for them. So the Holy One blessed be He suppressed his [Noah's] fount,
> and he begot only at five hundred years. Rabbi Neḥemya said in the name of
> Rabbi Eliezer son of Rabbi Yosei HaGelili: [It was so that] even Yefet, who
> was the eldest, would not yet be one hundred years old – when he became
> subject to punishment – when the Flood came.

This is what it looks like to take every number in the chapter as a fact
with a reason. Noah fathers at 500 where everyone else fathers under 200 —
so the rabbis ask why, and answer from the plot: so that his sons would be
too young to be judged when the flood came (a hundred, on the reckoning of
Isaiah 65:20, which the passage goes on to cite). It is ingenious and it is affectionate, and it is the opposite of the
session's approach. Both are ways of honouring the text. Ours asks what the
*pattern* of the numbers is doing; theirs asks what each number is for.

### Maimonides, *Guide for the Perplexed* 2:47 (c. 1190) ⇢ *a different direction — the numbers as literal, but limited*

> As regards the Scriptural statement about the length of man's life in
> those days, I say that **only the persons named lived so long, whilst other
> people enjoyed the ordinary length of life.** The men named were
> exceptions, either in consequence of different causes, as e.g., their food
> or mode of living, or by way of miracle, which admits of no analogy.

The chapter this comes from is about figurative language in Scripture —
mountains that sing, treasuries of rain, God's "book." Maimonides is
prepared to read all of those as figures. The ages he keeps literal — but
only for the ten men on the list. Everyone else in the pre-flood world, he
says, lived a normal span. He is not conceding anything about genre; he is
protecting a different claim — that human nature has not changed, so a world
of nine-hundred-year-olds is not on the table. Ramban thought that was the
wrong thing to protect.

### Ramban on 5:4 (c. 1260) → *toward the session's reading — the decline is real, and gradual*

> The reason for their longevity is that the first man, the handiwork of the
> Holy One, blessed be He, was made in absolute perfection as regards beauty,
> strength, and height. Even after it was decreed upon him that he be mortal,
> it was in his nature to live a long time. **But when the flood came upon the
> earth, the atmosphere became tainted, and as a result their days kept on
> decreasing.** … The days of his sons who were born after the flood were
> still more shortened until they came down to four hundred years. You can
> see that this degree of longevity remained with them until the generation
> of the Dispersion, when the change of climates caused by the Dispersion
> affected them, and their days were again shortened. … It would appear that
> in the generations of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, people lived seventy and
> eighty years, just as Moses, our teacher, mentioned in his prayer. …
> **Now what the Rabbi has written in the Moreh Nebuchim does not seem right
> to me**, namely, that the longevity was only in those individuals mentioned,
> while the rest of the people in those generations lived lives of ordinary
> natural length.

Ramban holds every number literal and every pre-flood life long — and then
reads the *curve* exactly as the handout does: a cliff at the flood, another
step down at Babel, and the seventy-and-eighty of Psalm 90 by the patriarchs.
He is the traditional commentator who gives the session its "counting down,"
and he gets there without giving up a single year. And for anyone who wants
the ages literal, note this: Jewish tradition never adjusted them. *Seder
Olam* counts the same 1,656 years to the flood, and the Jewish calendar year
is reckoned from these figures to this day.

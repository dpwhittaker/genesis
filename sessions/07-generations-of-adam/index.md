## A note before we begin

Today's passage is the part of Genesis most of us skip: two family trees and
a column of very large numbers. It is also the passage where the question
this class has been circling since Session 3 — *what kind of writing is
this?* — stops being abstract. Adam lived 930 years. Methuselah lived 969.
Either those are ages or they are something else, and it is fair to want to
know which.

Some of us were taught that they are ages, and hold that sincerely. This
session makes a case; you may find it persuasive or not, and no one will ask
you to pretend either way. What it asks is smaller and, we think, more
interesting: **read the chapter the way its first hearers would have** —
people who already knew what a list of long-lived men before a flood sounded
like, because their neighbours had one — and notice what they would have
noticed. Then decide what you think.

## Aim of this session

Last time, the murder and the arithmetic of vengeance. Today, the lists.

Genesis 4 ends with **seven generations** of Cain, and Genesis 5 runs **ten
generations** of Seth. Six of the names are nearly the same. The second list
has numbers; the first does not. Both end at the flood. And Mesopotamia kept
a list of exactly this shape for two thousand years — kings before the flood,
reigns in the tens of thousands, a seventh who was brought before the gods.

So the session is about what a genealogy is **for**. Five movements: a
question about iron, a king list from Sumer, two lists that rhyme, the
numbers, and the refrain — *and then he died* — with the two men who break
it.

## Part 1 — What a genealogy is for

Start with a question that came up last time and deserves a real answer.

> Now Zillah also gave birth to **Tubal-Cain**, who heated metal and shaped
> all kinds of tools made of **bronze and iron**. *(Genesis 4:22)*

**Iron does not become the everyday metal until roughly David's time. So
what is Tubal-Cain, eight generations from Adam, doing with it?**

The instinct behind the question is right. Archaeologists date the start of
the "Iron Age" in the Levant to about 1200 BCE, but that is a label, not a
description: through the next two centuries **over ninety percent of tools
were still bronze**, and iron only becomes the ordinary working metal in the
**tenth to ninth centuries** — David and after. Smelted iron appears on a
small scale in Anatolia in the early second millennium; before that, the only
iron anyone worked fell out of the sky. Tutankhamun's iron dagger (c. 1350
BCE) is meteoritic. On any traditional dating, Genesis 4:22 is early by about
two thousand years.

**And the Bible elsewhere remembers metalwork as scarce:**

> A blacksmith could not be found in all the land of Israel… So all Israel
> had to go down to the Philistines in order to get their plowshares,
> cutting instruments, axes, and sickles sharpened. *(1 Samuel 13:19–20)*

So the Bible remembers a day when Israel could not find a smith at all — and
credits a man before the flood with bronze and iron. **The text is not
embarrassed by that, and we need not be either.**

### Nobody asks the same question about Jubal

Verse 21 gives the eighth generation from Adam **the harp and the flute**.
That is just as compressed as the iron, and no one has ever written in about
it — because we do not happen to have a period named after the flute.

Genesis 4:17–22 is an **etiology**: a story of origins that explains where
the things in the narrator's world came from by tracing each to a founder.
Cities, herding, music, metalwork. And an etiology always projects **the
narrator's own inventory** backward. Whenever Genesis was written — Part 3
comes to that question — its writer lived in a world that worked bronze and
iron, and asked where metalworking came from, he names the two metals *he*
knows and hands them to the ancestor. The technology in the list is the
author's technology, on any dating. That is what the genre does, and it is a
Session 3 "problem in plain sight" with a clean answer. (The other answer you
may have heard — that the skill was real before the flood and lost in it — is
a position people hold in good faith; note only that Genesis itself says
nothing either way.)

### And the name points somewhere real

**Tubal** is a *nation* elsewhere in the Bible — a son of Japheth in the Table
of Nations (10:2), and in Ezekiel a metal-trading people: *"Javan, Tubal, and
Meshech were your clients; they exchanged slaves and **bronze items** for
your merchandise"* (27:13). Scholars identify this Tubal with **Tabal**, a
kingdom in **Anatolia** known from Assyrian records — one of the earliest
regions where smelted iron appears. Greek writers remembered the Chalybes, a
people of that coast, as the inventors of ironworking.

So **Tubal-Cain** parses as something like *"Tubal the smith"* — a region
famous for metal, plus *qayin*, the Semitic word for a metalworker that is
also Cain's own name. The chronology is compressed, but the geography points
somewhere real. The Hebrew of 4:22 is awkward enough that translations smooth
it: read as it stands it says *"a sharpener of every craftsman in bronze and
iron"* — the one who equips the other smiths — though many scholars suspect
the phrase is damaged and once matched verses 20–21, *"the father of all who
work bronze and iron."* Either way the sense is the same. This is an
**eponym** — a founder who *is* his trade — not a biography. (Tubal = Tabal
is mainstream; tying the Genesis 4 figure to it is an inference, and you
should hold it as one.)

**Talk about it.** The text dates itself by its own technology, and does not
seem embarrassed about it. Does that unsettle you or relieve you? Where else
have you assumed a text has to be timeless in order to be true?
</div>

## Part 2 — The list from Sumer

Israel's first hearers did not read Genesis 5 cold. Their neighbours had a
document of the same shape, and it was already ancient.

The **Sumerian King List** goes back to about 2100 BCE; the section on kings
*before* the flood was added around 1900–1800 BCE, and its best copy, a clay
prism from about 1800 BCE, opens like this:

> After the **kingship descended from heaven**, the kingship was in Eridug.
> In Eridug, Alulim became king; he ruled for **28800 years**. Alalĝar ruled
> for 36000 years. … In Zimbir, **En-men-dur-ana** became king; he ruled for
> 21000 years. … In 5 cities 8 kings; they ruled for 241200 years. **Then the
> flood swept over.**

Eight kings in this copy; nine or ten in others. **Berossus**, a Babylonian
priest writing in Greek around 280 BCE, gives **ten**, and the tenth is the
flood hero. Look at the numbers: every reign is a whole
number of *šár*s (3,600 years) and *nēr*s (600); the total is exactly
sixty-seven *šár*. **These are not remembered ages. They are numbers in a
system.**

Three more things the list does, all of which Genesis will also do.

**The numbers collapse after the flood.** Kingship "descends from heaven"
again, and the first post-flood king reigns 1,200 years, then 960; a few
lines later Gilgameš reigns 126, his son 30, his grandson 15. The list slides
from myth toward memory, and the flood is the hinge.

**The arts of civilisation arrive before the flood.** Berossus tells of
**Oannes**, a being with the body of a fish and the head and feet of a man,
who came up out of the sea in the reign of the first king and *"instructed
people in letters and the techniques of different arts [including] city and
temple [building]."* He is the first
of the seven *apkallu*, the sages, one for each early king.

**The seventh king was brought before the gods.** En-men-dur-ana —
*Enmeduranki* — ruled at **Sippar, the city of the sun-god**. A later
Babylonian text has Shamash and Adad *bring him into their assembly*, seat
him on a golden throne, and give him the tablet of the gods, the secrets of
heaven and earth; he comes back and founds the guild of diviners.
It never says where the assembly sat; *"ascended to heaven"* is said of the
seventh **sage**, Utuabzu, paired with this king only in a list from 165 BCE.
The parallel to Enoch is real, and it is **assembled from three texts** — the
[Sumerian sheet](texts/sumerian-king-list.html) keeps them apart.

Now hold that next to Genesis 5. Ten from Adam to the flood. Enormous
numbers, in a system. And the seventh — Enoch — lived **365 years**, a solar
number, walked with God, and *"was not, for God took him."*

| | Sumer / Babylon | Genesis 4–5 |
|---|---|---|
| **Before the flood** | 8, 9 or 10 kings (Berossus: 10) | 7 of Cain, 10 of Seth |
| **The numbers** | multiples of 3,600 and 600 | multiples of 5, plus 7s |
| **After the flood** | 1,200 → 126 → 30 | 950 → 438 → 175 |
| **The seventh** | Enmeduranki of Sippar (the sun): brought into the gods' assembly, taught its secrets, founder of the diviners; his sage "ascended to heaven" | Enoch: 365 years, walked with God, "God took him"; later tradition makes him the first scribe |
| **The arts** | a gift — sages from the sea, under the first kings | invented by Cain's line, under no blessing |
| **How it starts** | kingship descends from heaven | the image of God is inherited (5:1–3) |
{: .ff-table .ff-wrap}

**The point is not that Genesis copied a list.** The point is that a Judean
hearer — and if the majority view in Part 3 is right, a Judean *in Babylon*,
where this list was still being copied — knew what this kind of document
*sounded like*, and would have heard at once what this one leaves out. No
sages from the sea. No kingship descending from heaven. And no kings — or
rather, the sharpest thing of all. *Image*, *ṣelem*, is
**royal** language across the ancient Near East: the king is the god's image,
his statue on earth. Genesis 5:1–3 hands that title to Adam, then to Seth,
then to every child on the list. Babylon lowers kingship from heaven onto one
man in Eridu; Genesis makes every human being the king. What Babylon received
as a gift from below, Genesis says a murderer's family *invented*; and where
Babylon's seventh king came back with omens, Israel's seventh man simply
walked with God. Same genre, different theology — the [Sumerian
sheet](texts/sumerian-king-list.html) has both lists.

## Part 3 — Two lists that rhyme

| Cain's line (Gen 4) | Seth's line (Gen 5) |
|---|---|
| Adam | Adam · Seth · Enosh |
| **Cain** *qayin* | **Kenan** *qênān* |
| **Enoch** | **Enoch** *(two places later)* |
| **Irad** | **Jared** |
| **Mehujael** | **Mahalalel** *(two places earlier)* |
| **Methushael** | **Methuselah** |
| **Lamech** | **Lamech** |
| Jabal · Jubal · Tubal-Cain · Naamah | Noah |
{: .ff-table .ff-wrap}

Two Enochs. Two Lamechs. Irad and Jared, Mehujael and Mahalalel, Methushael
and Methuselah, a consonant apart; Kenan is Cain with a suffix. **Six of
Cain's seven names have a twin in Seth's line.** That is not chance.

Why do they rhyme? To answer that we have to say something about how Genesis
was put together, and there are two honest answers.

**The majority view among scholars** is that Genesis weaves together older
written sources, and that these two lists come from two of them. Genesis 4
belongs to a strand that calls God *the LORD* and tells stories — the garden,
Cain, Lamech's song. Genesis 5 belongs to a priestly strand that calls him
*God*, loves headings, formulas and numbers, and wrote Genesis 1. On this
view the chapters preserve **two versions of one older list**, and the editor
who joined them left the seams showing. You can see one: 5:29 breaks the
chapter's formula (*he had a son*, not *he became the father of*), says *the
LORD* in a chapter that otherwise says *God*, and quotes the garden's curse —
it reads like a line from the story-strand set into the priestly list. And
4:26 says people began to call on the name of the LORD in Enosh's day, while
Exodus 6:3 says that name was not known before Moses: two strands, two
memories. Most who hold this view date the priestly writer to the exile or
just after — which would make Genesis 5's first hearers Judeans *in Babylon*,
with the King List still being copied down the street.

**The other view**, held by the tradition before the nineteenth century and by
many evangelical scholars today, is that one author — for the tradition,
Moses — wrote both chapters and **built the second list to answer the
first**. The rhyming names are design, not duplication; the divine names
track the subject (*the LORD* in covenant stories, *God* in creation and
order); and 5:29 quotes the curse because the author wants you to hear it
there. On this view the seams are joints in a deliberately built structure,
and the first hearers are Israel in the land, with Babylon's stories arriving
by trade and memory rather than by exile.

You will meet both in good commentaries, and you do not have to settle it to
read the chapter — because **the effect on a reader is the same either way:
Seth's line is Cain's line, redone.** The same names, walked again, going
somewhere else. Watch the twins diverge.

**Two Enochs.** Cain's Enoch gets a *city* named after him — the first in the
Bible, built by a man sentenced to wander (4:17). Seth's Enoch gets no city
and no invention. He walks with God, and is taken.

**Two Lamechs.** Cain's Lamech is the seventh from Adam, and he sings: *"I
have killed a man for wounding me… Lamech seventy-seven times!"* Seth's
Lamech lives **777** years and names his son *Rest*. One Lamech turned seven
into a licence; the other lives it out and hands it to a child as a hope.

**And what each line produces.** Cain's line produces things — a city, tents
and herds, the harp and the flute, bronze and iron — and Genesis reports it
all without praise or blame: human culture comes out of a murderer's house,
and the text does not flinch. Seth's line produces nothing you could hold. It
produces **people**: a son *in his likeness* (5:3), a man who walked with
God, a man who hoped for rest. And, in the verse that turns between the two
lists, one thing more:

> And a son was also born to Seth, whom he named Enosh. **At that time people
> began to worship the LORD.** *(4:26)*

*Enosh* means "man" in its frailest sense — mortal. It is in the generation
named *mortal* that people first call on the name of the LORD. (The synagogue
translators read the verb as *began to profane* — the start of idolatry; the
[rabbinic sheet](texts/rabbinic-sources.html) has it.)

**And two things the chapter does quietly.** In 4:25 Eve names Seth and
speaks — *God has given me another **seed***, the Hebrew says, the word from
3:15 that the NET renders *child*. By 5:3 Adam is naming him and she is gone.
From there Genesis 5 names no woman and no mother at all: ten fathers, ten
heirs, and *other sons and daughters*. The line the promise runs through is
told without the women who bore it, and that is worth saying out loud.

**One rabbi then married the two lines.** *Genesis Rabbah* 23:3 records
Rabbi Abba bar Kahana's view that **Naamah**, the last name in Cain's line,
was Noah's wife — so that everyone after the flood descends from both. The
other Rabbis dissent in the same breath, and one Targum goes the other way
entirely, denying that Cain was Adam's son at all. But the parallel names
already whisper it: **these are not two peoples. They are one family, going
two ways.**

<div class="discuss" markdown="1">
**Talk about it.** City, music and metal all come from Cain's house, and
Genesis will not call that bad. Where do you see culture and cruelty coming
out of the same door today — and what does it do to you that the text refuses
to sort them?
</div>

## Part 4 — The numbers

Now the column everyone skips. Here it is in one place, from the Hebrew text:

| | Fathered his heir at | Lived | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adam | 130 | 930 | *and then he died* |
| Seth | 105 | 912 | *and then he died* |
| Enosh | 90 | 905 | *and then he died* |
| Kenan | 70 | 910 | *and then he died* |
| Mahalalel | 65 | 895 | *and then he died* |
| Jared | 162 | 962 | *and then he died* |
| **Enoch** | 65 | **365** | *he was not, for God took him* |
| Methuselah | 187 | 969 | *and then he died* — in the flood year |
| Lamech | 182 | **777** | *and then he died* |
| **Noah** | 500 | (950, in 9:29) | the flood comes in his 600th year |
{: .ff-table .ff-wrap}

Read it as a pattern rather than a ledger and it starts to talk.

**The numbers are built.** Look at how they end. Nineteen of the twenty-eight
figures in the chapter are round — multiples of five: 130, 105, 90, 70, 65,
930, 905, 910, 895, 500. Eight more are a round number plus seven: 162, 187,
182, 807, 782, 912, 962, 777. The last, Methuselah's 969, is 955 plus
fourteen. Umberto Cassuto noticed the pattern in 1944. Be honest about what it
proves: a rule that admits *round, or round plus seven or fourteen* lets in
three numbers of every five, so by itself it is suggestive, not a proof. Two
things sharpen it. Nobody fathers his heir at 71 or 133 — the begetting ages
are round or round-plus-seven without exception. And several of the Samaritan
and Greek figures **break the rule** (Greek Lamech fathers at 188 and lives
753; Samaritan Lamech lives 653), so the pattern belongs to the Hebrew column
specifically — the one the other two seem to have reworked. Whatever else
these numbers are, the ones in front of you were **composed** — the way the
Sumerian numbers were composed in *šár*s and *nēr*s.

**The people who copied them treated them as chronology — and reworked it.**
We have the chapter in three ancient forms, and the numbers differ:

| | Hebrew (Masoretic) | Samaritan | Greek (Septuagint) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adam fathers Seth at | 130 | 130 | 230 |
| Seth fathers Enosh at | 105 | 105 | 205 |
| Jared fathers Enoch at | 162 | 62 | 162 |
| Methuselah fathers Lamech at | 187 | 67 | 167 |
| **Creation to flood** | **1,656** | **1,307** | **2,242** |
{: .ff-table .ff-wrap}

This is not copying error. The Greek adds a hundred years to a father's age
*and takes the same hundred off his remaining years*, so each man's total
still comes out right — a mistake does not compensate for itself six times.
The Samaritan trims three fathers so that Jared, Methuselah and Lamech all
die *in the flood year*. In the main Greek manuscripts Methuselah **outlives
the flood by fourteen years** — a problem ancient readers noticed, and some
copies quietly fixed. And when 1 Chronicles retells the same ten names
(1:1–4), it **drops every number**. Read that both ways, because it cuts both
ways: the scribes cared about these figures as *dates*, enough to keep the
sums working — and they were willing to change them to make the story add
up.

**What they say without being added up.** They fall. 969, 950, then after
the flood 600, 438; Abraham 175, Jacob 147, Joseph 110, Moses 120 — and then
Psalm 90's *seventy years, or eighty if one is strong*. That is the King
List's curve: enormous before the flood, a cliff, then a long slide. (Genesis
6:3's *"120 more years"* has been read since antiquity both as a new limit on
human life and — as Rashi and the NET take it — as the time left before the
flood; the curve does not depend on it.) **The numbers are counting down.**
They are the sound of a world getting further from the garden.

None of this settles whether Methuselah drew breath for 969 years, and
careful readers have answered that differently for two thousand years.
Josephus said yes, and cited Berossus. Maimonides said yes for the ten named
men only. Ramban said yes for everyone before the flood, and read the decline
afterward as gradual and physical — a poisoned air. Jewish tradition has never
adjusted these figures: the Jewish calendar year is counted from them to this
day, so for the rabbis *composed* and *true* were never alternatives. Among
Christians, Ussher added them up to date creation; the Old Princeton
theologians William Henry Green (1890) and B. B. Warfield (1911) argued that
biblical genealogies are demonstrably *selective* — Matthew 1 skips kings to
make its fourteens — so a list can be shaped while every age in it stays
literal; and Kenneth Kitchen sets Genesis 5 beside the King List and finds
Genesis the sober one. **You may land anywhere on that.** What the chapter
asks is not that you decide, but that you notice what its numbers are *doing*
— and what they are doing is descending, in a pattern, toward a flood.

<div class="discuss" markdown="1">
**Talk about it.** If the numbers are a composed pattern rather than a
register, what changes about how you read the chapter — and what, honestly,
does not change at all?
</div>

## Part 5 — The refrain, and the two who break it

Now read Genesis 5 for what it actually says, which is mostly one sentence.

### First, what it inherits

> This is the record of the family line of Adam. When God created humankind,
> he made them in the likeness of God. He created them male and female; when
> they were created, **he blessed them** and named them "humankind." When Adam
> had lived 130 years he fathered a son **in his own likeness, according to
> his image**, and he named him Seth. *(5:1–3)*

Verses 1–2 quote Genesis 1:26–28 nearly word for word — the likeness, male
and female, and **the blessing**. Keep the blessing in your ear and the
chapter changes: *he had other sons and daughters*, nine times over, is
Genesis 1:28 — *be fruitful and multiply* — being kept, generation after
generation, in a world under the curse. Then verse 3 uses the same two words
— *likeness* and *image*, in reverse order — for what Adam passes to Seth.
Read straight (and this is our observation; the old commentators make a
different one, on the [rabbinic sheet](texts/rabbinic-sources.html)), it
answers a question the garden left hanging: **the image of God was not
lost.** It is inherited. It goes down a line that begins with the replacement
for a murdered son, in the verse after a boast about killing a boy.

Ben Azzai heard how much rides on that. *Genesis Rabbah* 24:7 sets his verse
— *"This is the book of the descendants of Adam"* — beside Rabbi Akiva's
*love your neighbour as yourself* as **the great principle of the Torah** (a
parallel version has him say his is the greater), and Rabbi Tanḥuma explains
why: *"in the likeness of God He made him"* — every name on the list, and
every person descended from it, carries the image. A genealogy is a theology
of who your neighbour is.

### Then, the refrain

*And then he died. And then he died. And then he died.* **Eight times.** The
chapter that opens with the blessing and the image is also a list of the
image dying — not a punishment shouted, but a limit quietly kept: each man
full of years, and then the end. Paul has the shape of it when he writes that
*"death reigned from Adam until Moses"* (Romans 5:14). The numbers are
composed to be enormous, and that is the point of them: even *that* long,
and the sentence still arrives.

Two men break the refrain.

### Enoch

> Enoch walked with God, and then he disappeared because God took him away.
> *(5:24)*

Three Hebrew details carry the weight here. **"Walked with God"** is the verb
form Genesis 3:8 uses for God *walking about in the garden* — Enoch is doing
what God did in Eden, and the preposition is *with*, not *before*; in
Genesis only Enoch and Noah get that phrase. **"He was not"** is what Jacob
says of Joseph — *gone*. And **"took"** is the verb of Elijah's chariot (2
Kings 2) and of the psalmist's hope past the grave: *"God will redeem my life
from Sheol, for he will **take** me"* (49:15).

That is the whole basis *in Genesis* for the reading that Enoch did not die
— and it is the reading the church holds, because **Hebrews 11:5 makes it**:
*"By faith Enoch was taken up so that he did not see death, and he was not to
be found because God took him up. For before his removal he had been
commended as having pleased God."* Genesis leaves the door ajar; Hebrews
walks through it; we read with Hebrews. The earliest Jewish readers were
already there. Ben Sira (c. 180 BCE): *"No man was born upon earth like
Henoch: for he also was taken up from the earth."* And *Jubilees* made him
**the first man to learn writing**, who *"wrote down the signs of heaven"* and
was *"shown the rule of the sun"* — which is Enmeduranki's résumé, handed to
Israel's seventh man with the omens taken out and righteousness put in. The
seventh king of Sippar came back from the gods with the secrets of
divination. The seventh from Adam walked with God, and God kept him. (Jude 14
counts him *"the seventh in descent beginning with Adam"* and quotes a line
from *1 Enoch* — quoting a line, as Paul quotes a Greek poet in Acts 17, is
not endorsing a book.)

**The rabbis read him differently, and it is worth knowing why.** *Genesis
Rabbah* 25:1: *"He is not written in the ledger of the righteous, but rather,
in the ledger of the wicked"*; Enoch was *"capricious; sometimes righteous,
sometimes wicked,"* so God *"took him away while he is still in his
righteousness"* — early, at 365, before he could fall. When "heretics" — often
taken to be Christians — argue from the Elijah verb that Enoch never died,
Rabbi Abahu answers with the same verb in Ezekiel 24:16, where God *takes* the
prophet's wife by death. His point stands on its own terms: **the word *took*
proves nothing by itself.** What settles it for us is not the word but
Hebrews. And the Jewish tradition did not stop at Rabbi Abahu either: the
synagogue's own Aramaic translation has Enoch *"ascend to the firmament… and
his name was called Metatron the Great Saphra"* — the heavenly scribe — and
later Jewish teaching holds the two as levels rather than rivals, the plain
sense that he died and the deeper sense that he rose. So: Genesis says *God
took him*; Hebrews says what that meant; and the oldest argument about it was
an argument about a verb.

### Noah

> He named him Noah, saying, "This one will bring us comfort from our labor
> and from the painful toil of our hands **because of the ground that the
> LORD has cursed**." *(5:29)*

*Noah* means *rest*; Lamech's verb means *comfort*; the pun is loose, and the
rabbis said so out loud. But the sentence is exact. **This is the first time
anyone in Genesis has mentioned the curse on the ground since God pronounced
it** (3:17) and Cain inherited it (4:11–12). Nine generations have been born
and buried under it, and the ninth names his son as the one who will finally
answer it. *Genesis Rabbah* 25:2 tells it as a story: since Adam, *"the cow
did not heed the plowman and the furrow did not heed the plowman"* — until
Noah, when they *rested*. Rashi, following an older midrash, says what the
comfort was: **Noah invented the plough.**

Cain's line answered the cursed ground by building **everything except a
farm**. Seth's line answers it with a man who will be, in the next chapter's
words, *"a man of the soil"* (9:20) — a farmer, who plants a vineyard when the
water goes down. And notice how the chapter ends. Every other entry closes
*and then he died*. Noah's does not. His death is recorded — four chapters
later (9:29) — but Genesis 5 will not say it. The list that counted down to
the flood stops, on its last man, with the refrain unspoken.

<div class="page-break"></div>

## So what?

**1. A genealogy is theology, not a registry.** Its technology is the
author's, its numbers are composed, and its names are chosen. None of that
is a flaw. It is how this kind of writing tells the truth — and Israel's
neighbours told theirs the same way, with a different God.

**2. The image was not lost.** Genesis 5:3 hands Adam's likeness to Seth in
the same words God used for Adam. Ben Azzai called it the greatest principle
in the Torah. The person in front of you is on this list.

**3. Death is the refrain, and God breaks it once — quietly.** *God took him*
is all Genesis says about Enoch; Hebrews tells us what it meant — *he did not
see death* — and the earliest Jewish readers had already heard it that way.
The oldest argument against it was an argument about a verb.

**4. Nine generations waited for rest, and one man said so.** Lamech named
his hope out loud, over a cursed field, with no evidence it would come. It
came from a farmer.

<div class="discuss" markdown="1">
**Talk about it — to close.** *First:* Lamech names a child *Rest* while
standing on ground that will not yield. What are you naming hope over right
now that has not yet come? *Second, this week:* pick one name on your own
list — someone you descend from, or someone who descends from you — and say
to yourself, in Ben Azzai's words, *in the likeness of God He made them*. See
what it changes.
</div>

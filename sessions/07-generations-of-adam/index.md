# 7. The Generations of Adam

**Passage:** Genesis 4:17–5:32 ([clean text for reading along](texts/genesis-5.html))

**Source sheets:** **[the Sumerian King List and Berossus](texts/sumerian-king-list.html)** · **[the earliest readings](texts/second-temple-sources.html)** (Ben Sira, Jubilees, 1 Enoch, Josephus, and the New Testament) · **[the rabbis on Genesis 4:17–5:32](texts/rabbinic-sources.html)**
{: .no-print}

## A note before we begin

Today's passage is the part of Genesis most of us skip: two family trees and
a column of very large numbers. It is also the passage where the question
this class has been circling since Session 3 — *what kind of writing is
this?* — stops being abstract. Adam lived 930 years. Methuselah lived 969.
Either those are ages or they are something else, and it is fair to want to
know which.

Some of us were taught that they are ages, and hold that sincerely. Nothing
today asks you to give that up on the spot. What today asks is smaller and,
we think, more interesting: **read the chapter the way its first hearers
would have** — people who already knew what a list of long-lived men before
a flood sounded like, because their neighbours had one — and notice what
they would have noticed. Then decide what you think.

## Aim of this session

Last time, the murder and the arithmetic of vengeance. Today, the lists.

Genesis 4 ends with **seven generations** of Cain, and Genesis 5 runs **ten
generations** of Seth. Six of the names are nearly the same. The second list
has numbers; the first does not. Both end at the flood. And Mesopotamia kept
a list of exactly this shape for two thousand years — kings before the flood,
reigns in the tens of thousands, a seventh who was taken up to heaven.

So the session is about what a genealogy is **for**. Five movements: a
question about iron, a king list from Sumer, two lists that rhyme, the
numbers, and the refrain — *and then he died* — with the two men who break
it.

## Part 1 — What a genealogy is for

Start with a question that came up last time and deserves a real answer.

> Now Zillah also gave birth to **Tubal-Cain**, who heated metal and shaped
> all kinds of tools made of **bronze and iron**. *(Genesis 4:22)*

**The Iron Age does not begin until roughly David's time. So what is
Tubal-Cain, seven generations from Adam, doing with iron?**

The instinct behind the question is right. Archaeologists date the start of
the "Iron Age" in the Levant to about 1200 BCE, but that is a label, not a
description: through the next two centuries **over ninety percent of tools
were still bronze**, and iron only becomes the ordinary working metal in the
**tenth to ninth centuries** — David and after. Smelted iron appears on a
small scale in Anatolia in the early second millennium; before that, the only
iron anyone worked fell out of the sky. Tutankhamun's iron dagger (c. 1350
BCE) is meteoritic. On any traditional dating, Genesis 4:22 is early by about
two thousand years.

**And the Bible elsewhere remembers the scarcity accurately:**

> A blacksmith could not be found in all the land of Israel… So all Israel
> had to go down to the Philistines in order to get their plowshares,
> cutting instruments, axes, and sickles sharpened. *(1 Samuel 13:19–20)*

So the text knows iron was rare in Saul's day, and credits a man before the
flood with forging it. **That is not a mistake. It is a tell.**

### Nobody asks the same question about Jubal

Verse 21 gives the seventh generation from Adam **the harp and the flute**.
That is just as compressed as the iron, and no one has ever written in about
it — because we do not happen to have a period named after the flute.

Genesis 4:17–22 is an **etiology**: a story of origins that explains where
the things in the narrator's world came from by tracing each to a founder.
Cities, herding, music, metalwork. And an etiology always projects **the
narrator's own inventory** backward. A writer living in the Iron Age, asked
where metalworking came from, names the two metals *he* knows — bronze and
iron — and hands them to the ancestor. The technology in the list is the
author's technology. That is what the genre does, and it is a Session 3
"problem in plain sight" with a clean answer.

### And the name points somewhere real

**Tubal** is a *nation* elsewhere in the Bible — a son of Japheth in the Table
of Nations (10:2), and in Ezekiel a metal-trading people: *"Javan, Tubal, and
Meshech were your clients; they exchanged slaves and **bronze items** for
your merchandise"* (27:13). Scholars identify this Tubal with **Tabal**, a
kingdom in **Anatolia** known from Assyrian records — and Anatolia is where
iron smelting actually began. Greek writers remembered the tribes of that
region as the inventors of metallurgy.

So **Tubal-Cain** parses as something like *"Tubal the smith"* — a region
famous for metal, plus *qayin*, the Semitic word for a metalworker that is
also Cain's own name. The chronology is compressed, but **the geography is
right.** The Hebrew of 4:22 even reads, literally, *"a sharpener of every
craftsman in bronze and iron"* — not merely a maker, but the one who
**equips the other smiths**. A guild founder. Which is exactly what an
eponymous ancestor is for. (Tubal = Tabal is mainstream; tying the Genesis 4
figure to it is an inference, and you should hold it as one.)

<div class="discuss" markdown="1">
**Talk about it.** The text dates itself by its own technology, and does not
seem embarrassed about it. Does that unsettle you or relieve you? Where else
have you assumed a text has to be timeless in order to be true?
</div>

## Part 2 — The list from Sumer

Israel's first hearers did not read Genesis 5 cold. Their neighbours had a
document of the same shape, and it was already ancient.

The **Sumerian King List** was composed around 2100 BCE and copied for
centuries. Its best copy, a clay prism written about 1800 BCE, opens like
this:

> After the **kingship descended from heaven**, the kingship was in Eridug.
> In Eridug, Alulim became king; he ruled for **28800 years**. Alalĝar ruled
> for 36000 years. … In Zimbir, **En-men-dur-ana** became king; he ruled for
> 21000 years. … In 5 cities 8 kings; they ruled for 241200 years. **Then the
> flood swept over.**

Eight kings before the flood in this copy; nine or ten in others. A Babylonian
priest named **Berossus**, writing in Greek around 280 BCE, gives **ten**, and
the tenth is the flood hero. Look at the numbers: every reign is a whole
number of *šár*s (3,600 years) and *nēr*s (600). Alulim's 28,800 is eight
*šár*. The total is exactly sixty-seven. **These are not remembered ages.
They are numbers in a system.**

Three more things the list does, all of which Genesis will also do.

**The numbers collapse after the flood.** Kingship "descends from heaven"
again, and the first post-flood king reigns 1,200 years, then 960; a few
lines later Gilgameš reigns 126, his son 30, his grandson 15. The list slides
from myth to something like memory, and the flood is the hinge.

**The arts of civilisation arrive before the flood.** Berossus tells of
**Oannes**, a being with the body of a fish and the head and feet of a man,
who came up out of the sea in the reign of the first king and *"instructed
people in letters and the techniques of different arts [including] city and
temple [building], knowledge of laws… how to collect seeds and fruits; indeed,
he taught humankind everything necessary for domestic life on earth."* He is
the first
of the seven *apkallu*, the sages, one for each early king.

**The seventh king was taken up.** En-men-dur-ana — *Enmeduranki* — ruled at
**Sippar, the city of the sun-god**. Later Babylonian tradition says the gods
Shamash and Adad *brought him into their assembly*, seated him on a golden
throne, showed him the secrets of heaven and earth, and gave him the tablet
of the gods; he came back and founded the guild of diviners. The seventh
sage, paired with him, is the one *"who ascended to heaven."*

Now hold that next to Genesis 5. Ten from Adam to the flood. Enormous
numbers, in a system. And the seventh — Jude 14 counts it for you, *"Enoch,
the seventh in descent beginning with Adam"* — lived **365 years**, a solar
number, walked with God, and *"was not, for God took him."*

| | Sumer / Babylon | Genesis 4–5 |
|---|---|---|
| **Before the flood** | 8, 9 or 10 kings (Berossus: 10) | 7 of Cain, 10 of Seth |
| **The numbers** | multiples of 3,600 and 600 | multiples of 5, plus 7s |
| **After the flood** | 1,200 → 126 → 30 | 950 → 438 → 175 |
| **The seventh** | Enmeduranki of Sippar (the sun): taken into the gods' assembly, taught the secrets of heaven, founder of the diviners | Enoch: 365 years, walked with God, "God took him"; later tradition makes him the first scribe |
| **The arts** | a gift — sages from the sea, under the first kings | invented by Cain's line, under no blessing |
| **How it starts** | kingship descends from heaven | the image of God is inherited (5:1–3) |
{: .ff-table .ff-wrap}

**The point is not that Genesis copied a list.** The point is that a Hebrew
hearer knew what this kind of document *sounded like* — and would have heard
at once what this one leaves out. No kings. No sages from the sea. No
kingship descending from heaven. What Babylon received as a gift from below,
Genesis says a murderer's family *invented*; and where Babylon's seventh king
came back with omens, Israel's seventh man simply walked with God. Same
genre. Different theology. The [Sumerian sheet](texts/sumerian-king-list.html)
has both lists in full.

## Part 3 — Two lists that rhyme

| Cain's line (Gen 4) | Seth's line (Gen 5) |
|---|---|
| Adam | Adam · Seth · Enosh |
| **Cain** *qayin* | **Kenan** *qênān* |
| **Enoch** | **Enoch** *(two places later)* |
| **Irad** | **Jared** |
| **Mehujael** | **Mahalalel** *(two places earlier)* |
| **Methushael** | **Methuselah** |
| **Lamech** | **Lamech** |
| Jabal · Jubal · Tubal-Cain · Naamah | Noah |
{: .ff-table .ff-wrap}

Two Enochs. Two Lamechs. Irad and Jared, Mehujael and Mahalalel, Methushael
and Methuselah, a consonant apart; Kenan is Cain with a suffix. **Six of
Cain's seven names have a twin in Seth's line.** That is not chance.

Scholars explain it two ways, and both have been on the table for a century:
the chapters preserve **two versions of one older list**, stitched together
— or one author **built the second list to answer the first**. You need not
choose, because the effect on a reader is the same either way: **Seth's line
is Cain's line, redone.** The same names, walked again, going somewhere else.
Watch the twins diverge.

**Two Enochs.** Cain's Enoch gets a *city* named after him — the first in the
Bible, built by a man sentenced to wander (4:17). Seth's Enoch gets no city
and no invention. He walks with God, and is taken.

**Two Lamechs.** Cain's Lamech is the seventh from Adam, and he sings: *"I
have killed a man for wounding me… Lamech seventy-seven times!"* Seth's
Lamech lives **777** years and names his son *Rest*. One Lamech turned seven
into a licence; the other lives it out and hands it to a child as a hope.

**And what each line produces.** Cain's line produces things — a city, tents
and herds, the harp and the flute, bronze and iron — and Genesis reports it
all without praise or blame: human culture comes out of a murderer's house,
and the text does not flinch. Seth's line produces nothing you could hold. It
produces **people**: a son *in his likeness* (5:3), a man who walked with
God, a man who hoped for rest. And, in the verse that turns between the two
lists, one thing more:

> And a son was also born to Seth, whom he named Enosh. **At that time people
> began to worship the LORD.** *(4:26)*

*Enosh* means "man" in its frailest sense — mortal. It is in the generation
named *mortal* that people first call on the name of the LORD. (The synagogue
translators read the verb as *began to profane* — the start of idolatry; the
[rabbinic sheet](texts/rabbinic-sources.html) has it.)

**The tradition then married the two lines.** *Genesis Rabbah* 23:3 records
Rabbi Abba bar Kahana's view that **Naamah**, the last name in Cain's line,
was Noah's wife — so that everyone after the flood descends from both. The
Rabbis dissent in the same breath — but it is what the parallel names already
whisper: **these are not two peoples. They are one family, going two ways.**

<div class="discuss" markdown="1">
**Talk about it.** City, music and metal all come from Cain's house, and
Genesis will not call that bad. Where do you see culture and cruelty coming
out of the same door today — and what does it do to you that the text refuses
to sort them?
</div>

## Part 4 — The numbers

Now the column everyone skips. Here it is in one place, from the Hebrew text:

| | Fathered his heir at | Lived | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adam | 130 | 930 | *and then he died* |
| Seth | 105 | 912 | *and then he died* |
| Enosh | 90 | 905 | *and then he died* |
| Kenan | 70 | 910 | *and then he died* |
| Mahalalel | 65 | 895 | *and then he died* |
| Jared | 162 | 962 | *and then he died* |
| **Enoch** | 65 | **365** | *he was not, for God took him* |
| Methuselah | 187 | 969 | *and then he died* — in the flood year |
| Lamech | 182 | **777** | *and then he died* |
| **Noah** | 500 | (950, in 9:29) | the flood comes in his 600th year |
{: .ff-table .ff-wrap}

Read it as a pattern rather than a ledger and it starts to talk.

**The numbers are built.** Every figure in Genesis 5 — all twenty-eight of
them — is a multiple of five, or a multiple of five with **seven** or **fourteen**
added. Seth, 912 (905 + 7). Jared, 962 (955 + 7). Methuselah, 969 (955 +
14). Lamech, 777 (770 + 7). The Italian-Israeli
scholar Umberto Cassuto spotted the rule in 1944, and no one has found an
exception. Whatever else these numbers are, they were **composed** — the way
the Sumerian numbers were composed in *šár*s and *nēr*s.

**The people who copied them treated them as adjustable.** We have the
chapter in three ancient forms, and the numbers differ:

| | Hebrew (Masoretic) | Samaritan | Greek (Septuagint) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adam fathers Seth at | 130 | 130 | 230 |
| Seth fathers Enosh at | 105 | 105 | 205 |
| Jared fathers Enoch at | 162 | 62 | 162 |
| Methuselah fathers Lamech at | 187 | 67 | 167 |
| **Creation to flood** | **1,656** | **1,307** | **2,242** |
{: .ff-table .ff-wrap}

The Greek adds a hundred years to most fathers. The Samaritan trims three of
them so that Jared, Methuselah and Lamech all die *in the flood year*. And in
the main Greek manuscripts, Methuselah **outlives the flood by fourteen
years** — a problem ancient readers noticed, and some copies quietly fixed.
And when 1 Chronicles retells the same ten names (1:1–4), it **drops every
number**. The tradition kept the ages, adjusted them, and once did without
them. It did not treat them as untouchable.

**What they say without being added up.** They fall. 969, 950, then after
the flood 600, 438; Abraham 175, Jacob 147, Joseph 110, Moses 120 — and then
Psalm 90's *seventy years, or eighty if one is strong*. That is the King
List's curve: enormous before the flood, a cliff, then a long slide. Genesis
makes the cliff explicit
one verse into the next chapter: *"they will remain for 120 more years"*
(6:3). **The numbers are counting down.** They are the sound of a world
getting further from the garden.

None of this settles whether Methuselah drew breath for 969 years. Josephus
thought so; Maimonides thought only the ten named men did; the rabbis took
each figure as a fact with a reason. **You may land anywhere on that.** What
the chapter asks is not that you decide, but that you notice what its numbers
are *doing* — and what they are doing is descending, in a pattern, toward a
flood.

<div class="discuss" markdown="1">
**Talk about it.** If the numbers are a composed pattern rather than a
register, what changes about how you read the chapter — and what, honestly,
does not change at all?
</div>

## Part 5 — The refrain, and the two who break it

Now read Genesis 5 for what it actually says, which is mostly one sentence.

### First, what it inherits

> This is the record of the family line of Adam. When God created humankind,
> he made them in the likeness of God… When Adam had lived 130 years he
> fathered a son **in his own likeness, according to his image**, and he
> named him Seth. *(5:1, 3)*

Verses 1–2 quote Genesis 1:26–28 nearly word for word. Then verse 3 uses the
same two words — *likeness* and *image*, in reverse order — for what Adam
passes to Seth. Read straight, this is the text's own answer to a question
the garden left hanging: **the image of God was not lost.** It is inherited.
It goes down a line that begins with the replacement for a murdered son, in
the verse after a boast about killing a boy.

The rabbis heard how much rides on that. *Genesis Rabbah* 24:7 records Ben
Azzai arguing that *"This is the book of the descendants of Adam"* is **the
central tenet of the Torah** — greater than *love your neighbour* — because,
as Rabbi Tanḥuma explains, *"in the likeness of God He made him"*: every
name on the list, and every person descended from it, carries the image. A
genealogy is a theology of who your neighbour is.

### Then, the refrain

*And then he died. And then he died. And then he died.* **Eight times.** The
chapter that begins with the image of God is a list of the image dying.
Paul is reading this when he writes that *"death reigned from Adam until
Moses"* (Romans 5:14). Nine hundred years is a long time; the sentence
arrives anyway.

Two men break the refrain.

### Enoch

> Enoch walked with God, and then he disappeared because God took him away.
> *(5:24)*

Three Hebrew details carry the weight here. **"Walked with God"** is the verb
form Genesis 3:8 uses for God *walking about in the garden* — Enoch is doing
what God did in Eden, and the preposition is *with*, not *before*; in
Genesis only Enoch and Noah get that phrase. **"He was not"** is what Jacob
says of Joseph — *gone*. And **"took"** is the verb of Elijah's chariot (2
Kings 2) and of the psalmist's hope past the grave: *"God will redeem my life
from Sheol, for he will **take** me"* (49:15).

That is the whole basis for the reading that Enoch did not die, and the
earliest readers ran with it. Ben Sira (c. 180 BCE): *"No man was born upon
earth like Henoch: for he also was taken up from the earth."* Hebrews 11:5:
*"By faith Enoch was taken up so that he did not see death."* And *Jubilees*
made him **the first man to learn writing**, who *"wrote down the signs of
heaven"* and was *"shown the rule of the sun"* — which is Enmeduranki's résumé,
handed to Israel's seventh man with the omens taken out and righteousness put
in. The seventh king of Sippar came back from heaven with the secrets of
divination. The seventh from Adam walked with God, and God kept him.

**The rabbis were not so sure**, and it is worth hearing why. *Genesis
Rabbah* 25:1: *"He is not written in the ledger of the righteous, but rather,
in the ledger of the wicked"*; Enoch was *"capricious; sometimes righteous,
sometimes wicked,"* so God *"took him away while he is still in his
righteousness"* — early, at 365, before he could fall. When "heretics" argue
from the Elijah verb that Enoch never died, Rabbi Abahu answers with the same
verb in Ezekiel 24:16, where God *takes* the prophet's wife by death. **The
word proves nothing by itself.** And yet the same tradition kept the other
reading in its synagogue translation: *"he ascended to the firmament… and his
name was called Metatron the Great Saphra"* — the heavenly scribe. Both are
Jewish, both are
ancient, and the tradition kept both. So should we: Enoch is the one man in
the list about whom the text says *God took him* instead of *he died* — and
it says nothing more.

### Noah

> He named him Noah, saying, "This one will bring us comfort from our labor
> and from the painful toil of our hands **because of the ground that the
> LORD has cursed**." *(5:29)*

*Noah* means *rest*; Lamech's verb means *comfort*; the pun is loose, and the
rabbis said so out loud. But the sentence is exact. **This is the first time
anyone in Genesis has mentioned the curse on the ground since God pronounced
it** (3:17) and Cain inherited it (4:11–12). Nine generations have been born
and buried under it, and the ninth names his son as the one who will finally
answer it. *Genesis Rabbah* 25:2 tells it as a story: since Adam, *"the cow
did not heed the plowman and the furrow did not heed the plowman"* — until
Noah, when they *rested*.

Cain's line answered the cursed ground by building **everything except a
farm**. Seth's line answers it with a man who will be, in the next chapter's
words, *"a man of the soil"* (9:20) — a farmer, who plants a vineyard when the
water goes down. And notice how the chapter ends. Every other entry closes
*and then he died*. Noah's does not. His death is recorded — four chapters
later (9:29) — but Genesis 5 will not say it. The list that counted down to
the flood stops, on its last man, with the refrain unspoken.

## So what?

**1. A genealogy is theology, not a registry.** Its technology is the
author's, its numbers are composed, and its names are chosen. None of that
is a flaw. It is how this kind of writing tells the truth — and Israel's
neighbours told theirs the same way, with a different God.

**2. The image was not lost.** Genesis 5:3 hands Adam's likeness to Seth in
the same words God used for Adam. Ben Azzai called it the greatest principle
in the Torah. The person in front of you is on this list.

**3. Death is the refrain, and God breaks it once — quietly.** *God took him*
is all the text says about Enoch, and the tradition argued for a thousand
years about what it meant. Hold him loosely and hopefully, the way the text
does.

**4. Nine generations waited for rest, and one man said so.** Lamech named
his hope out loud, over a cursed field, with no evidence it would come. It
came from a farmer.

<div class="discuss" markdown="1">
**Talk about it — to close.** *First:* Lamech names a child *Rest* while
standing on ground that will not yield. What are you naming hope over right
now that has not yet come? *Second, this week:* pick one name on your own
list — someone you descend from, or someone who descends from you — and say
to yourself, in Ben Azzai's words, *in the likeness of God He made them*. See
what it changes.
</div>
